# Phase 2 — See the system, design what is wanted

**Purpose.** In one long meeting, or two half-days a week apart, the people you enlisted build a shared picture of how the neighborhood's institutions, associations, and small groups currently work together around residents' lives (the linkage map), and then design what they would want right now if history did not bind them (the Idealized Design). Areas for developing RenDanHeYi-style scenarios surface in the gap between the two. Phase 2 produces candidate scenario areas, not projects and not finished scenarios.

What surfaces can take any of three forms, and the room should know that going in. Medford and Spokane were hired to help a disjointed medical and social system find opportunities for more coordinated service, so the linkage map there showed work between institutions. In a neighborhood, the gap may just as well call for a new association or a new local business, or for a time-limited project that organizes around one need and then ends. Idealized Design may suggest any of the three, and all three are outcomes this work exists for.

**The Six Questions.** Marc opens every facilitation with them: WHY, WHERE, WHEN, WHO, WHAT, HOW. Kerry usually opens with a causal loop diagram for strategic focus, and agrees the Six Questions are a good first step. Phase 2 is built on them. Session one asks WHY, WHERE, WHEN, and WHO, and then maps what is. Session two asks WHAT: Idealized Design is the answer to what is wanted. HOW comes last, in Phases 3 through 5, and it comes last on purpose. Most of the work will be in the HOW, and a room that starts there does, in Ackoff's phrase, the wrong thing righter. Having heard the diversity of answers to the other five questions first is what makes the HOW worth doing.

**Conversation type.** Possibilities. Nobody commits to anything in this meeting. If someone offers a promise, thank them and write it down for Phase 4.

**Reasoning.** Design Record Sections 3 (VSM, Idealized Design as S4, Deming's three levels), 5 (what a scenario is and how it surfaces), 7 (the WHY sequence, moods in the room), 8 (the between-institution form the map shows, and the other forms of neighborhood value creation).

**Time.** Six to eight hours in the room, plus your preparation, which can be extensive, and the write-up. At county scale CMG ran this as one full day; in Spokane, at five-county scale, one full day and then five additional days. At neighborhood scale two half-days a week apart work better, because people have jobs and children and because a week lets the possibilities settle in.

**The picture of the phases.** A graphic of the five phases belongs at the front of this guide and beside each phase, and one exists: the color-coded chevrons Marc made for Medford, attached to the LMID space in Confluence as "Phases of Linkage Mapping Work.png." A Graph Tool version to redraw it for the neighborhood cycle is in `../diagrams/field-guide-five-phases.rcn.json`.

## Preparation

Schedule the founded commons for both sessions and for the diagrams to stay on the wall afterward. Bring: a wall's worth of large paper or a whiteboard, sticky notes in three colors, markers, tape, and a way to photograph the wall.

Use pens, hands, arms, and legs to co-create the diagrams. The wall is where the group thinks. Make the computer version in the RCN Graph Tool after the fact and validate it with the group at the next meeting; once the group accepts the computer representation as faithful to its own drawing, it can be used and extended by the group, with a projector if you have one. Do not let the computer tool replace the wall. The tool is where the diagrams, maps, and models are kept afterward.

Prepare, from your Phase 1 notes, a personal exploratory starter list of the parts: the small groups from your RCN Table, the institutions residents deal with, the two or three residents' situations you heard most about. This is what CMG's Phase 1 called the draft high-level linkage map, and it is yours, not the room's. Do not prepare the diagram for the group. The group, in campfire conversation, makes its own drawings, cave drawings a room can read and point at.

Have an exploratory e-VSM synthesis in your pocket, read before the meeting. The exploratory use is yours: look for gaps, barriers, and big risks before you step onto the improvisational stage. Then, separately, have the results ready to show the room after the WHY round (the worlds side by side, where they agree and where they diverge, a page of free text in respondents' words), as Phase 1 says. Both uses, not one: the synthesis prepares you, and the results belong to the room.

Prepare one side sheet for session two, in large letters, so that the Idealized Design building blocks are on the wall as prompts and not in your mouth as a lecture. Write on it, in four short columns: **Concepts** — understandable, safe, connected (it is a system), continuity; **Every design considers** — life stages, barriers, communication, place, time of day and week, transportation, the professional-client relationship; **Principles** — personal, convenient, comprehensive, teamwork; **Components** — WHY (purpose; the experience people should have), FOR WHOM and WHERE (the whole family; place matters), WHAT (basic needs; services), HOW (planning, organizing, communication, knowledge and skills, access, coordination, a shared care plan, expenses that match funding). Leave room under each column for the room's own words.

## Session one: the linkage map

### Opening: WHY, from where each person stands

Go around the room once. Ask each person: why are you here, from where you stand? Not what you hope to get, not what is wrong; why this, for you. These are declarations of purpose. Purpose is brought into being by being said, and the room needs to hear itself say it before it looks at anything. Write each one on the wall, in their words, and leave them up for both sessions. Keep them afterward; in some real way they are a declaration of independence.

Then, briefly, WHERE (what place are we talking about, whose edges), WHEN (what span of time matters, this year, this generation), and WHO, for whom (whose lives). Each question changes the answers to the ones after it. Together they create the frame for community creativity and collective action. They can and should be discussed and updated over time, as experience accumulates and as the group changes.

For people in resignation, you will hear it in the WHY round: "I'm here because I was asked." Do not argue. Say, if it helps, that the room has tried before and been let down, and that today is for exploring, not for promising. Then go on.

### What the neighborhood has already said

Before the map, show the e-VSM results. Say what they are: fourteen people, or however many, in these worlds, asked how the neighborhood's capacities and the relationships among them are doing. Show the worlds side by side. Point at where they agree and where they diverge, and read a few lines of the free text aloud in the respondents' words. Ask the room what it makes of the divergences. Ten or fifteen minutes. It is the neighborhood's own several pictures of itself, gathered from more people than are in the room, and the room should have it before it draws. Do not present it as findings that settle anything; it is what has already been said, for the room to argue with and correct on the map.

### Building the map

The linkage map shows the parts and the flows between them from three perspectives, which are Deming's three levels and which CMG used to organize every map:

- **Mainstay** — the client's perspective: the residents and families, and the services and groups that touch their lives directly. The ways value is created and delivered.
- **Support** — the infrastructure perspective: the coordination, information, transport, and improvement functions that let the mainstay work.
- **Driver** — the stakeholder's perspective: the people and bodies who plan, fund, decide, and initiate change.

Start with a very limited number of mainstays. Put a resident's situation at the center, the one or two the room most wants to talk about, and build outward. Ask: who touches this person's or this family's life? Each part goes up as a note. Then the flows: what actually moves between these parts, clients, information, materials, money, and what is supposed to move and does not. Draw the arrows. Label every arrow with what moves; an unlabeled arrow only says "connected," and the map has to be checkable.

Three ways in, from the 2014 material; use whichever the room reaches for:

1. Start from the pain points between institutions and associations: where does a family fall through? Where does a referral die? Where do two agencies, or two associations, each think the other is handling it?
2. Start from a specific client's needs, end to end: what does this family need, in what sequence, across a year, and who provides each piece?
3. Start from what people wish they had today. That is Idealized Design, and it is session two; if it comes early, catch it on a separate sheet.

The first two make current-state maps. The third makes the best possible future-state map.

Then the support level: what formal and informal infrastructure exists to support these parts working well and in coordination today? What information moves, and what does not? Then the driver level: who has influence, who can make commitments, who funds, who plans, and where do those people meet, if anywhere?

Keep the map small enough to read from arm's length, so people can point and talk together. Fifteen to twenty parts is a map a group can discuss; sixty is a database. If the room wants more detail, it goes in a note beside the part.

Watch the moods while the map grows. The map will show things that are infuriating. Let the resentment be said and put it on the map: "the district promised and let it lapse, twice" is a fact about a flow, and belongs there. What you must protect against is the slide from resentment into resignation, "so nothing will work"; when you hear it, point at the map and ask what already works, because something always does, and it is usually a small group.

### Closing session one

Photograph the wall. Read the map back to the room, out loud, part by part, and ask what is wrong with it. Get agreement on the language. Fix it on the spot. Then ask one question and write the answers down without discussing them: whose lives, on this map, are caught between parts, in a way no single part alone can fix? Those are the beginnings of scenario areas.

Leave the map up. Between sessions, enter it in the RCN Graph Tool. The tool has no linkage-map mode; you draw the map in its ordinary mode and give it a legend, a small table of the kinds of thing on the map (a family, an institution, an association, a flow of clients, a flow of information, a missing flow) with an icon and a color for each, and every part and arrow follows its kind. That is what "a legend preset, not a special mode" means: the legend is the linkage-map notation, and the tools file has one ready to copy. Bring a large printout, or use a projector, in session two, alongside the original hand-made diagram, and ask the room whether the computer version is faithful before you use it.

## Session two: Idealized Design — the WHAT

### Opening

Read the WHY declarations from the wall again. Then Ackoff's move, said plainly: the system on this wall was destroyed last night. Nothing else was; everything else is the same. We are going to design the one we would want right now if we were free to have it, subject to two constraints: it has to be technologically feasible, no telepathy, and it has to be able to survive in the world as it is, legal, affordable, workable. It does not have to be reachable from where we are. Truly act as though the system does not exist any more. You have to create a new one. Will you rebuild the old one, or will it become something better, maybe much better?

Section 8 of the Design Record tells the Bell Labs story if you want it for the room. Tell it briefly: a room full of engineers were told by their boss that the national telephone system had been destroyed the night before. Over the next couple of weeks they collectively imagined ninety-two things they wanted a telephone to do, without a single disagreement, and that list was turned into reality, some of it immediately and some of it over the next forty years. They had a very clear sense of what they were doing together.

### Building blocks

Marc's 2014 material gives the design its scaffolding, and it can work at neighborhood scale as it did at county level. Put the side sheet up. Do not lecture it. Point at a column when the room stalls: "is there a time-of-day problem here?" "what would convenient mean for this family?" Ask the room for its own words under each column and write them there.

### The design

Take the resident's situation from the center of the map and ask: what would this person's or this family's experience look like if it were designed and delivered? Go through the timeline of a life if it helps, birth, early childhood, school, work and raising a family, later years, end of life, and ask where this situation sits. Build the wanted system on a fresh sheet beside the map of the current one. Same parts, or new parts; flows as they should be; who does what. Remember the three forms: the wanted system may need a new association, a new business, or a project with an end, as much as better coordination among what exists.

Then the SWOT-type scan, briefly: what is coming at this neighborhood, demographic, economic, environmental, cultural, that the design has to survive? Add it to the sheet. Idealized Design without the scan is a wish list; the scan without the design is firefighting. SWOT and Idealized Design work together.

### Co-defining the ground

CMG's Phase 2 also had the room co-define its guiding values, principles, goals, beliefs, hopes, and fears. At neighborhood scale, take twenty minutes at the end: hopes and fears go around the room, in a sentence each, and up on the wall next to the WHYs. The principles the room names will become the weighting criteria in Phase 3, so write them down carefully in the room's own words. Five or six is enough, but record all that are offered.

### Scenario areas surface

Now stand the two sheets side by side, what is and what is wanted, and ask the question Section 5 of the Design Record gives you: whose lives are caught in the gap, in what complex or complicated ways, across what end-to-end context? Avoid oversimplifying. Each answer is a candidate scenario area. Give each one a title in the residents' own language ("a child arriving new at school as a refugee, first year," not "school-based refugee service coordination"). List the multiple interlocking needs it contains. Note who articulated it. That is enough for now; the scenario gets written properly in Phase 4.

Expect three to eight candidates. If fewer than three, encourage more; if more than ten, look for overlap and integrate. Regardless, keep everything. The weighted selection matrix in Phase 3 will help sequence the work.

Bring in the scenarios from other neighborhoods you read in Phase 1 now, and only where one matches: "another neighborhood wrote this one; here is how they put it." Forking and adapting a scenario can be helpful, if it is a really good fit.

### Closing session two

Read the candidate scenario areas back. Ask the room which of them it wants to keep on the list for Phase 3. Explain what happens next: in the coming week, each person will weight the principles and score the candidates, on their own, and a short meeting will settle the choice. Thank the people by name.

## After the meeting

Within three days: enter both diagrams from the wall into the Graph Tool; write each candidate as a page in the FedWiki using the scenario template (title, situation end to end, interlocking needs, who counts as the user and their neighbors, where it is real, existing attempts, attribution); and send the room the photographs and the list. Keep the paper on the wall of the founded commons. People will add to it.

## Handoff to Phase 3

Phase 2 is done when: the current-state map and the ideal-state design exist on the wall and in the RCN Graph Tool; the room's principles are written in its words; three to eight candidate scenario areas have titles, interlocking needs, and authors; and all the people who were in the room have the list.

## Worked example — Phase 2

**Honesty note.** The parts and relationships on the map are real; they come from Spokane linkage map #7. The meeting, what people said, and the candidates that surfaced are made up to show what the phase produces.

Session one, the garage, a Saturday morning. The WHY round takes forty minutes and the convener does not shorten it. The school nurse says she is here because she meets every new child on the first day and then never hears what happened. One of the Somali mothers says, through the CHW, that she is here because a friend asked her to participate.

The convener shows the e-VSM results after the round: newer arrivals and longtime residents marking the same relationship, awareness within the neighborhood to coordination, in opposite directions, and the mothers' free text about the first weeks of school. The room argues about it for ten minutes and the argument becomes the first thing on the map.

The map's customer is one parent's daughter, who started school in September. Around her: the family; the school (enrollment, the nurse, two teachers); the resettlement agency, which handled the family's arrival and whose caseworker the family has not seen since; the clinic; the mosque and the mothers' group; the CHW; the childcare ring; the tenants' association; the district office. The arrows: the family presents to the school and to the agency; the agency and the school share the client and do not talk to each other; the CHW requests coordination from all of them and gets it from none reliably; the clinic and the agency share the client on paper. Two arrows that ought to exist and do not, drawn dotted: nobody tells the school what the agency knows about the family, and nobody tells the family what to do when something goes wrong at school. The resentment about the district's two lapsed programs goes on the map as a note on the district.

Whose lives are caught between parts? Three answers, written down without discussion: the new student in the first weeks; the mother trying to reach the school; the CHW carrying all of it.

Session two, the next Saturday. The system was destroyed last night. What would this girl's year look like if it were designed? The room builds it: one intake conversation that captures what the agency knows, with the family, at the school, with the CHW present; one named person, the CHW, whom every institution recognizes as the family's navigator; a check-in at two weeks, six weeks, and each term; a way for the family to raise a problem that does not begin with calling the school office in English. The scan adds two things: the district's funding for its newcomer program ends in June, and the apartment buildings are changing hands.

Then hopes and fears, one sentence from each person, up on the wall beside the WHYs. From those the room names five principles, in its own words, which will weight the choice in Phase 3: nothing about us without us; the family should have one person it already trusts; whatever we do has to still be there next year; it should cost the school less, not more; and the mothers decide what "going well" means.

Four candidate scenario areas: a refugee student's first school year; a family's first contact with the clinic; what happens when a program ends; the CHW's own load. The convener says that a scenario like the first one already exists in the repository, written in Spokane at county scale. The room decides to write its own for this neighborhood and to fork the Spokane one so that its record of what was tried comes along. By Wednesday the four are on the FedWiki template, and the two maps are in the Graph Tool with the dotted arrows in red.
