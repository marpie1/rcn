# Phase 2 — See the system, design what is wanted

**Purpose.** In one long meeting, or two half-days a week apart, the people you enlisted build a shared picture of how the neighborhood's institutions and small groups currently work together around residents' lives (the linkage map), and then design what they would want right now if history did not bind them (the Idealized Design). Scenarios surface in the gap between the two. Phase 2 produces scenarios, not projects.

**Conversation type.** Possibilities. Nobody commits to anything in this meeting. If someone offers a promise, thank them and write it down for Phase 4.

**Reasoning.** Design Record Sections 3 (VSM, Idealized Design as S4, Deming's three levels), 5 (what a scenario is and how it surfaces), 7 (the WHY sequence, moods in the room), 8 (the between-institution form the map shows).

**Time.** Six to eight hours in the room, plus your preparation and the write-up. At county scale CMG ran this as one full day; at neighborhood scale two half-days a week apart work better, because people have jobs and children and because a week lets the map settle.

## Preparation

Book the founded commons for both sessions and for the map to stay on the wall afterward. Bring: a wall's worth of large paper or a whiteboard, sticky notes in three colors, markers, tape, and a way to photograph the wall. If you use the RCN Graph Tool, bring a laptop and, if you can, a projector, but do not let the tool replace the wall. The wall is where the group thinks; the tool is where the map is kept afterward.

Prepare, from your Phase 1 notes, a starter list of the parts: the small groups, the institutions residents deal with, the two or three residents' situations you heard most about. Do not prepare the map. The group makes the map.

Have the e-VSM synthesis ready to show: the worlds side by side, where they agree and where they diverge, and a page of the free text in respondents' own words.

## Session one: the linkage map

### Opening: WHY, from where each person stands

Go around the room once. Ask each person: why are you here, from where you stand? Not what you hope to get, not what is wrong; why this, for you. These are declarations of purpose. Purpose is brought into being by being said, and the room needs to hear itself say it before it looks at anything. Write each one on the wall, in their words, and leave them up for both sessions.

Then, briefly, WHERE (what place are we talking about, whose edges), WHEN (what span of time matters, this year, this generation), and FOR WHOM (whose lives). These are shorter. They settle the frame.

If the room is in resignation, you will hear it in the WHY round: "I'm here because I was asked." Do not argue. Say, if it helps, that the room has tried before and been let down, and that today is for looking, not for promising. Then go on.

### What the neighborhood has already said

Before the map, show the e-VSM results. Say what they are: fourteen people, or however many, in these worlds, asked how the neighborhood's capacities and the relationships among them are doing. Show the worlds side by side. Point at where they agree and where they diverge, and read a few lines of the free text aloud in the respondents' words. Ask the room what it makes of the divergences. Ten or fifteen minutes. It is the neighborhood's own several pictures of itself, gathered from more people than are in the room, and the room should have it before it draws. Do not present it as findings that settle anything; it is what has already been said, for the room to argue with and correct on the map.

### Building the map

The linkage map shows the parts and the flows between them from three perspectives, which are Deming's three levels and which CMG used to organize every map:

- **Mainstay** — the client's perspective: the residents and families, and the services and groups that touch their lives directly.
- **Support** — the staff's perspective: the coordination, information, transport, and improvement functions that let the mainstay work.
- **Driver** — the stakeholder's perspective: the people and bodies who plan, fund, and decide.

Start with the mainstay. Put a resident's situation at the center, the one the room most wants to talk about, and build outward. Ask: who touches this family's life? Each part goes up as a note. Then the flows: what actually moves between these parts, clients, information, materials, money, and what is supposed to move and does not. Draw the arrows. Label every arrow with what moves; an unlabeled arrow only says "connected," and the map has to be checkable.

Three ways in, from the 2014 material; use whichever the room reaches for:

1. Start from the pain points between institutions: where does a family fall through? Where does a referral die? Where do two agencies each think the other is handling it?
2. Start from a specific client's needs, end to end: what does this family need, in what sequence, across a year, and who provides each piece?
3. Start from what people wish they had. That is Idealized Design, and it is session two; if it comes early, catch it on a separate sheet.

Then the support level: what coordination exists among these parts today, formal (a monthly meeting) and informal (the person who knows who to call)? What information moves, and what does not? Then the driver level: who decides, who funds, who plans, and where do those people meet, if anywhere?

Keep the map small enough to read from across the room. Fifteen to twenty parts is a map a group can argue with; sixty is a database. If the room wants more detail, it goes in a note on the part, not on the wall.

Watch the moods while the map grows. The map will show things that are infuriating. Let the resentment be said and put it on the map: "the district promised and let it lapse, twice" is a fact about a flow, and belongs there. What you protect against is the slide into resignation, "so nothing will work"; when you hear it, point at the map and ask what already works, because something always does, and it is usually a small group.

### Closing session one

Photograph the wall. Read the map back to the room, out loud, part by part, and ask what is wrong with it. Fix it on the spot. Then ask one question and write the answers down without discussing them: whose life, on this map, is caught between parts, in a way no single part can fix? Those are the beginnings of scenarios.

Leave the map up. Between sessions, enter it in the RCN Graph Tool if you can (the tools file says how; it is a legend preset, not a special mode), and bring the printout to session two next to the paper.

## Session two: Idealized Design

### Opening

Read the WHY declarations from the wall again. Then Ackoff's move, said plainly: the system on this wall was destroyed last night. Nothing else was; everything else is the same. We are going to design the one we would want right now if we were free to have it, subject to two constraints: it has to be technologically feasible, no telepathy, and it has to be able to survive in the world as it is, legal, affordable, workable. It does not have to be reachable from where we are. We are nowhere; the system does not exist.

Section 8 of the Design Record tells the Bell Labs story if you want it for the room. Tell it short: a room full of engineers told their system was destroyed, who wrote ninety-two things they wanted from a telephone in two weeks without a single disagreement, and whose list took forty years to build out.

### Building blocks

Marc's 2014 material gives the design its scaffolding, and it works at neighborhood scale as it did at county:

- **Design concepts** — what the whole thing should be like. The 2014 set: transparent and understandable, safe, connected (it is a system), progress and continuity. Ask the room for its own words.
- **Design requirements** — what every design must address. The 2014 set: life stages, barriers, communication, place, time of day and week, transportation, the relationship between professional and client. Walk through them for the situation at the center of the map.
- **Design principles** — the humanizing ones: personal, convenient, comprehensive, teamwork.
- **Design components** — WHY (purpose, the experience people should have), FOR WHOM and WHERE (the whole family; place matters), WHAT (basic needs, services), and HOW (planning, organizing, communication, knowledge and skills, access, coordination, a shared care plan, and expenses that match funding).

Do not lecture these. Put them on a sheet at the side and use them as prompts when the room stalls.

### The design

Take the resident's situation from the center of the map and ask: what would this family's year look like if it were designed? Go through the timeline of a life if it helps, birth, early childhood, school, work and raising a family, later years, end of life, and ask where this situation sits. Build the wanted system on a fresh sheet beside the map of the current one. Same parts, or new parts; flows as they should be; who does what.

Then the SWOT-type scan, briefly: what is coming at this neighborhood, demographic, economic, environmental, cultural, that the design has to survive? Add it to the sheet. Idealized Design without the scan is a wish list; the scan without the design is firefighting.

### Co-defining the ground

CMG's Phase 2 also had the room co-define its values, principles, goals, beliefs, hopes, and fears. At neighborhood scale, take twenty minutes at the end: hopes and fears go around the room, in a sentence each, and up on the wall next to the WHYs. The principles the room names will become the weighting criteria in Phase 3, so write them down carefully in the room's own words. Five or six is enough.

### Scenarios surface

Now stand the two sheets side by side, what is and what is wanted, and ask the question Section 5 of the Design Record gives you: whose life is caught in the gap, in what compound way, across what end-to-end context? Each answer is a candidate scenario. Give each one a title in the residents' own language ("a child arriving new at school as a refugee, first year," not "school-based refugee service coordination"). List the compound needs it contains. Note who articulated it. That is enough for now; the scenario gets written properly in Phase 4.

Expect three to eight candidates. If the room has fewer than three, the map was too small or the room too comfortable; if more than ten, some are the same scenario with different titles.

Bring in the scenarios you read in Phase 1 from other neighborhoods, now, and only where one plainly matches: "another neighborhood wrote this one; here is how they put it." Forking a scenario is cheaper than writing one.

### Closing session two

Read the candidate scenarios back. Ask the room which of them it wants to keep on the list for Phase 3; anything the room drops, drop. Tell the room what happens next: in the coming week, each person will weight the principles and score the scenarios, on their own, and a short meeting will settle the choice. Thank the people whose situations were at the center of the map, by name.

## After the meeting

Within three days: enter both maps in the Graph Tool, write each candidate scenario as a compound-need page on the FedWiki scenario template (title, situation end to end, compound needs, who counts as the user and their neighbors, where it is real, existing attempts, attribution), and send the room the photographs and the scenario list. Keep the paper on the wall of the founded commons. People will add to it.

## Handoff to Phase 3

Phase 2 is done when: the current-state map and the wanted-state design exist on the wall and in the tool; the room's principles are written in its words; three to eight candidate scenarios have titles, compound needs, and authors; and the people who were in the room have the list.

## Worked example — Phase 2

**Honesty note.** The map's parts and relationships are from Spokane linkage map #7: Schools, World Relief (here "the resettlement agency"), CHW Network, Family & Individual, health providers; SHARE_CLIENT, PRESENTS_TO, REQUEST_COORD. The building blocks are from Marc's 2014 Idealized Design deck. The meeting itself, what people said, and the scenarios that surfaced are constructed.

Session one, the garage, a Saturday morning. The WHY round takes forty minutes and the convener does not shorten it. The school nurse says she is here because she meets every new child on the first day and then never hears what happened. One of the Somali mothers says, through the CHW, that she is here because the mechanic asked her.

The map's center is the father's daughter, who started school in September. Around her: the family; the school (enrollment, the nurse, two teachers); the resettlement agency, which handled the family's arrival and whose caseworker the family has not seen since; the clinic; the mosque and the mothers' group; the CHW; the childcare ring; the tenants' association; the district office. The arrows: the family presents to the school and to the agency; the agency and the school share the client and do not talk to each other; the CHW requests coordination from all of them and gets it from none reliably; the clinic and the agency share the client on paper. Two arrows that ought to exist and do not, drawn dotted: nobody tells the school what the agency knows about the family, and nobody tells the family what to do when something goes wrong at school. The resentment about the district's two lapsed programs goes on the map as a note on the district.

Whose life is caught between parts? Three answers, written down without discussion: the new student in the first weeks; the mother trying to reach the school; the CHW carrying all of it.

Session two, the next Saturday. The system was destroyed last night. What would this girl's year look like if it were designed? The room builds it: one intake conversation that captures what the agency knows, with the family, at the school, with the CHW present; one named person, the CHW, whom every institution recognizes as the family's navigator; a check-in at two weeks, six weeks, and each term; a way for the family to raise a problem that does not begin with calling the school office in English. The scan adds two things: the district's funding for its newcomer program ends in June, and the apartment buildings are changing hands.

Hopes and fears, one sentence each. The principles the room names: nothing about us without us; one person the family already trusts; it has to still be there next year; it should cost the school less, not more; the mothers decide what "going well" means.

Candidate scenarios, four: a refugee student's first school year; a family's first contact with the clinic; what happens when a program ends; the CHW's own load. The convener mentions that the first one exists in the scenario repository from Spokane, written at county scale; the room wants to write its own and fork the Spokane one for its list of attempts. By Wednesday the four are on the FedWiki template, and the two maps are in the Graph Tool with the dotted arrows in red.
