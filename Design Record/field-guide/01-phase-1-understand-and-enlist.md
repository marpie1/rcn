# Phase 1 — Understand and enlist

**Purpose.** Learn whether this neighborhood is ready, find its small groups and its place, survey how it sees itself, and invite the right people into the first meeting. Or learn that it is not ready, say what is missing, and stay in relationship.

This is not a lifeline for failing neighborhoods. It is support for the vanguard of stressed neighborhoods that have the current or latent capacity to help themselves, against the outside extractors that stress them. Phase 1 is how you tell which is which.

**Conversation type.** Coffee: conversations for connection and trust. You are finding out whether there is shared interest in building anything at all. Do not ask anyone for a commitment in this phase.

**Reasoning.** Design Record Sections 3 (what the neighborhood has to have already), 4 (the founded commons and the small groups filter), 5 (the scenario repository), 6 (the Platform and its actors), 7 (moods).

**Time.** Uncertain, and improvisational. At least three to four weeks, most of it in conversations with one or two people at a time.

## Before you start: your own preparation

Do three things before you talk to anyone.

**Orient yourself to moods.** Read the one page in the tools file. You need to be able to tell resignation from acceptance and ambition from a wish, in yourself first. Most of Phase 1 is listening for the mood of a place, and a convener must not confuse their own mood with the mood of others. Moods change. Moods live in small groups as well as in individuals, and one group's mood is not the neighborhood's.

**Read the scenarios that already exist.** The FedWiki scenario repository holds scenarios other neighborhoods have written. Read the ones that plausibly apply here, so that when someone tells you about a family caught between a school and a clinic you recognize it as a scenario others have named, and can say so. Do not bring the scenarios to the neighborhood yet; bring the awareness.

Before this guide is shared beyond RCN, Chris, Jerry, Carl, and Brent each contribute one well-developed scenario from their own neighborhood to the repository. A convener new to the work needs real ones to read, and four is the minimum.

**Know your own Platform relationship.** You are, or will be, an actor of a Neighborhood-Catalyzing Industry Platform: WWHA's granting arm in Whatcom, or its equivalent where you are. That means three things. Your compensation and your reputation ride on what the MEs you help form deliver, as recognized by the residents they are for; nobody pays you a salary for facilitating. You seed and support those MEs, with mentorship, contacts, contract help, and a little catalytic money released against gates, and you do not run them. And what comes back to you is paid last, after residents recognize value and after the ME's own members are paid. Be clear with yourself about this before you invite anyone, because it is the honest answer to the question "what's in it for you," and someone will ask. Design Record Section 6 sets out the role in full; pattern 29 in the tools file, Seed and Support, Do Not Run, is the one-page version.

## The two filters

A neighborhood is ready for Phase 2 when both of these are present: a founded commons, a place its people made and recognize as their own, and small groups already getting things done. Your Phase 1 job is to find out, and to be willing to say no.

### Filter one: a founded commons

The neighborhood has a place its people created for their own collective work, recognize as theirs, use as a commons, and that no outside actor directs. Leo's in Superior and The Fledge in Lansing are the reference cases. A friendly coffee shop with a corner table is not one; a church basement the pastor lends is usually not one; a room the county lets you use is not one.

**What it looks like, in RCN and outside it.** Leo's, in Superior, Arizona, is Chris Casillas's: a place the neighborhood uses for social life, mutual support, and its own improvement work, gardens, home repair, fire prevention. The Fledge, in Lansing, Michigan, is Jerry's: a place that has helped locals start many small businesses, open to anyone with an idea. Both began with one person and have been adopted by the neighborhood as its own. Outside RCN the same pattern has appeared on its own many times, and these are worth knowing so the range is visible. The Men's Sheds that began in Australia in the 1980s, community workshops where older men fix and build things together, now number well over a thousand there and have spread to several countries; each is run by its members. The Repair Cafés that Martine Postma started in Amsterdam in 2009, where neighbors bring broken things and volunteers fix them for free, have spread to tens of thousands of volunteers in more than thirty countries, each café local and self-run. The community-owned pubs and shops of British villages, some 170 pubs bought by their villages when the last one was closing, run by the people who drink there. The settlement houses of a century ago, Hull House in Chicago among them, where a neighborhood's own life was organized in a building the neighborhood came to regard as its own. What these share is what the four conditions name: made from inside, for collaborative work, used as a commons, not captured. What works, across all of them: the place is cheap to keep open; it tolerates mess and unfinished work; more than one person holds the keys; it has a steward rather than a proprietor; and it has survived at least one hard thing. What does not work: a place that exists because of a grant; a place whose programming one person decides; a place that closes when its founder is tired; a place the neighborhood visits but would not defend.

You hold eleven questions internally. You do not ask them as a list; you learn most of the answers by being there and looking for them. The full set with what each is looking for is in the tools file. The short conversational set you can actually raise:

- Who decides who comes in here?
- Who has the keys?
- Who cleans up?
- Who pays, if there is a cost?
- Who decides what happens here?

If every answer routes to one person, the convener, a landlord, an institution, the space is not the neighborhood's. If the answers distribute across neighborhood members who recognize each other as the answer-givers, it is.

Three of the harder questions you will not ask directly. Cultural fit (whose language is spoken here, in every sense), safety (do people say things here they would not say elsewhere), and history under stress (has this place been through something hard and come out the other side) are answered by what you observe across the pre-work period. Go to what happens there when it is not your meeting.

**If you are a founder yourself.** Chris and Jerry are. The question is whether the neighborhood has adopted the space as its own or whether it is still your space the neighborhood visits. Ask someone you trust to walk the five questions for you and tell you the truth. If your role has moved toward steward, the place has crossed over. If everything still routes to you, it has not, and the discipline is to say so.

**What a steward is, here.** A proprietor owns the agenda: decides who comes in, what happens, and when it closes, and the place ends when they do. A steward keeps the place open and safe and decides almost nothing else alone. Specifically: a steward holds a key and so do others; opens the door and lets other people decide what happens inside; absorbs costs or finds them covered without using that to set the program; settles disputes about the space by convening the people who use it rather than by ruling; can be away for a month and the place goes on; and could be replaced by the neighborhood, and knows it. The mechanic in the worked example below calls himself the landlord and behaves like a steward; the behavior is what counts.

### Filter two: small groups already getting things done

Any place with any vestige of neighborhood already has small groups doing work outside business and government: the mutual aid group, the food distribution, the informal childcare ring, the trail crew, the elders who check on people alone. If those groups exist and are findable, the neighborhood has the ground this work needs, whether or not the founded commons is fully formed yet; the two usually come together.

These groups are, most often, associations: named or unnamed bodies of organized volunteers doing real work, the oldest form of civic life this country has. Tocqueville noticed it in the 1830s and made it the center of his account of American democracy: Americans of every age, condition, and disposition, he wrote, form associations for everything, to give fêtes, found seminaries, build inns, send missionaries, and where in France you would find the government and in England a lord, in the United States you would find an association. John McKnight, whose work runs through the whole Design Record, built his distinction between associational care and professional service on the same observation: an association is people who came together by choice around something they care about, whose care is a gift and whose members are its authority. The small groups you are looking for are associations in this sense, whether or not they have a name, a bank account, or a meeting time. They are what the field guide amplifies, and they are what an institution captures when it turns one into its program.

Find them. Ask each person you talk to who else is getting things done. Follow the names. Keep a list of the groups, what they do, who holds them together, and what they would want if they could have it, which you will hear if you listen and must not solicit yet.

Keep that list in an RCN Table from the start, one row per group: name, what it does, who holds it together, where it meets, what it would want, who named it to you. The Table is the neighborhood's first structured record of itself, it feeds the linkage map in Phase 2, and it goes into the Neo4j graph under the RCN schema so that the Workbench can read it later. This is a tool step the tools file names; the Table exists, the schema entry for a small group is work still to do.

Notice the business owners and government officials who appear in peripheral roles around these groups. That is a sign of health. Notice too whether any institution, and especially any philanthropic institution, is already treating a small group as its own; that is the capture pattern, and it matters for Phase 4.

### When one filter is present and the other is not

Patience, and patience is not optional. If there are small groups but no founded commons: know who the groups are, stay in touch, do not invite them into Phase 2, do not offer money, and watch for whether a place emerges, a garage that gets activated, a church basement the neighborhood adopts as its own, a maker space. When it does, they may be ready. If there is a founded commons but no groups have formed around it: do not fund the place as if it were the neighborhood's collective work, do not invite the founder alone into Phase 2, and watch for whether groups begin using the space for their own initiatives. The wrong response in either case is to force what is missing onto the granting arm's timeline.

Money poured onto ground without these two things runs off like rain on cement. Nothing grows.

**If neither is present, or if what is missing will not emerge soon.** Say so, plainly and without blame, to the people you have been talking with: this neighborhood is not ready to be worked with in this way, and here is what is missing. Then offer the generative alternative. Highlander 3.0 will be inviting cohorts of neighborhood actionists, Chris Casillas's word, to spend weeks in places with vibrant founded commons, Superior, Lansing, Whatcom, learning from the places and from one another. Learning with peers and by immersion is one of the ways new founded commons come to be founded. Introduce the people who would go to each other and to Chris, Jerry, and Carl. And offer mentorship: a convener from a neighborhood that is already self-organizing can be of real help to a nascent leader in one that is not yet, in ways no guide can be.

## Coffee conversations

Most of Phase 1 is one-to-one or few-to-one conversations, over coffee or its equivalent, in the neighborhood, at the person's convenience. Aim for fifteen to twenty-five across the weeks, weighted toward the people who hold the small groups together and the people the small groups serve.

**What you are doing.** Finding out whether there is any shared interest in creating a more expansive shared future in the neighborhood. Listening for the mood. Learning the map: who does what, who knows whom, who is trusted, where the frictions between institutions land on residents. Listening for what people would want.

**What you are not doing.** Recruiting. Pitching. Promising. Explaining RCN. If someone asks what this is, say it in a sentence: a way for the people already getting things done here to see the whole picture together, decide what to work on, and perhaps get a little help doing it. Then ask what they are working on.

**Questions that open.** What are you and your people working on right now? What gets in the way? When something goes wrong for a family here, where do they go first? Who around here always seems to know what is going on? If you could have one thing different by this time next year, what would it be?

**Listen for the moods.** Resentment about what an institution did will be accurate and cannot be facilitated away; acknowledge what happened, and hold the possibility that work is still possible without minimizing it. Resignation, "we've tried, nothing changes here," is the mood you most need to notice, because a room in it cannot do Phase 2; name it gently if naming helps, and never argue with it. Acceptance, "this is what we have and we can work with it," is the mood you are hoping to find, and curiosity and ambition after it.

**Keep notes** in your own words the same day: who, what they do, what they said they want, what mood, who they named. This is the beginning of the neighborhood's linkage map and you will bring it into Phase 2.

## The e-VSM survey, before

Once you know who the small groups are and you expect to proceed, run the e-VSM survey of the neighborhood. It gives you a multi-perspective picture of how the neighborhood sees its own eleven capacities and the relationships among them, before the loudest voice in a room sets the frame, and it is the baseline that Phase 5 will compare against.

Before this guide goes to the four conveners, Marc and Claude check the neighborhood version of the e-VSM survey against this guide and Section 3 of the Design Record and update its labels and questions to fit; the survey was written before the guide and needs the pass.

Set up the survey with the neighborhood's own expressions for the spheres, ask the small-group holders and a spread of residents to fill it in, and have respondents choose their worlds, so that Phase 2 can see where different people see different things. Run the aggregator's synthesis and read it before the first meeting.

**Present the results at the meeting.** The first pass of this guide said not to, on the reasoning that showing the survey first would let it frame the room before the room made its own map. Marc's judgment is that this is wrong, and it is: the survey is the neighborhood's own several views of itself, gathered from more people than will be in the room, and withholding it would mean the convener knew something the room did not. So bring it, after the WHY round and before the map is built, and show it as what it is, several worlds' pictures side by side, with the places they agree and the places they diverge, and the free text in respondents' own words. Do not present it as findings that settle anything; present it as what the neighborhood has already said, for the room to argue with. Phase 2 says where it goes in the meeting.

**Worlds.** A world is a group a respondent says they belong to, so that the aggregator can show that group's picture beside the others. Choose three or four per survey, no more, from a list like this, in the neighborhood's own words: longtime resident (ten years or more); newer arrival (under three years); renter; owner; under thirty; over sixty; raising children; caring for an elder; works in the neighborhood; owns a business here; employed by an institution that serves the neighborhood (school, clinic, agency, church); member of a faith community here; speaks a language other than English at home; financially precarious; holds a small group together; receives services from one of the institutions on the map. Which three or four depends on where you expect the neighborhood to see itself differently; in the worked example below it was newer arrival, longtime resident, works at an institution, and holds a group. This list is a first pass for Marc and Claude to refine.

The tools file says how to set the survey up. If the survey is too much for this neighborhood's first cycle, skip it and say so in your notes; do not let it become the reason the meeting does not happen.

## Enlisting

By the end of Phase 1 you invite the people who will be in the room for Phase 2. Eight to fifteen people, which is what a founded commons can seat for a working session and what a linkage map on a wall can hold.

Who: the people who hold the small groups together; two or three residents whose lives are caught between institutions, who will be the work's focus; one or two institutional people who show up in the small groups' work already, a school nurse, a clinic's outreach worker, chosen because they are trusted here and not because their institution sent them; and, if the neighborhood has one, the person who founded and stewards the commons.

How: in person, one at a time, by someone they trust, which may not be you. The invitation is to one long meeting, or two half-days, at the founded commons, to look at the whole picture together and discover who commits to what to work on. Say what it is and what it is not. Say who else is coming. Ask them to think about what gets in the way for the people they care about.

Write down who accepted, who declined, and why. Declining is a legitimate answer and tells you something.

## Handoff to Phase 2

You are ready for Phase 2 when: both filters are met; you have the small groups in an RCN Table with who holds them; you have eight to fifteen people who have said yes; the founded commons has agreed to hold the meeting and to keep the map on its wall afterward; you have the e-VSM baseline or a note saying why not; and the mood of the people coming is at least acceptance.

If any of these is missing, Phase 1 is not done.

## Worked example — Phase 1

**Honesty note.** The neighborhood, the school, and the resettlement agency in this example are constructed. Spokane linkage map #7 documents the institutions and relationships (Schools, World Relief, CHW Network, Family & Individual; SHARE_CLIENT, PRESENTS_TO, REQUEST_COORD), and the Medford GGCS backlog holds the scenario type; neither documents a Phase 1 in a neighborhood. Everything below about conversations and moods is what this guide expects, not what happened.

The convener has lived in this neighborhood for eleven years and runs the food distribution on Tuesdays. Over three weeks she has coffee with nineteen people. Six of them hold small groups: the Tuesday food distribution, a Somali mothers' group that meets at the mosque, a childcare ring among four families on one block, a tenants' association at the two apartment buildings that house most of the newer arrivals, a bicycle repair evening in a garage, and the CHW network's two workers who cover this part of town. The school nurse turns up in three of these conversations without being invited, because she comes to the food distribution. The six groups go into an RCN Table, one row each, with who holds them and what each said it would want.

The place is the garage. Two years ago a retired mechanic started opening it on Thursday evenings to fix kids' bikes; the tenants' association now meets there, the mothers' group has used it twice for something the mosque could not host, and when the convener asks who has the keys, the answer is four names. The mechanic calls himself the landlord and behaves like a steward. It is a founded commons, small, with a history of one hard thing (a complaint to the city about the noise, resolved by the neighbors themselves).

The mood in most conversations is acceptance with an undertow of resentment toward the school district, which twice announced programs for new families and twice let them lapse when a grant ended. Three of the Somali mothers are in resignation about the school specifically: nothing will change there. The convener does not argue. She notes it, and notes that all three said the CHW who speaks their language is the only reason their children are enrolled at all.

She runs the e-VSM survey with fourteen respondents in four worlds: newer arrival, longtime resident, works at an institution serving the neighborhood, holds a small group. Two things stand out in the synthesis: the relationship between "awareness within the neighborhood" and "coordination" is marked Disagree by every newer-arrival respondent and Agree by every longtime resident, and the free text from the mothers keeps mentioning the first weeks of school. She will show the room both.

She invites twelve people to a Saturday at the garage: the six group holders, the school nurse, one of the CHWs, two mothers from the Somali group, the mechanic, and a father from the childcare ring whose daughter started school this year. Eleven say yes. The tenants' association chair declines, saying the last time anyone mapped anything here it went in a report; the convener writes that down.
