# Phase 1 — Understand and enlist

**Purpose.** Learn whether this neighborhood is ready, find its small groups and its place, survey how it sees itself, and invite the right people into the first meeting. Or learn that it is not ready, say what is missing, and stay in relationship.

**Conversation type.** Coffee. You are finding out whether there is shared interest in building anything at all. Do not ask anyone for a commitment in this phase.

**Reasoning.** Design Record Sections 3 (what the neighborhood has to have already), 4 (the founded commons and the small groups filter), 5 (the scenario repository), 7 (moods).

**Time.** Three to four weeks, mostly in ones and twos.

## Before you start: your own preparation

Do three things before you talk to anyone.

**Orient yourself to moods.** Read the one page in the tools file. You need to be able to tell resignation from acceptance and ambition from a wish, in yourself first. Most of Phase 1 is listening for the mood of a place, and a convener who arrives in ambition into a neighborhood in resentment will hear nothing true.

**Read the scenarios that already exist.** The FedWiki scenario repository holds scenarios other neighborhoods have written. Read the ones that plausibly apply here, so that when someone tells you about a family caught between a school and a clinic you recognize it as a scenario others have named, and can say so. Do not bring the scenarios to the neighborhood yet; bring the awareness.

**Know your own Platform relationship.** You are, or will be, an Industry Platform actor: someone whose compensation and reputation ride on what the MEs you seed deliver, and who is paid downstream of residents' recognition. Be clear with yourself about that before you invite anyone, because it is the honest answer to the question "what's in it for you," and someone will ask.

## The two filters

A neighborhood is ready for Phase 2 when both of these are present. Your Phase 1 job is to find out, and to be willing to say no.

### Filter one: a founded commons

The neighborhood has a place its people created for their own collective work, recognize as theirs, use as a commons, and that no outside actor directs. Leo's in Superior and The Fledge in Lansing are the reference cases. A friendly coffee shop with a corner table is not one; a church basement the pastor lends is usually not one; a room the county lets you use is not one.

You hold eleven questions internally. You do not ask them as a list; you learn most of the answers by being there over the weeks. The full set with what each is looking for is in the tools file. The short conversational set you can actually raise:

- Who decides who comes in here?
- Who has the keys?
- Who cleans up?
- Who pays, if there is a cost?
- Who decides what happens here?

If every answer routes to one person, the convener, a landlord, an institution, the space is not the neighborhood's. If the answers distribute across neighborhood members who recognize each other as the answer-givers, it is.

Three of the harder questions you will not ask directly. Cultural fit (whose language is spoken here, in every sense), safety (do people say things here they would not say elsewhere), and history under stress (has this place been through something hard and come out the other side) are answered by what you observe across the pre-work period. Go to what happens there when it is not your meeting.

**If you are a founder yourself.** Chris and Jerry are. The question is whether the neighborhood has adopted the space as its own or whether it is still your space the neighborhood visits. Ask someone you trust to walk the five questions for you and tell you the truth. If your role has moved toward steward, the place has crossed over. If everything still routes to you, it has not, and the discipline is to say so.

### Filter two: small groups already getting things done

Any place with any vestige of neighborhood already has small groups doing work outside business and government: the mutual aid group, the food distribution, the informal childcare ring, the trail crew, the elders who check on people alone. If those groups exist and are findable, the neighborhood has the ground this work needs, whether or not the founded commons is fully formed yet; the two usually come together.

Find them. Ask each person you talk to who else is getting things done. Follow the names. Keep a list of the groups, what they do, who holds them together, and what they would want if they could have it, which you will hear if you listen and must not solicit yet.

Notice the business owners and government officials who appear in peripheral roles around these groups. That is a sign of health. Notice too whether any institution is already treating a small group as its program; that is the capture pattern, and it matters for Phase 4.

### When one filter is present and the other is not

Patience, and patience is operational. If there are small groups but no founded commons: know who the groups are, stay in touch, do not invite them into Phase 2, do not offer money, and watch for whether a place emerges, a garage that gets activated, a church basement the neighborhood adopts as its own, a maker space. When it does, you are ready. If there is a founded commons but no groups have formed around it: do not fund the place as if it were the neighborhood's collective work, do not invite the founder alone into Phase 2, and watch for whether groups begin using the space for their own initiatives. The wrong response in either case is to force what is missing onto the granting arm's timeline.

Money poured onto ground without these two things runs off like rain on cement. Nothing grows.

**If neither is present, or if what is missing will not emerge soon.** Say so, plainly and without blame, to the people you have been talking with: this neighborhood is not ready to be worked with in this way, and here is what is missing. Then offer the generative alternative. Highlander 3.0 takes cohorts of neighborhood actionists, Chris Casillas's word, to spend weeks in places with vibrant founded commons, Superior, Lansing, Whatcom, learning from the places and from one another. Learning by immersion is one of the ways new founded commons come to be founded. Introduce the people who would go.

## Coffee conversations

Most of Phase 1 is one-to-one and two-to-one conversations, over coffee or its equivalent, in the neighborhood, at the person's convenience. Aim for fifteen to twenty-five across the weeks, weighted toward the people who hold the small groups together and the people the small groups serve.

**What you are doing.** Finding out whether there is any shared interest in creating a shared future. Listening for the mood. Learning the map: who does what, who knows whom, who is trusted, where the frictions between institutions land on residents. Hearing, without asking, what people would want.

**What you are not doing.** Recruiting. Pitching. Promising. Explaining RCN. If someone asks what this is, say it in a sentence: a way for the people already getting things done here to see the whole picture together, decide what to work on, and get a little help doing it. Then ask what they are working on.

**Questions that open.** What are you and your people working on right now? What gets in the way? When something goes wrong for a family here, where do they go first? Who around here always seems to know what is going on? If you could have one thing different by this time next year, what would it be?

**Listen for the moods.** Resentment about what an institution did will be accurate and cannot be facilitated away; acknowledge what happened, and hold the possibility that work is still possible without minimizing it. Resignation, "we've tried, nothing changes here," is the mood you most need to notice, because a room in it cannot do Phase 2; name it gently if naming helps, and never argue with it. Acceptance, "this is what we have and we can work with it," is the mood you are hoping to find, and ambition after it.

**Keep notes** in your own words the same day: who, what they do, what they said they want, what mood, who they named. This is the beginning of the neighborhood's linkage map and you will bring it into Phase 2.

## The e-VSM survey, before

Once you know who the small groups are and you expect to proceed, run the e-VSM survey of the neighborhood. It gives you a multi-perspective picture of how the neighborhood sees its own eleven capacities and the relationships among them, before the loudest voice in a room sets the frame, and it is the baseline that Phase 5 will compare against.

Set up the survey with the neighborhood's own words for the spheres, ask the small-group holders and a spread of residents to fill it in, and use the worlds (longtime resident, under thirty, business owner, financially precarious, whatever fits here) so that Phase 2 can see where different people see different things. Run the aggregator's synthesis and read it before the first meeting. Do not present the results at the meeting as findings; use them to know what the room may not say.

The tools file says how to set it up. If the survey is too much for this neighborhood's first cycle, skip it and say so in your notes; do not let it become the reason the meeting does not happen.

## Enlisting

By the end of Phase 1 you invite the people who will be in the room for Phase 2. Eight to fifteen people, which is what a founded commons can seat for a working session and what a linkage map on a wall can hold.

Who: the people who hold the small groups together; two or three residents whose lives are caught between institutions, who will be the map's center; one or two institutional people who show up in the small groups' work already, a school nurse, a clinic's outreach worker, chosen because they are trusted here and not because their institution sent them; and, if the neighborhood has one, the person who founded and stewards the commons.

How: in person, one at a time, by someone they trust, which may not be you. The invitation is to one long meeting, or two half-days, at the founded commons, to look at the whole picture together and decide what to work on. Say what it is and what it is not. Say who else is coming. Ask them to think about what gets in the way for the people they serve.

Write down who accepted, who declined, and why. Declining is a legitimate answer and tells you something.

## Handoff to Phase 2

You are ready for Phase 2 when: both filters are met; you have a list of the small groups and who holds them; you have eight to fifteen people who have said yes; the founded commons has agreed to hold the meeting and to keep the map on its wall afterward; you have the e-VSM baseline or a note saying why not; and the mood of the people coming is at least acceptance.

If any of these is missing, Phase 1 is not done.

## Worked example — Phase 1

**Honesty note.** The neighborhood, the school, and the resettlement agency in this example are constructed. Spokane linkage map #7 documents the institutions and relationships (Schools, World Relief, CHW Network, Family & Individual; SHARE_CLIENT, PRESENTS_TO, REQUEST_COORD), and the Medford GGCS backlog holds the scenario type; neither documents a Phase 1 in a neighborhood. Everything below about conversations and moods is what this guide expects, not what happened.

The convener has lived in this neighborhood for eleven years and runs the food distribution on Tuesdays. Over three weeks she has coffee with nineteen people. Six of them hold small groups: the Tuesday food distribution, a Somali mothers' group that meets at the mosque, a childcare ring among four families on one block, a tenants' association at the two apartment buildings that house most of the newer arrivals, a bicycle repair evening in a garage, and the CHW network's two workers who cover this part of town. The school nurse turns up in three of these conversations without being invited, because she comes to the food distribution.

The place is the garage. Two years ago a retired mechanic started opening it on Thursday evenings to fix kids' bikes; the tenants' association now meets there, the mothers' group has used it twice for something the mosque could not host, and when the convener asks who has the keys, the answer is four names. The mechanic calls himself the landlord and behaves like a steward. It is a founded commons, small, with a history of one hard thing (a complaint to the city about the noise, resolved by the neighbors themselves).

The mood in most conversations is acceptance with an undertow of resentment toward the school district, which twice announced programs for new families and twice let them lapse when a grant ended. Three of the Somali mothers are in resignation about the school specifically: nothing will change there. The convener does not argue. She notes it, and notes that all three said the CHW who speaks their language is the only reason their children are enrolled at all.

She runs the e-VSM survey with fourteen respondents in four worlds. Two things stand out in the synthesis: the relationship between "awareness within the neighborhood" and "coordination" is marked Disagree by every newer-arrival respondent and Agree by every longtime resident, and the free text from the mothers keeps mentioning the first weeks of school.

She invites twelve people to a Saturday at the garage: the six group holders, the school nurse, one of the CHWs, two mothers from the Somali group, the mechanic, and a father from the childcare ring whose daughter started school this year. Eleven say yes. The tenants' association chair declines, saying the last time anyone mapped anything here it went in a report; the convener writes that down.
