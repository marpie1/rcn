# The RCN Field Guide — a tool kit for neighborhood conveners

**First pass, 2026-09-11.** Drafted by Claude in Claude Code against the RCN Design Record as it stood the same day, for Marc Pierson to react to. Nothing in it has been run in a neighborhood. Where the Design Record holds the reasoning, this guide holds the moves.

## Who this is for

You are convening a neighborhood: Carl in East County, Chris at Leo's in Superior, Jerry at The Fledge in Lansing, Brent in southwest Lansing, or someone who follows them. You already occupy the convener position. You have a portfolio of things you have done for this place, and people who would come if you asked. This guide does not make you a convener. It gives you a sequence to run and the tools to run it with, so that the neighborhood ends up able to do it without you.

You do not need to have read the RCN Design Record to use this guide. Each phase says where in the record the reasoning lives, for when you want it.

## What the guide does, in one paragraph

Over about three months, you help a neighborhood's small groups see how they and the local institutions currently work together around residents' lives, design what they would want if history did not bind them, choose a few scenarios worth working on, charter a small self-organizing team around each with explicit promises and a little seed money, and then do the work, look at what happened, and settle up on what residents recognize. Then the neighborhood does it again, with less of you.

## The five phases

The guide is organized by phase, and each phase has its own file. Read all five once to see the arc. Then work from the current phase's file while you are in it.

| phase | what happens | roughly |
|---|---|---|
| Phase 1 — Understand and enlist | you learn whether this neighborhood is ready, find its small groups and its place, and invite | weeks 1–4 |
| Phase 2 — See the system, design what is wanted | one long meeting, or two half-days: the linkage map and the Idealized Design; scenarios surface in the gap | weeks 4–6 |
| Phase 3 — Choose | the weighted selection: which scenarios this cycle will work on | weeks 6–7 |
| Phase 4 — Charter | the scenario written, the ecosystem sketched, the value network drawn, the shares agreed, the promises made, the seed released | weeks 7–10 |
| Phase 5 — Do, look, settle, and go again | the ME works; the neighborhood measures; the settlement; the next round, or graduation | weeks 10–13 and on |

The five phases descend from the five-phase Linkage Mapping playbook Cambridge Management Group ran at county scale in Jackson County, Oregon, and Spokane in 2014–2016, over about seven months. At neighborhood scale the timeline compresses to three, and the structural changes described in the Design Record's Section 2 are what keep the neighborhood from depending on you the way those counties depended on CMG.

## Three things to hold before you start

**Two hard filters.** A neighborhood without a founded commons, a place its people already recognize as theirs, safe, culturally its own, that will hold recurring gatherings for months, is not ready, and neither is a neighborhood without small groups already getting things done outside business and government. Phase 1 exists to find out. "Not yet, and here is what is missing" is a legitimate outcome, including in your own neighborhood, which is the harder discipline.

**Three kinds of conversation.** A coffee conversation asks whether there is any shared interest at all. A conversation for possibilities asks what could exist. A conversation for action commits specific people to specific work by specific times. Phase 1 is coffee, Phase 2 is possibilities, Phase 3 turns possibilities into the ground for action, Phase 4 is action. Skipping ahead, asking for promises in a possibilities conversation or floating possibilities when the room is ready to commit, is the most common way conveners lose a room. The guide names which conversation each moment is.

**Moods are information.** A room in resignation cannot design; a room in resentment cannot be facilitated past what happened to it; a room in acceptance can work. You will not teach anyone this vocabulary. You will use it yourself, and name a mood out loud only when naming it would help. The Kit has the one-page version, pattern 19.

## What this guide is not

It is not a program to adopt, a curriculum to deliver, or a service to render. It does not create conveners, founded commons, or small groups; it works with the ones that exist. It is not a route to operating funding; the money in it is catalytic seed capital that departs. And it is not compatible with anyone being paid to facilitate or to give grants on a salary that does not depend on what residents recognize. If that is the arrangement on offer, this is the wrong guide.

## The worked example

One scenario is carried through all five phases: a refugee student's first year in a US school. It comes from Spokane linkage map #7 (School-Refugee Transition) and the Medford GGCS backlog, and the Design Record's Sections 5 and 8 develop it. Nobody has run this scenario through all five phases under this design. So the example is a composite, and each phase's example opens with a short note saying which parts are documented history and which are constructed to show what the phase would produce. Read the notes; the example is only as honest as they are.

## The tools

The RCN foundational tools carry parts of every phase: FedWiki for the scenario pages, the e-VSM survey before and after, the RCN Graph Tool for the linkage map, the ecosystem sketch, and the value network, SODOTO for portfolios, CfA-dSC for the charter, Value Stream Mapping and A3 for the work inside an institution. Some steps have no tool yet and are done on paper. The Kit says which is which, and the Design Record's Section 9 diagram (`diagrams/me-lifecycle-end-to-end.rcn.json`) colors every step by its state. Do not wait for a tool to be built to run a phase; every step in this guide can be done by hand, and running it by hand is how we learn which tool to build next.
