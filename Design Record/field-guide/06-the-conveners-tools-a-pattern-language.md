# The convener's tools — a pattern language

**Second pass, 2026-09-14**, from Marc's edits to the first. This is Marc's idea, written out: the tools and methods of the field guide as a pattern language on Alexander's model. Each pattern helps complete larger patterns above it and is completed by smaller patterns below it, so the whole forms a network of supporting and supported tools rather than a list. A convener grabs a pattern when a phase calls for it and follows its links to what it needs.

Each pattern has the same parts, each on its own line.

**Where it fits** says which phase of the cycle the pattern belongs to and which larger patterns it helps complete.

**Problem** says, plainly, what goes wrong without it.

**Therefore** is the move.

**Needs** names the smaller patterns this one depends on, with a phrase saying what each supplies.

**State** says whether a tool carries the pattern today. *Built* means an RCN tool does it now. *Partial* means a tool exists and needs extending for this use. *Paper* means there is no tool; the pattern is done by hand, on paper or a wall, and that works.

**Learn it from** points at what already exists: the intro, manual, deck, or schema in the rcn repository, and the section of the Design Record that holds the reasoning.

**Skills** is the Basic / Intermediate / Advanced ladder, the seed for the SODOTO pages that will teach each pattern in FedWiki. Every tool and method needs these three pages; this is the first statement of what each level means.

The network is drawn in `../diagrams/field-guide-pattern-language.rcn.json`. The numbering follows the cycle: pattern 0 is the cycle itself; then the patterns of readiness, then seeing, choosing, chartering, doing and settling, and last the patterns that run through everything.

---

## 0. The Cycle

**Where it fits.** The largest pattern. Every other pattern in this language helps complete it.

**Problem.** Without a sequence a neighborhood can run and repeat, good work happens once, around one person, and stops when they stop.

**Therefore.** Run the five phases, understand and enlist, see the system and design what is wanted, choose, charter, do and look and settle, over about three months, in the neighborhood's own founded commons, and then run them again with less of the convener in them. Each phase has a file in this guide.

**Needs.** All of the patterns below. The picture of the five phases is `../diagrams/field-guide-five-phases.rcn.json`.

**State.** Paper. The cycle is a sequence of meetings and conversations; the tools carry its steps.

**Learn it from.** `00-how-to-use-this-guide.md`; the Medford playbook in Confluence (LMID space, "Phases of Linkage Mapping Work"); Design Record Sections 1 and 2.

**Skills.**
Basic: can name the five phases and say what each produces.
Intermediate: can run one cycle in a neighborhood with this guide open.
Advanced: can run a cycle, then hand phases over one at a time until the neighborhood runs it alone.

## 1. Founded Commons Diagnostic

**Where it fits.** Phase 1. One of the two filters that decide whether the cycle starts at all.

**Problem.** Work done in a borrowed room ends when the lender loses patience, and the neighborhood never saw it as its own.

**Therefore.** Before inviting anyone, find out whether the neighborhood has a place its people created, recognize as theirs, use as a commons, and that no outside actor directs. Answer these eleven questions: origin; the founder's relationship now; governance in practice; access and membership; safety; cultural fit; function match; financial durability; constituency; history under stress; neighborhood recognition. Ask the five short ones out loud: who decides who comes in, who has the keys, who cleans up, who pays, who decides what happens. Learn the hard three by being there: cultural fit (whose language is spoken, in every sense), safety (do people say things here they would not say elsewhere), and history under stress (has the place been through something hard and come out the other side). Decline to proceed if there is no viable founded commons in place.

**Needs.** Coffee Conversation, where most of the answers come from. Moods Assessment, to hear whether the place is in acceptance. Patience and Highlander 3.0, for what to do when the answer is no.

**State.** Paper. The questions are held by the convener and answered by observation; no tool.

**Learn it from.** Design Record Section 4 has the full eleven with what each is looking for; `01-phase-1-understand-and-enlist.md` has the examples inside and outside RCN and the definition of a steward.

**Skills.**
Basic: can find answers to the five questions in a neighborhood.
Intermediate: can find answers to the eleven questions, including the hard three, from observation over weeks, and write a short honest report on the findings.
Advanced: one or more locals can now do this assessment themselves and can teach a new convener to see the difference between a founded commons and a friendly space.

## 2. Small Groups Filter

**Where it fits.** Phase 1. The second of the two filters.

**Problem.** A neighborhood with no groups already getting things done has nothing for the cycle to amplify, and money poured on it runs off like rain on cement.

**Therefore.** Find the small groups doing work outside business and government, the associations, named or not, that Tocqueville found everywhere and McKnight built his work on: mutual aid, food distribution, the childcare ring, the trail crew, the elders who check on people alone. Follow the names from one conversation to the next. Keep one row per group in an RCN Table: what it does, who holds it together, where it meets, what it would want, who named it. Notice business owners and officials in peripheral roles, which is health, and any institution treating a group as its program, which is capture. Proceed only if the groups exist and are findable.

**Needs.** Coffee Conversation, which is how the names are followed. The RCN Table, to keep the list where the linkage map and the Neo4j graph can use it.

**State.** Paper for the finding; the RCN Table exists for the list; a schema entry for a small group in Neo4j is work to do.

**Learn it from.** Design Record Section 4, "Beyond the diagnostic"; `01-phase-1-understand-and-enlist.md` on associations; `tools/rcn-table-intro.pptx` for the Table.

**Skills.**
Basic: can find and list the groups by following names.
Intermediate: can tell a group from a program, and a healthy peripheral role from capture, and keep the Table current.
Advanced: can see from the list which groups will form the center of the first map, and which institution is about to capture which group.

## 3. Coffee Conversation

**Where it fits.** Phase 1. It supplies the answers to both filters and the names for Enlisting.

**Problem.** Conveners recruit before they know whether anyone shares their interest, and get polite yeses that evaporate.

**Therefore.** Meet one or two people at a time, in the neighborhood, over coffee or its equivalent, and find out whether they see the world in enough shared terms to imagine building anything together. Ask what they are working on and what gets in the way. Do not pitch, promise, or explain. Write notes the same day: who, what they do, what they want, what mood, who they named.

**Needs.** Moods Assessment, to hear what is under the words.

**State.** Paper.

**Learn it from.** Design Record Section 7, "Conversations for coffee, possibilities, and action" (Marc and Robin Asby's addition to Dunham); `01-phase-1-understand-and-enlist.md`, "Coffee conversations."

**Skills.**
Basic: can have one without asking for anything.
Intermediate: can hear the mood and the map in what is said, and write it down.
Advanced: can tell from a set of coffee conversations whether a neighborhood is in acceptance, and who should be in the room.

## 4. Enlisting

**Where it fits.** The end of Phase 1 and the door into Phase 2. It turns the two filters and the coffee conversations into a room that can make a map: the right eight to fifteen people, invited by the right person, to the right place.

**Problem.** The wrong room, too big, full of institutional representatives sent by their bosses, with nobody present whose life is at the center, cannot make a map worth having.

**Therefore.** Invite eight to fifteen people: the holders of the small groups; two or three residents whose lives are caught between institutions; one or two institutional people who already show up in the groups' work and are trusted here, chosen for trust and not for title; and the steward of the founded commons. Invite in person, one at a time, by someone they trust, which may not be you. Say what the meeting is and is not, and who else is coming. Record who declined and why; a decline is information.

**Needs.** Coffee Conversation, which found the people. The Small Groups Filter, whose Table is the invitation list.

**State.** Paper.

**Learn it from.** `01-phase-1-understand-and-enlist.md`, "Enlisting."

**Skills.**
Basic: can make the list of people and make the invitations.
Intermediate: can choose the institutional people by trust rather than title, and choose who should do the inviting.
Advanced: can read a decline as information and change the room because of it.

## 5. e-VSM Survey, Before and After

**Where it fits.** Phase 1 for the baseline, Phase 4 to validate a scenario, Phase 5 for the comparison. It supplies evidence to Enlisting, the Retrospective, and the Neighborhood Scorecard.

**Problem.** A room's picture of the neighborhood gets set by its loudest voice, and afterward nobody can say whether an ME changed the neighborhood or only hit its own targets.

**Therefore.** Survey the neighborhood on the eleven spheres and thirty-three paired homeostats before the first meeting, with the neighborhood's own words for the spheres and respondents choosing three or four worlds; run the aggregator's synthesis; show the results to the room after the WHY round; survey again on the same pairs after the ME's work and compare.

**Needs.** The Scenario Repository, for the scenario-specific items. The Neighborhood Scorecard, for Rasch calibration when there are enough respondents.

**State.** Built: `evsm-svg-v3.html`, `evsm-aggregator.html`, `evsm-report.html`, `evsm_excel_tool.html`. Not built: Rasch calibration of the results, the environment interface, the neighborhood version's re-check against this guide.

**Learn it from.** `docs/evsm-intro.html`, `evsm-workflow.html`, `evsm-architecture.html`, and the four manuals (survey, aggregator, report, excel); Design Record Section 3's shared reference; `01-phase-1-understand-and-enlist.md` for the list of worlds.

**Skills.**
Basic: can answer the survey and read their own report.
Intermediate: can set up a survey with local labels and worlds, snapshot it, collect responses, and run the aggregator with synthesis.
Advanced: can design scenario-specific items, run compare mode across worlds and across time, read the homeostatic-pairs report, and judge when Rasch calibration is warranted.

## 6. The Six Questions, WHY First

**Where it fits.** Phase 2, the opening of both sessions. It frames the Linkage Map and the Idealized Design.

**Problem.** A room that starts on the problem never finds out why it is together, and the map it makes is nobody's.

**Therefore.** Ask WHY first, around the room, from where each person stands, and write every answer on the wall in their words. Then WHERE, WHEN, and WHO. WHAT is session two's Idealized Design. HOW comes last, in Phases 3 to 5, because a room that starts with HOW does the wrong thing righter. These are declarations; hold space for declaring rather than for asserting a right answer. Keep the WHYs; they are a declaration of independence.

**Needs.** Moods Assessment, to hear resignation in a WHY and not argue. Speech Acts, to know a declaration when you hear one.

**State.** Paper.

**Learn it from.** `02-phase-2-see-the-system-design-what-is-wanted.md`; Design Record Section 7, "Phase 2's WHY sequence"; Marc's 2014 Idealized Design deck.

**Skills.**
Basic: can run the WHY round without shortening it.
Intermediate: can hear resignation in a WHY and go on without arguing.
Advanced: can bring the WHYs back at the right moment in a later phase, and update them with the group over time.

## 7. Linkage Map

**Where it fits.** Phase 2, session one. It is the current-state picture that Idealized Design designs against and that scenario areas surface from; one of the neighborhood's standing cave drawings.

**Problem.** Nobody in a neighborhood can see how its institutions and groups actually work together around a resident's life, so the failures between them are invisible or are blamed on people.

**Therefore.** With a resident's situation at the center, put up the parts and draw the flows, clients, information, materials, money, each arrow labeled with what moves and the missing ones dotted, from three perspectives: mainstay (the client's), support (the infrastructure's), driver (the stakeholders'). Fifteen to twenty parts, readable at arm's length. Start from pain points between institutions, or from one client's end-to-end needs. Keep it on the wall; enter it in the Graph Tool afterward and validate it with the group.

**Needs.** The Six Questions, which frame it. Moods Assessment, to keep resentment on the map and off the room. The RCN Graph Tool, to keep it after the wall.

**State.** Built for keeping (Graph Tool, ordinary mode with a legend of kinds); a ready linkage-map legend is work to do.

**Learn it from.** `02-phase-2-see-the-system-design-what-is-wanted.md`; Marc's Logic of VSM Linkage Maps deck (Design Record/sources/); the thirty Spokane maps; Design Record Sections 3 and 8; `tools/graph-tool-intro.html` and `graph-tool-manual.html`.

**Skills.**
Basic: can put up parts and labeled arrows on a wall with a room.
Intermediate: can hold the three levels, keep the map small enough to read, and enter it in the Graph Tool with a legend.
Advanced: can read the between-institution form off the map, see where a scenario lives in it, and compare a map across cycles.

## 8. Idealized Design

**Where it fits.** Phase 2, session two: the WHAT. It produces the wanted-state picture, and scenario areas surface in the gap between it and the Linkage Map.

**Problem.** Without a designed picture of what is wanted, a neighborhood only reacts to what is coming, and the gap that scenarios live in never appears.

**Therefore.** Tell the room the system on the wall was destroyed last night. Design the one it would want right now, subject only to being technologically feasible and able to survive in the world as it is. Keep the 2014 building blocks on a side sheet as prompts, concepts, what every design considers, principles, components, and never lecture them. Add the SWOT-type scan of what is coming. Stand the wanted design beside the current map. Remember the wanted system may need a new association, a new business, or a time-limited project as much as better coordination.

**Needs.** The Linkage Map, which it designs against. The Six Questions, for the WHY it designs toward. Moods Assessment, to invite ambition only after acceptance has settled.

**State.** Paper.

**Learn it from.** Marc's Logic of VSM Linkage Maps Idealized Design deck; Ackoff's Bell Labs lecture (Design Record/sources/); Design Record Sections 3 and 8; `02-phase-2-…md`, "Session two."

**Skills.**
Basic: can state the two constraints and hold the room to them.
Intermediate: can use the side sheet as prompts without lecturing, and run the scan.
Advanced: can keep a room in ambition without letting it become a wish list, and see the scenario areas in the gap.

## 9. Customer Scenario

**Where it fits.** Phase 2 (the interlocking-needs half) and Phase 4 (the vision-blueprint half). It is the durable object the Charter forms around and the Scenario Repository holds.

**Problem.** Programs come and go and the need they were for stays, because nothing holds the need except whoever happens to be working on it.

**Therefore.** Write the need down as a scenario, in the residents' own words, so that it outlives any team. Two levels: in Phase 2, the interlocking-needs scenario (title, situation end to end, interlocking needs, who counts as user and neighbor, where it is real, existing attempts, attribution, the invitation); in Phase 4, the vision-blueprint (value proposition, value creation process, the primary-ME's proposal, the EMC network invited, bid terms, story). Write it as a story someone would want to join. Fork one from another neighborhood when it really fits.

**Needs.** Story Structure, for what a well-formed scenario holds. The Scenario Repository, where it lives. The e-VSM Survey, to validate it before an ME forms.

**State.** Built: the FedWiki template pages. Not built: the playbook for writing one; the four conveners' own scenarios.

**Learn it from.** Marc's FedWiki pages "Customer Scenario Template" and "Writing a Customer Scenario" (Design Record/sources/pastes/); Design Record Section 5 (the fourteen elements, the bid mechanic); `04-phase-4-charter.md`, "Step one."

**Skills.**
Basic: can fill the interlocking-needs half from a Phase 2 meeting.
Intermediate: can write a vision-blueprint a reader would want to bid on, and fork one.
Advanced: can coach an Experience-ME to write from zero-distance work, and validate a scenario with an e-VSM survey.

## 10. Story Structure

**Where it fits.** Phases 2 and 4, inside Customer Scenario.

**Problem.** A scenario written as a form moves nobody.

**Therefore.** Use Marc's twenty-six-node Story Structure model as a checklist for what a well-formed scenario could hold and as a diagnostic for what a thin one is missing. Reach for it; do not memorize it.

**Needs.** The RCN Graph Tool; the model is a graph.

**State.** Built, as a graph and a FedWiki page.

**Learn it from.** Design Record/sources/pastes/turn-140-0-fedwiki-story-structure.json, Simple-Story-Structure-Plus-Organizing-Path.svg and .json; Design Record Section 5; substrate-notes.md.

**Skills.**
Basic: knows the model exists and can find it.
Intermediate: can use its categories to see what a scenario is missing.
Advanced: can teach scenario writing with it.

## 11. Weighted Selection Matrix

**Where it fits.** Phase 3, the whole of it. It turns the candidates from Phase 2 into the one to three scenarios the Charter forms around.

**Problem.** Left to a discussion, the choice goes to the loudest voice or the elders' pick.

**Therefore.** Weight the room's principles first, each person alone; score every candidate against every principle, each person alone, plus catalyzable, right scale, and who would do it; combine; meet only to adjudicate the outliers; declare the choice together. Split the cycle's seed across small, medium, and large in advance.

**Needs.** Idealized Design, whose co-defined principles are the weights. Moods Assessment, because outliers are usually moods or information rather than conflicts of value. Speech Acts, for the declaration at the end.

**State.** Paper; a tool is on the list to build.

**Learn it from.** The 2014 CMG Linkage Mapping playbook (Confluence, community4health.atlassian.net, LMID space); Design Record Sections 7 and 9; `03-phase-3-choose.md`.

**Skills.**
Basic: can run it on paper with a room.
Intermediate: can adjudicate outliers as moods and information rather than as conflicts.
Advanced: can build and maintain the matrix as a tool and run it across cycles.

## 12. Vester Sensitivity Model

**Where it fits.** An alternative for Phase 3, and an input to Idealized Design, for a neighborhood ready for it.

**Problem.** A weighted selection chooses among options but does not say where the leverage in the system is.

**Therefore.** Build Vester's impact matrix of the map's variables, rate each direct impact, and read the active, reactive, critical, and buffering variables off the cross-plot; choose scenarios that sit on active variables.

**Needs.** The Linkage Map, for the variables.

**State.** Built (SensiMod).

**Learn it from.** `docs/vester-intro.html`, `vester-manual.html`, `vester-overview.pptx`; Design Record Section 7's contrast of the two matrices.

**Skills.**
Basic: can rate a small matrix.
Intermediate: can read the four quadrants and explain them to a room.
Advanced: can run a full sensitivity model and integrate it with a linkage map.

## 13. Ecosystem Sketch — the EIP Stage

**Where it fits.** Phase 4, step two, and again at the Phase 5 retrospective. It supplies the roles the Value Network is drawn on.

**Problem.** An ME forms around whoever was in the room instead of the capacities the scenario needs.

**Therefore.** With the ME core, on the wall, sketch the ecosystem the scenario needs, Environment, Institutions, Politics, fifteen to twenty roles, missing connections drawn as questions. Keep every sketch; the series is the ME's storyboard.

**Needs.** The RCN Graph Tool (EIP mode). The Linkage Map, which it zooms in from.

**State.** Partial: EIP mode exists; the stage underlay, the wall print path, and the storyboard are on `docs/eip-stage-todo.md`.

**Learn it from.** `tools/graph-tool-manual.html` (EIP mode); `docs/eip-stage-todo.md`; `04-phase-4-charter.md`, "Step two."

**Skills.**
Basic: can sketch roles on a wall.
Intermediate: can use the EIP stage in the Graph Tool and keep a sketch series.
Advanced: can read a storyboard of sketches as the story of an ecosystem changing.

## 14. Value Network

**Where it fits.** Phase 4 (what moves and what should; the red arrows become VAM shares) and Phase 5 (the second drawing at retrospective). It is how the Neighborhood Scorecard sees the structure of value.

**Problem.** Nobody can see who is giving and getting nothing back, where value dead-ends, or whether money and service run in the same circuit.

**Therefore.** On the ecosystem's roles, draw every deliverable that moves as a labeled arrow, solid for tangible, dashed for intangible, dotted red for a return that ought to exist and does not; declare on every arrow whether it is observed or asserted; read reciprocity, dead ends, bottlenecks, circuit shape, and hop depth off the drawing. Draw it at chartering and again at retrospective and lay the two over each other.

**Needs.** The RCN Graph Tool (value-network preset and validator). The Ecosystem Sketch, for the roles.

**State.** Built.

**Learn it from.** `docs/value-network-notation.md`; `tools/schemas/graph-tool-v22.md`, "Value networks"; the six drawings in `tools/vna-*.rcn.json` and companions; Design Record Sections 8, 9, 10; `04-phase-4-charter.md`, "Step two."

**Skills.**
Basic: can draw one on paper with labeled arrows.
Intermediate: can draw it in the Graph Tool under the two rules and pass the validator.
Advanced: can read the five findings off a drawing, lay two drawings over each other, and write a VAM from the red arrows.

## 15. Value Added Mechanism

**Where it fits.** Phase 4, step three, agreed before any work; Phase 5, paid at settlement. It is the negotiated link inside the Charter.

**Problem.** Everyone is paid on a job description, or nobody is paid at all, and either way nobody's pay depends on what residents receive.

**Therefore.** Before the work starts, agree on one page, for each party: contribution, share on settlement, currency (fiat, time, gifts, reputation), lower and upper limits. Write it from the value network's red arrows. Include the team's own stake and the Platform's share, paid last.

**Needs.** The Value Network, for the flows the shares attach to. Recognition and Settlement in Four Currencies, for how it pays out. Speech Acts, because agreeing it is a conversation for action.

**State.** Paper; the research on neighborhood VAMs is not done and CfA-dSC does not carry it yet.

**Learn it from.** Design Record Sections 6 (VAM explained at first reference), 8, 9 (VAM proportion); Hamel and Zanini, HBR 2018, for Haier's; `04-phase-4-charter.md`, "Step three."

**Skills.**
Basic: can fill the one-page VAM for a small ME.
Intermediate: can negotiate limits and currencies with several parties.
Advanced: can write a VAM from a value network and settle it partially.

## 16. Charter

**Where it fits.** Phase 4, the chartering evening. It is where the cycle turns from possibilities into action.

**Problem.** Vague chartering, promises that are hopes, resources that are maybes, targets that are wishes, breaks promises later.

**Therefore.** In one evening, with the residents present: declare the scenario; declare the Leading Targets; each ME member promises specific work to the primary-ME by a date; ecosystem members offer; each institution's authorized person promises separately; the Platform promises the seed in tranches against the gates and declares its VAM share; the residents are named as those the promises are to; anyone may decline. One sheet, signed, photographed, into FedWiki.

**Needs.** Speech Acts and Conversations for Action, to name each act as it happens. The Customer Scenario, declared. The Value Added Mechanism, agreed. Catalytic Seed with Gates, promised. CfA-dSC, to record it when it can.

**State.** Partial: CfA-dSC carries two-party conversations for action; the multi-party charter is the committed enhancement. Until then the signed sheet is the contract.

**Learn it from.** `docs/cfa-dsc-intro.html`; `tools/cfa-dsc-creator.html`; Design Record Sections 7 and 8; `04-phase-4-charter.md`, "Step six."

**Skills.**
Basic: can name each speech act as it happens in a chartering.
Intermediate: can turn a hope into a promise or a declined promise without pressure, and record institutions' commitments separately.
Advanced: can run a chartering with a dozen parties and enter it in CfA-dSC.

## 17. Catalytic Seed with Gates

**Where it fits.** Phase 4 (sized and promised) and Phase 5 (released and closed out). It is the money in the Charter.

**Problem.** Seed money turns into a budget the ME cannot come down from, and the Platform is subsidizing by year three.

**Therefore.** Size the seed to what the launch actually needs; release it in tranches against three gates, launch; first resident recognition within a window; metabolism; stop at a missed gate, settle what was created, discard nobody; take the team's own stake; count skills spread and scenario parts completed as return. Split the cycle's pool among project sizes in advance.

**Needs.** The Value Added Mechanism, which the seed is the first line of. The Neighborhood Scorecard, which gate two reads. Speech Acts, for the promise and the gate conversations.

**State.** Paper; proposed and untested.

**Learn it from.** Design Record Section 9; the two diagrams `../diagrams/catalytic-capital-money-flows.rcn.json` and `me-lifecycle-end-to-end.rcn.json`; `04-phase-4-charter.md`, "Step five."

**Skills.**
Basic: can state the three gates to an ME and mean them.
Intermediate: can size a seed between under- and over-seeding and close one out.
Advanced: can run a Platform's pool across a cycle with Alexander's split.

## 18. Speech Acts and Conversations for Action

**Where it fits.** Every phase; load-bearing in Phases 4 and 5 and in every Platform-ME conversation.

**Problem.** Coordination fails on vague speech: an "I'll try" heard as a promise, a decline never made, a satisfaction never declared.

**Therefore.** Know the five foundational acts, assertion, declaration, request, offer, promise, and the four-phase conversation for action: request; promise, decline, counteroffer, or commit-to-commit; declare complete, cancel, or revoke; declare satisfied or dissatisfied. Name the act when naming helps. Walk the ME's promises weekly. Protect the right to decline and to revoke out loud.

**Needs.** Moods Assessment, since a promise made in resignation is not one. CfA-dSC, to record the conversations.

**State.** Partial: CfA-dSC tracks two-party conversations for action.

**Learn it from.** `docs/cfa-dsc-intro.html`; Marc's nine-state diagram (Design Record/sources/pastes/turn-154-1-dunham-conversation-for-action.svg) and his FedWiki "Action Conversation Template"; Design Record Section 7.

**Skills.**
Basic: can tell a promise from a hope and a request from a complaint.
Intermediate: can hold a conversation for action through all four phases and record it.
Advanced: can run a room's commitments on speech acts, as the hospital was run, and teach it.

## 19. Moods Assessment

**Where it fits.** Every conversation in the guide. It is the floor the other patterns stand on.

**Problem.** A convener in ambition walks into a room in resignation and hears nothing true; a room in resentment gets argued with and is lost.

**Therefore.** Hold six moods as a working set: three closing, resentment (past), anxiety (present), resignation (future); three opening, acceptance (past), curiosity (present), ambition (future). Read the mood of a person or a room as information about the neighborhood's state, and read your own first. Acknowledge resentment; name resignation and do not argue with it; invite ambition only after acceptance has settled. Name a mood aloud only when naming would help.

**Needs.** Nothing smaller.

**State.** Paper.

**Learn it from.** Design Record Section 7; Marc's five years of training with Dunham, Turkovitch, and Lucy (Section 11); `01-phase-1-understand-and-enlist.md`, "Listen for the moods."

**Skills.**
Basic: can tell resignation from acceptance in themselves.
Intermediate: can read the mood of a room and choose a move.
Advanced: can shift a room's mood from closing to opening without pretending the closing mood was wrong.

## 20. Doing the Work at Zero Distance

**Where it fits.** Phase 5, the work itself.

**Problem.** An ME drifts toward what its institutions find convenient and away from the residents it is for.

**Therefore.** Keep the residents whose scenario it is on the ME or a call away; walk the promises weekly; revoke out loud; watch the gate window; mentor only on request. When a promise reaches inside an institution, use Value Stream Mapping and A3 with that institution's staff.

**Needs.** Speech Acts, for the promises. Moods Assessment, for week six. Catalytic Seed with Gates, for the window. Value Stream Mapping and A3 Problem Solving, for the work inside an institution.

**State.** Paper.

**Learn it from.** Design Record Section 8 (execution); `05-phase-5-do-look-settle-and-go-again.md`, "Doing the work."

**Skills.**
Basic: can walk a promise list.
Intermediate: can keep an ME at zero distance under institutional pressure.
Advanced: can mentor an ME through a missed gate without taking it over.

## 21. Retrospective

**Where it fits.** Phase 5, one evening after the work reaches its chartered completion or its gate. It feeds Settlement and enriches the Scenario Repository.

**Problem.** A retrospective in resignation or in celebration learns nothing, and numbers read before residents speak replace recognition with measurement.

**Therefore.** Survey again first; lay the sketches and value networks side by side; then, in order: what happened (assertions); what residents received (they speak first, at length); what the numbers say; what was learned (declarations); what next. Write it into the scenario's existing attempts the same week.

**Needs.** The e-VSM Survey, the Value Network, the Ecosystem Sketch, and the Neighborhood Scorecard for the evidence; the Timeline to hold it in order; Moods Assessment and Speech Acts to run the evening.

**State.** Paper, with the tools it uses built.

**Learn it from.** Design Record Sections 7, 8, 10; the 2014 assessment-of-situation template (the mood matrix past / present / future) in the Medford material; `05-phase-5-…md`, "Looking."

**Skills.**
Basic: can run the five steps in order.
Intermediate: can hold the room in acceptance and keep numbers after words.
Advanced: can read the before-and-after e-VSM and sketches with a room and turn them into next promises.

## 22. Neighborhood Scorecard with Rasch

**Where it fits.** Phase 5, and gate two of the seed. It is how the neighborhood measures value on its own yardsticks.

**Problem.** Value gets measured by outsiders' frameworks, or by summed ratings that are not measures, and the neighborhood's own recognition gets replaced.

**Therefore.** Let the neighborhood write the questions that reveal what matters to it; gather thirty to two hundred responses; calibrate with Rasch to discover which questions make which yardsticks, how far each reaches, and with what precision; keep the residents' declaration above the numbers. Use the Civic Activation Measure as the precedent and, when its result would have a use, as an instrument.

**Needs.** The e-VSM Survey, for respondents and items. The Value Network, for the structure the numbers sit on. The RCN Rasch tool, for the calibration. Recognition and Settlement, which the scorecard serves and never replaces.

**State.** Partial: `tools/rcn-rasch.html` exists; the CAM pool is drafted and unfielded; Rasch on e-VSM is a committed project.

**Learn it from.** `tools/rcn-rasch.html` and `tools/schemas/rcn-rasch.md`; Design Record/sources/CAM-Item-Pool-v0.1.md; `docs/wright/`; Design Record Section 10.

**Skills.**
Basic: can help a neighborhood write revealing questions and field them.
Intermediate: can run a calibration and read the item map, the gaps, and whether the stick reaches both ends.
Advanced: can build a neighborhood scorecard dimension from scratch, compare across neighborhoods, and integrate with e-VSM.

## 23. Recognition and Settlement in Four Currencies

**Where it fits.** Phase 5, after the Retrospective. It closes the Charter and pays the Value Added Mechanism.

**Problem.** Compensation flows on numbers, or on time served, and not on what residents recognize.

**Therefore.** The residents declare, in their own words, whether they received value they recognize, and their determination is final; then the VAM pays each party's share in its currency, fiat, time, gifts, reputation, partial if recognition was partial; the seed closes out; returns go back to the pool; the Platform is paid last and only on recognition.

**Needs.** The Value Added Mechanism, for the shares. The Neighborhood Scorecard, for the evidence beneath the declaration. The SODOTO Portfolio, where the reputation lands. Speech Acts, for the declarations.

**State.** Paper.

**Learn it from.** Design Record Sections 6 (the four currencies), 8, 9, 10 (recognition as something a person does); `05-phase-5-…md`, "Settling."

**Skills.**
Basic: can run a settlement conversation with residents speaking first.
Intermediate: can pay a VAM partially in four currencies and record it.
Advanced: can settle several MEs across a cycle and close a Platform's pool.

## 24. SODOTO Portfolio

**Where it fits.** Phase 5 at settlement; read again in Phase 1 (who to invite) and Phase 4 (who bids). It counts skills spread as return on the seed.

**Problem.** Nobody's history of work is visible when an ME forms or a bid goes out, so trust is rebuilt from nothing each time.

**Therefore.** After every settlement, each promiser's portfolio gains an entry: the ME, the role, what they did, the residents' attestation, whom they taught. See One, Do One, Teach One: whoever learns must teach, and the chain is the record.

**Needs.** Recognition and Settlement, which produce the attestations.

**State.** Partial: SODOTO carries signed teaching badges; ME participation and attestations are the committed extension.

**Learn it from.** `docs/sodoto-intro.html`, `sodoto-manual.html`, `sodoto-pitch.pptx`, `sodoto-teaching-graph-intro.html`, `sodoto-teaching-graph-manual.html`, `sodoto-teaching-graph.pptx`.

**Skills.**
Basic: has a portfolio and can read a teach chain.
Intermediate: can issue badges and attestations after a settlement.
Advanced: can design the portfolio entries an ME needs and read a neighborhood's teach chains for dead ends and bottlenecks.

## 25. Scenario Repository in FedWiki

## 26. RCN Graph Tool

**Where it fits.** Every phase. It keeps the Linkage Map, the Ecosystem Sketch, the Value Network, the Value Stream Map, the Story Structure, and the diagrams of the record.

**Problem.** Drawings made on a wall are lost, and drawings made in software cannot be read by a room.

**Therefore.** Draw on the wall; keep it in the Graph Tool, and in the neighborhood's Neo4j database, with a legend as the registry of kinds; export SVG for documents and rcngraph items for FedWiki so the drawing stays editable; validate before sharing. Icons, border colors, and edge styles carry meaning; keep vocabularies small.

**Needs.** Nothing smaller in this language.

**State.** Built.

## 28. Patience, and Highlander 3.0

**Where it fits.** Phase 1, when one of the two filters fails. It is what the Founded Commons Diagnostic and the Small Groups Filter hand off to when the answer is no.

**Problem.** A convener forces what is missing to appear on the granting arm's timeline, and the neighborhood learns that this too is a program.

**Therefore.** Patience is operational, and it is not optional. It means staying in relationship without demanding readiness: know who the groups are and stay in touch; do not invite them into Phase 2; do not offer money; watch for the place, or the groups, to emerge, a garage that gets activated, a church basement the neighborhood adopts, a maker space, groups beginning to use a founder's place for their own initiatives. When it emerges, they may be ready. Say plainly and without blame what is missing. Then offer the generative alternative: Highlander 3.0, cohorts of neighborhood actionists spending weeks in places with vibrant founded commons and learning from the places and one another; and mentorship from a convener in a neighborhood that is already self-organizing.

**Needs.** Coffee Conversation, to stay in relationship.

**State.** Paper; Highlander 3.0 is an initiative in formation.

**Learn it from.** Design Record Section 4; `01-phase-1-understand-and-enlist.md`, "When one filter is present and the other is not"; Design Record/chat-memory/areas/highlander-3.md.

**Skills.**
Basic: can say "not yet, and here is what is missing" without blame.
Intermediate: can stay in relationship over months without starting.
Advanced: can host a Highlander cohort, or mentor a nascent leader in another neighborhood.

## 29. Seed and Support, Do Not Run

**Where it fits.** Phases 4 and 5 and the whole arc. It is the pattern for the convener's own role as a Platform actor.

**Problem.** The Platform actor who helped an ME form starts running it, and the neighborhood has a program again.

**Therefore.** Be the EMC organizer: help the ME form, mentor on request, bring contacts and contract help, bring the seed against gates, take a VAM share paid last, and hold no authority over the work. Your compensation and reputation ride on what the ME's residents recognize. From the second cycle on, do less; hand over the phase that still depends on you.

**Needs.** Catalytic Seed with Gates, Speech Acts, Moods Assessment.

**State.** Paper.

**Learn it from.** Design Record Sections 2 (Principle Two), 6, 8 (how the Platform persists); `01-phase-1-understand-and-enlist.md`, "Know your own Platform relationship."

**Skills.**
Basic: can say what is in it for them honestly when asked.
Intermediate: can watch an ME struggle without taking it over.
Advanced: can graduate a neighborhood and move on.

## 30. Value Stream Mapping

**Where it fits.** Phase 4 (agreeing how a process change inside an institution will be worked) and Phase 5 (doing it). It names the storm bursts that A3 Problem Solving takes on.

**Problem.** Everyone knows the process and nobody has watched it; ask five people how a referral moves and you get five sincere, incomplete answers.

**Therefore.** With the institution's own staff, map the process as it actually runs, lanes for who does what, the observed times, the storm bursts where the flow breaks, in the Graph Tool's value-stream mode; write every burst down, even the ones you will not work on now; hand the worst one to an A3. This is lean's value stream, a tool for inside an institution, and it is a different thing from the RenDanHeYi value chain and from Allee's value network; Design Record Section 8 keeps the three apart.

**Needs.** The RCN Graph Tool. Speech Acts, since the institution's lent person leads it.

**State.** Built: the value-stream mode of the Graph Tool, with lanes, storm bursts, the computed sawtooth, and CSV / Markdown / A3 export.

**Learn it from.** `tools/rcn-lean-healthcare-intro.pptx`; `tools/graph-tool-manual.html` (value-stream mode); Medford's Phase 4, which trained project staff in it through Lean Healthcare West.

**Skills.**
Basic: can walk a process and draw its lanes and times.
Intermediate: can map a value stream in the tool with an institution's staff and name the bursts.
Advanced: can run the loop, map, burst, A3, measure, across several processes and teach it.

## 31. A3 Problem Solving

**Where it fits.** Phase 5, for one burst at a time; its measure feeds the Neighborhood Scorecard's run charts and gate two of the seed for within-institution work.

**Problem.** Most A3 templates are project-management forms with Japanese labels: the problem never gets stated as a gap, the root cause becomes a person, and the improvement is proved with two numbers.

**Therefore.** One page, in pencil, by the person who does the work, about one problem seen once, in Cindy Jimmerson's healthcare form: the problem as the gap between what is supposed to happen and what actually happens; the root cause as a broken process, never a person, checked by the five whys; one measure with a baseline, a target, and a date; carried to where the work happens, argued over, marked up. Chart the measure over time in the SPC tool and look for a signal, not a before-and-after.

**Needs.** Value Stream Mapping, for the burst. The RCN SPC tool, for the measure.

**State.** Built: `tools/a3.html` enforces the three rules, prints to 11×17, and takes the value stream's bursts and times in one click.

**Learn it from.** `tools/a3-intro.html`, `tools/a3-manual.html`, `tools/rcn-lean-healthcare-intro.pptx`; `tools/rcn-spc.html` with its intro and manual, for the measure; Jimmerson, *A3 Problem Solving for Healthcare* (2007).

**Skills.**
Basic: can write a problem as a gap with a standard and an observation.
Intermediate: can complete an A3 with a process root cause and a measure, and chart it.
Advanced: can coach an institution's staff through an A3 without writing it for them, and read the run chart honestly.
