### Section 10 — Value: Created by Residents for Residents and Their Neighbors, Recognized by Them, Measured by Their Instruments

#### Why value gets its own section

Every section of the design record has referred to value. Section 2 named the fourth dependency-elimination principle in terms of value created by residents for residents and their neighbors. Section 5 anchored the customer scenario in what would constitute value for the residents whose lives the scenario touches. Section 6 tied Industry Platform compensation to residents' recognition of value received. Section 8 grounded the Solution ME's Leading Targets in the value the ecosystem community was chartered to produce. Section 9 conditioned catalytic capital's recovery on value that residents recognize.

If value in all these usages meant the ordinary thing — outcomes measured against outsider-authored frameworks, benefits assessed by evaluators, impacts scored against standard metrics — the design would collapse into the philanthropic-industrial pattern the four principles from Section 2 exist to prevent. The design's coherence requires that value mean something specific and different, and that the operational mechanisms for producing, recognizing, and measuring value be developed with the same care as the other structural components.

That is what this section does. Value is defined precisely, the mechanisms for its production and recognition are specified, the instrumentation for its measurement is described, and the anti-capture properties that keep the definition from drifting back toward outsider-authored standards are made explicit.

#### The definition, unpacked

The full form of the definition arrived through the design conversation in Marc's own words: value created by residents for residents and their neighbors, with neighbors defined by the value creators themselves. Each element does work worth naming.

**Created by residents.** The value's origin is in the residents' own agency, not in an external provider's contribution to residents. This is a strong claim. It rules out framings in which an outside organization creates value that is then delivered to residents; the residents in that framing are recipients, not creators. In the design's framing, residents are the primary actors — the neighborhood's small groups, the founded commons' users, the ME participants, the neighbors of neighbors — and the value comes into being through what they do. External contributions (Industry Platform capital, Platform actor mentorship, kit facilitation, substrate infrastructure) support the residents' creation of value; they do not substitute for it.

**For residents and their neighbors.** The value's beneficiaries are the residents themselves and the people the residents count as their neighbors. This closes the loop between value creation and value reception within the neighborhood's own ecology, rather than routing benefits outward to shareholders, investors, funders, or outside evaluators. When an ME successfully addresses a customer scenario, the value produced flows to residents living inside that scenario and to the people they count as connected to their situation.

**With neighbors defined by the value creators themselves.** This clause is the most important. It refuses the standard move of having outsiders define who counts as legitimate beneficiary — service-area boundaries, eligibility criteria, target populations, protected classes. The residents creating value define, in their own terms, who counts as their neighbor for the purposes of this scenario and this value. A refugee family may count their extended kin, their cultural community, the neighbors on their block, and their child's classmates as neighbors; the definition emerges from them, not from an external framework. This clause is what makes the value definition genuinely neighborhood-authored rather than merely neighborhood-delivered.

**Recognized by them.** Value that residents do not recognize is not value under this definition, regardless of what outside assessors may say about it. This is a stringent test. A well-designed intervention that produces measurable improvements in indicators the residents don't care about, or improvements the residents don't experience as improvements, or improvements to which the residents attribute other causes, has not produced recognized value. The recognition is done by the residents themselves, in their own terms, using their own instruments.

**Measured by their instruments.** The instruments through which value is seen are the neighborhood's own instruments. The balanced scorecard is neighborhood-constructed and neighborhood-maintained. The surveys are neighborhood-authored. The retrospectives happen in the neighborhood's own founded commons, in the neighborhood's own language, on the neighborhood's own timeline. External instruments (foundation evaluation frameworks, state agency outcome metrics, academic assessment methodologies) may exist and may inform the neighborhood's instrument-construction if the neighborhood chooses, but they do not substitute for the neighborhood's own measurement.

Together these five elements define value in a form that structurally resists the capture patterns that have collapsed most attempts at neighborhood-scale civic work over the past century. Each element closes a specific door.

#### The neighborhood-authored balanced scorecard

The balanced scorecard is the operational form of the neighborhood's value-measurement instrument. It sits at the center of the Industry Platform's compensation mechanism, the ME retrospective cycles, and the accumulation of scenario-enrichment data over time.

"Balanced" here means what it meant in Kaplan and Norton's original 1990s formulation — measurement across multiple dimensions rather than reduction to a single dimension. But the dimensions themselves are neighborhood-authored rather than inherited from Kaplan and Norton's four (financial, customer, internal process, learning and growth). What matters is that the neighborhood constructs a scorecard that captures what value means to that neighborhood in enough distinct dimensions that no single dimension can be gamed against the others.

Section 6 named some starting candidates for dimensions a neighborhood might include: resident well-being outcomes tied to specific ME work; between-institution linkage improvements that survived beyond seed capital; neighborhood capacity growth beyond the ME's own participants; portfolio depth of individuals involved; founded-commons health; ME viability without further seed; replicability of patterns created; cultural fit and neighborhood legitimacy. These are candidates, not a template. Each neighborhood produces its own set, informed by what has come up in the neighborhood's Phase 2 meetings, what residents have named as important, and what the ME's specific scenario requires attention to.

The Platform's role in scorecard construction is skilled but bounded. The Platform actor helps the neighborhood construct a scorecard that will show its own value creation clearly — that requires knowledge of how scorecards can be well or badly constructed, which dimensions tend to work and which tend to fail, how to phrase items so they capture what they intend to capture. But the Platform actor does not hand the neighborhood a template. The scorecard emerges from the neighborhood's own naming of what matters, with the Platform actor's help in making sure the naming produces something that can actually function as an instrument.

Because the Platform's own compensation depends on the neighborhood's scorecard (as described in Section 6), the Platform has strong incentive to help construct a scorecard that is real rather than one that flatters the Platform's contribution. Two-way discipline is built into the instrument itself. A Platform actor who tries to soften the scorecard to make their own compensation easier will find that the softened scorecard fails to reflect what residents actually recognize, and the compensation flows dry up regardless of how favorable the softened metrics appear.

The scorecard is not static. It develops as the neighborhood's Phase 2 conversations return, as MEs surface new understandings of what the scenarios they address require, as residents' own experience of what matters shifts. The Platform maintains the scorecard as a living instrument, revised on the neighborhood's timeline rather than on any external review cycle.

#### Rasch measurement as the methodological substrate

The methodological substrate that allows neighborhood-authored scorecards to function as real measurement instruments is Rasch analysis, from the same measurement tradition Marc worked in with Benjamin Wright at the University of Chicago and applied through the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation's Pursuing Perfection program. Rasch is not the only possible substrate, but it is uniquely well-suited to what neighborhood scorecards need to do.

Rasch measurement holds a specific property: it calibrates items to an underlying latent construct along a shared dimension, while allowing the specific items measuring that construct to vary across contexts. This is exactly what neighborhood-authored scorecards need. The dimensions of value that show up across neighborhoods can be comparable enough to be usable across the cluster the Platform serves, while the specific items measuring those dimensions in any given neighborhood can be authored in that neighborhood's own language, culture, and priorities.

Practical implications for the design:

A dimension like "residents' recognition of value received from ME work" is a latent construct that can be measured through many possible item sets. A neighborhood scorecard might include items like "I have noticed changes in how [scenario area] works for my family in the past six months" or "People in my building talk about how this is different now" or "My kids' school has changed something about how they handle [specific situation]." Different neighborhoods will phrase these items differently; the same underlying construct is being measured; comparisons across neighborhoods are meaningful even where the specific items differ.

A dimension like "between-institution linkage improvement that survives beyond seed capital" is another latent construct with many possible item sets. Different neighborhoods will find different specific items that reveal this construct; Rasch calibration lets those different items sit along the same underlying dimension.

The CAM work in RCN's substrate — Civic Activation Measure, the 40-item Rasch-modeled instrument for civic engagement — is the methodological precedent. The scorecard's dimensions can be developed using the same architecture, with Winsteps or similar Rasch analysis software providing the calibration infrastructure. The CAM's open-governance clause (all items, calibration data, and control files published in commons) is a model for how scorecard dimensions should be developed and maintained across neighborhoods.

Rasch is not required for every neighborhood scorecard. Simpler measurement approaches (frequency counts, Likert-scale summaries, qualitative narratives) can serve where they fit. What Rasch provides is the option of psychometrically real measurement when the neighborhood or the Platform needs to be confident that dimensions measure what they claim to measure, and when cross-neighborhood comparison is required for Platform-scale learning.

#### The e-VSM survey layer

The e-VSM survey family — Survey, Diagram, Dialogue — provides the multi-perspective read that scorecards and retrospectives need. e-VSM's operational architecture is developed in the RCN substrate work; here the design record names its role in the value-measurement layer.

At Pre-Phase 1, an e-VSM survey of the neighborhood or the potentially-emerging neighborhood surfaces whether the substrate is present — small groups working, founded commons, cross-sphere relationships — through multi-perspective structured input rather than through the convener's own judgment. The Generic Neighborhood Development Center survey (11 spheres, 66 directed edges, Markov Blanket layer mapping) is the specific instrument this design record contemplates using.

At Phase 2 preparation, participants surveyed before the linkage-mapping meeting bring multiple perspectives to the room before the loudest voice sets the frame. The survey's Claude API synthesis produces integrated reads of what respondents are saying, with world-set filters (Exclusive/Inclusive) letting the neighborhood examine its own diversity of perspective rather than collapsing to a consensus view.

At scenario validation, once a scenario has been articulated, an e-VSM survey of the broader neighborhood assesses whether the scenario is real, whose experience it reflects, and what it misses. Scenarios that survive validation get Solution ME formation; scenarios that do not get revised or archived with what was learned.

At Phase 5 retrospective, e-VSM surveys how the ME's work has landed across the neighborhood's spheres. The scorecard's dimensions inform survey item construction; the survey provides the multi-perspective evidence that supports (or fails to support) the scorecard's assessment.

Claude's operational role across these uses is the interpretation and integration Marc's e-VSM design contemplates. Claude reads the survey evidence and surfaces suggestions; the convener, the Platform actors, and the neighborhood participants use those interpretations as inputs to their own judgment. Claude does not substitute for the neighborhood's own recognition; Claude provides synthesis that makes the neighborhood's own recognition more visible than raw survey data would be.

#### Recognition as an act, not a metric

Recognition of value is a specific human act, not a metric that instruments produce automatically. This distinction matters because instruments can produce numbers that look like recognition without any actual recognition having happened.

A resident recognizes value when they experience the value in their own life or in the life of someone they count as a neighbor, understand what happened as connected to specific work (an ME's activity, a scenario being addressed), and can articulate the connection in their own terms. All three components matter. Experience without articulation is data. Articulation without experience is testimony. Connection to specific work distinguishes recognized value from generic goodwill.

Recognition happens in conversation, in survey response, in retrospective participation, in whether people show up again when invited. It also happens in absence — value that produced no recognition (however measured) has not been recognized, whatever the measurement instruments suggest.

The design's commitment is to preserve recognition as human act, supported by instruments but not replaced by them. The scorecard's numbers, the e-VSM survey's syntheses, and the retrospective's narratives are all inputs to a determination that residents did or did not recognize value from this specific ME's work. The determination itself is made by the neighborhood, not by the instruments. Platform compensation flows according to the determination, not according to the instruments alone.

#### The anti-capture properties

The value definition's structural anti-capture properties are what make it different from ordinary outcome-based funding, and they are worth naming explicitly.

**Outsiders cannot originate the scorecard.** WWHA's board, funders, state agencies, evaluators, and academic partners can contribute methodological help, suggest possible dimensions, and offer to fund neighborhoods whose scorecards they find credible. They cannot substitute their scorecard for the neighborhood's judgment of what has been received. This is a governance constraint on WWHA itself and belongs in WWHA's charter as a founding limit.

**Grantmakers who dislike a neighborhood's scorecard have one legitimate response: decline to fund.** They do not get to negotiate the scorecard toward their own preferences as a condition of funding. That negotiation would be the capture pattern the design exists to prevent, and the ability to walk away is what protects the neighborhood's authorship.

**Neighborhoods with scorecards that funders reject may still function.** Diversified funding sources means no single funder's rejection can starve a neighborhood whose scorecard the funder disagrees with. The Platform's aggregate model must maintain enough diversity of capital sources that any single source's departure is survivable.

**Scorecard revisions cannot be triggered by funder pressure.** The neighborhood revises its scorecard on its own timeline, informed by what the neighborhood learns from its own experience. Funder feedback may be one input the neighborhood considers, but the decision to revise is the neighborhood's alone. Revisions triggered by funder pressure violate the definition.

**Recognition cannot be manufactured through instrument design.** Instruments that produce apparent-recognition without corresponding lived experience are failures of instrument design, not evidence of recognition. The design record's Rasch commitment includes commitment to psychometric honesty — items must actually measure what they claim to measure, not merely produce numbers that appear to.

**The neighborhood's "no" is respected.** A neighborhood that determines an ME's work produced no recognized value has produced a legitimate assessment, even where evidence-of-outputs and process-of-work suggest the ME did its job. The design does not allow the ME to appeal the determination to any higher authority — the residents' determination is final within the value definition's own terms. This is what distinguishes recognition from evaluation.

#### What Section 10 commits the design to

Value throughout the design record means what this section has defined: value created by residents for residents and their neighbors, with neighbors defined by the value creators themselves, recognized by the residents and measured by their own instruments. No looser or more inclusive definition is available for use anywhere in the design.

The neighborhood-authored balanced scorecard is the operational form of the value-measurement instrument. Each neighborhood constructs and maintains its own; dimensions are candidates rather than templates; the Platform helps with scorecard construction but does not substitute its own judgment for the neighborhood's.

Rasch measurement is the methodological substrate that allows scorecards to function as real instruments while remaining neighborhood-authored. The CAM work is the precedent. Rasch is not required for every scorecard but is available when the neighborhood or Platform needs psychometrically real measurement or cross-neighborhood comparison.

The e-VSM survey layer provides multi-perspective input at multiple phases — Pre-Phase 1 diagnostic, Phase 2 preparation, scenario validation, Phase 5 retrospective. Claude's API-driven synthesis of survey results makes multi-perspective evidence usable without substituting for human recognition.

Recognition is a specific human act supported by instruments rather than replaced by them. Instruments provide input; the neighborhood's determination is what constitutes recognition; Platform compensation flows according to the determination.

The value definition's structural anti-capture properties (outsiders cannot originate the scorecard, grantmakers can only decline to fund, revisions cannot be triggered by funder pressure, the neighborhood's "no" is respected) are the specific mechanisms that keep the value definition from drifting back toward outsider-authored standards.

Section 11 addresses the prior-work substrate that this design record rests on and carries forward — CMG's 2016 methodology, the Medford and Spokane implementations, the thirty-year arc of Marc's related work, and the specific tools and frameworks (Ackoff's Idealized Design, Beer's VSM, Ostrom's polycentricity, Vester's biocybernetics, Meadows' leverage points, McKnight's associational-versus-service distinction, Flores' speech acts, Rasch measurement) that constitute the intellectual foundation.

WWHA's board, funders, state agencies, evaluators, and academic partners can contribute methodological help, suggest possible dimensions, and offer to fund neighborhoods whose scorecards they find credible. They cannot substitute their scorecard for the neighborhood's judgment of what has been received. This is a governance constraint on WWHA itself and belongs in WWHA's charter as a founding limit.

Grantmakers who dislike a neighborhood's scorecard have one legitimate response: decline to fund. They do not get to negotiate the scorecard toward their own preferences as a condition of funding. That negotiation would be the capture pattern the design exists to prevent, and the ability to walk away is what protects the neighborhood's authorship.

Neighborhoods with scorecards that funders reject may still function. Diversified funding sources means no single funder's rejection can starve a neighborhood whose scorecard the funder disagrees with. The Platform's aggregate model must maintain enough diversity of capital sources that any single source's departure is survivable.

Scorecard revisions cannot be triggered by funder pressure. The neighborhood revises its scorecard on its own timeline, informed by what the neighborhood learns from its own experience. Funder feedback may be one input the neighborhood considers, but the decision to revise is the neighborhood's alone. Revisions triggered by funder pressure violate the definition.

Recognition cannot be manufactured through instrument design. Instruments that produce apparent-recognition without corresponding lived experience are failures of instrument design, not evidence of recognition. The design record's Rasch commitment includes commitment to psychometric honesty — items must actually measure what they claim to measure, not merely produce numbers that appear to.

The neighborhood's "no" is respected. A neighborhood that determines an ME's work produced no recognized value has produced a legitimate assessment, even where evidence-of-outputs and process-of-work suggest the ME did its job. The design does not allow the ME to appeal the determination to any higher authority — the residents' determination is final within the value definition's own terms. This is what distinguishes recognition from evaluation.

### Section 10 — Value: Created by Residents for Residents and Their Neighbors, Recognized by Them, Measured by Their Own Measures

#### Why value gets its own section

Every section of the design record has referred to value. Section 2 named the fourth dependency-elimination principle in terms of value created by residents for residents and their neighbors. Section 5 anchored the customer scenario in what would constitute value for the residents whose lives the scenario touches. Section 6 tied Industry Platform compensation to residents' recognition of value received. Section 8 grounded the Solution ME's Leading Targets in the value the ecosystem community was chartered to produce. Section 9 conditioned catalytic capital's gates and recovery on value that residents recognize.

If value in all these usages meant the ordinary thing, outcomes measured against outsider-authored frameworks, benefits assessed by evaluators, impacts scored against standard metrics, the design would collapse into the philanthropic-industrial pattern the four principles from Section 2 exist to prevent. The design's coherence requires that value mean something specific and different, and that the operational mechanisms for producing, recognizing, and measuring value be developed with the same care as the other structural components.

That is what this section does. Value is defined precisely; the mechanisms for its production and recognition are specified; the measures are described, with three tools doing distinct jobs; and the anti-capture properties that keep the definition from drifting back toward outsider-authored standards are made explicit.

The three tools, named here so the reader can hold them apart. A value network drawing, in Verna Allee's notation on the RCN Graph Tool, shows where value moves between roles and where a return that ought to exist does not; it is how the neighborhood sees the *structure* of value. The balanced scorecard, built from local surveys and calibrated with Rasch measurement where the neighborhood has enough respondents, is how the neighborhood *measures* value on its own yardsticks. And the e-VSM survey, before and after ME work, is how the neighborhood sees whether its own connective tissue changed. The Timeline tool has a smaller part, holding the before and after in time so the story of a scenario can be read in order.

#### The definition, unpacked

The full form of the definition arrived through the design conversation in Marc's own words: value created by residents for residents and their neighbors, with neighbors defined by the value creators themselves. Each element does work worth naming.

**Created by residents.** The value's origin is in the residents' own agency. This is a strong claim. It rules out framings in which an outside organization creates value that is then delivered to residents; in that framing the residents are recipients. In the design's framing, residents are the primary actors, the neighborhood's small groups, the founded commons' users, the ME participants, the neighbors of neighbors, and the value comes into being through what they do. External contributions, Industry Platform capital, Platform actor mentorship, the field guide, the RCN foundational tools, support the residents' creation of value; they do not substitute for it.

**For residents and their neighbors.** The value's beneficiaries are the residents themselves and the people the residents count as their neighbors. This closes the loop between value creation and value reception within the neighborhood's own ecology, rather than routing benefits outward to shareholders, investors, funders, or outside evaluators. When an ME successfully addresses a customer scenario, the value produced flows to residents living inside that scenario and to the people they count as connected to their situation.

**With neighbors defined by the value creators themselves.** This clause is the most important. It refuses the standard move of having outsiders define who counts as a legitimate beneficiary: service-area boundaries, eligibility criteria, target populations, protected classes. The residents creating value define, in their own terms, who counts as their neighbor for the purposes of this scenario and this value. A refugee family may count their extended kin, their cultural community, the neighbors on their block, and their child's classmates as neighbors; the definition emerges from them, not from an external framework. This clause is what makes the value definition neighborhood-authored rather than merely neighborhood-delivered.

**Recognized by them.** Value that residents do not recognize is not value under this definition, whatever outside assessors say about it. This is a stringent test. A well-designed intervention that produces measurable improvements in indicators the residents do not care about, or improvements the residents do not experience as improvements, or improvements the residents attribute to other causes, has not produced recognized value. The recognition is done by the residents themselves, in their own terms, on their own measures.

**Measured by their own measures.** The measures through which value is seen are the neighborhood's own. The balanced scorecard is neighborhood-constructed and neighborhood-maintained. The surveys are neighborhood-authored. The retrospectives happen in the neighborhood's own founded commons, in the neighborhood's own language, on the neighborhood's own timeline. External measures, foundation evaluation frameworks, state agency outcome metrics, academic assessment methodologies, may exist and may inform the neighborhood's own measure-building if the neighborhood chooses, but they do not substitute for the neighborhood's measurement.

Together these five elements define value in a form that structurally resists the capture patterns that have collapsed most attempts at neighborhood-scale civic work over the past century. Each element closes a specific door.

#### Seeing the structure of value: the value network

Before anything is measured, the neighborhood can see where value moves. Section 8 describes the notation and Section 9 draws one, and this section names their use in value work generally. A value network drawing puts each role on the page, draws every deliverable that moves between roles as a labeled arrow, tangible solid and intangible dashed, and draws in red any return that ought to exist and does not. Reciprocity, dead ends, bottlenecks, and the divergence of the money circuit from the service circuit are all read off the drawing without computing anything.

For value in this section's sense, the drawing answers a prior question the scorecard cannot: is the value the ME claims to create actually reaching the people it is for, and what comes back to the people who create it? A CHW network that delivers rides, forms, follow-up, trust, and translation to households and receives nothing back from the clinic that benefits is a burnout pattern, visible in the drawing before any survey is fielded. The scorecard then measures what the drawing shows. The design commits to drawing the value network of every ME at chartering and again at retrospective, so that the second drawing can be laid over the first.

#### The neighborhood-authored balanced scorecard

The balanced scorecard is the operational form of the neighborhood's value measure. It sits at the center of the Industry Platform's compensation mechanism, Section 9's gates, the ME retrospective cycles, and the accumulation of scenario-enrichment data over time.

"Balanced" here means what it meant in Kaplan and Norton's original 1990s formulation: measurement across several dimensions rather than reduction to a single one. But the dimensions are neighborhood-authored rather than inherited from Kaplan and Norton's four (financial, customer, internal process, learning and growth). What matters is that the neighborhood constructs a scorecard that captures what value means to that neighborhood in enough distinct dimensions that no single dimension can be gamed against the others.

Which dimensions those are is a question Rasch measurement can answer from data rather than from anyone's opinion, and this is Marc's central point about the method. Rasch identifies dimensions from survey responses. It never lets length be mixed with weight: if a set of items is really measuring two different things, the analysis says so, and says which items belong to which. It then reports how well the items cover each dimension from end to end, in what increments, and with what precision or fuzziness at each point. So the scorecard's dimensions are discovered, not declared. The neighborhood writes the questions it thinks reveal what matters; the data say which yardsticks those questions actually make.

Section 6 named some starting candidates for dimensions a neighborhood might find: resident well-being outcomes tied to specific ME work; between-institution linkage improvements that survived beyond seed capital; neighborhood capacity growth beyond the ME's own participants; portfolio depth of the individuals involved; founded-commons health; ME viability without further seed; replicability of patterns created; cultural fit and neighborhood legitimacy. These are candidates, not a template, and they are candidates for questions, not for dimensions; the dimensions are what survive calibration. Each neighborhood produces its own set, informed by what has come up in its Phase 2 meetings, what residents have named as important, and what the ME's specific scenario requires attention to.

The Platform's role in scorecard construction is skilled but bounded. The Platform actor helps the neighborhood construct a scorecard that will show the neighborhood's value creation clearly; that requires knowing how scorecards can be well or badly constructed, which questions tend to work and which tend to fail, how to phrase an item so that it captures what it intends to. But the Platform actor does not hand the neighborhood a template. The scorecard emerges from the neighborhood's own naming of what matters, with the Platform actor's help in making sure the naming produces something that can actually function as a measure.

The scorecard is not static. It develops as the neighborhood's Phase 2 conversations return, as MEs surface new understandings of what the scenarios they address require, as residents' own experience of what matters shifts. The Platform maintains the scorecard as a living measure, revised on the neighborhood's timeline rather than on any external review cycle.

#### Rasch measurement as the method underneath

The method that lets neighborhood-authored scorecards function as real measures is Rasch analysis, from the measurement tradition Marc learned directly from Benjamin Wright at the University of Chicago and applied through the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation's Pursuing Perfection program, and from thirty years alongside Bill Mahoney, who did the Rasch work behind the Patient Activation Measure and whom Marc hired into PeaceHealth. Rasch is unusually well suited to what neighborhood scorecards need to do.

Rasch measurement has a specific property: it calibrates items to an underlying latent construct along a shared dimension, while allowing the specific items that measure the construct to vary across contexts. Marc asked for that sentence in practical terms with an example, and here it is.

Suppose the dimension is "residents' recognition of value received from ME work." In Superior the questions that reveal it might be "I have noticed changes in how the garden project works for my family this year" and "People on my street talk about how the fire-prevention work is different now." In Lansing they might be "My kids' school has changed something about how they handle new families" and "I know someone who started a business this year because of the help at The Fledge." The wording, the referents, and the language are all different. What Rasch does is put every one of those items on one yardstick, in logits, by how hard each is to endorse, and put every respondent on the same yardstick by what they endorse. Once that is done, a Superior resident who sits at 1.2 logits and a Lansing resident who sits at 1.2 logits have the same amount of recognized value, even though they answered different questions. That is the sense in which items can vary while the measure stays comparable, and it is what makes cross-neighborhood learning at the Platform possible without imposing one questionnaire on everyone. The same holds for a dimension like "between-institution linkage improvement that survives beyond seed capital": different neighborhoods find different specific items that reveal it, and the calibration lets those items sit along one dimension.

The method Marc describes for building such a measure is cheap and it is the one the design commits to. People with deep domain knowledge, which in a neighborhood is the residents and the conveners, write potentially revealing questions. Thirty to two hundred people answer them. The responses go into Winsteps, which is the honest broker: it says whether a variable exists at all, which items cohere into which yardsticks, where the marks are dense and where the gaps gape, and whether the stick reaches both ends. The last question is the one commercial survey vendors dodge. A yardstick that stops short of the hard end guarantees that no client ever measures short, and that is flattery calibrated to look like science. RCN's measures must reach the hard end, which is why the Civic Activation Measure's item pool runs up to "I take responsibility for outcomes of decisions I shared in, even ones I opposed," an item almost nobody can honestly endorse.

Marc asked whether Likert scales have any place when Rasch is available, and said he would never use them. The design agrees, with one clarification about words. A four-category response format, strongly disagree to strongly agree with no neutral midpoint, is the raw material Rasch calibrates; the Andrich rating-scale model is built for exactly that data, and the CAM uses it. What the design rejects is what is usually meant by "Likert scale": adding up the category numbers as if they were measures. A sum of ordinal categories is not a measure, has no known units, and cannot be compared across items or neighborhoods. Rasch turns the same responses into measures with units and known precision. So the questionnaire looks the same on the page; what happens to the answers is different, and only one of the two yields a yardstick.

Simpler summaries, frequency counts, narratives, still serve where they fit, which is for things that are not measures: how many people came, what someone said. Wherever the neighborhood or the Platform needs to be confident that a dimension measures what it claims, or needs cross-neighborhood comparison for Platform-scale learning, Rasch is the method.

##### The Civic Activation Measure

The CAM is the design's methodological precedent and the first instrument built on this method. It has not been fielded; the item pool exists at v0.1, and it can be fielded whenever it is really needed and the results have a real use. The construct, as drafted: civic activation is the belief, knowledge, confidence, and practiced capacity a person needs to participate in decisions affecting their neighborhood, up to and including sustaining working relationships through disagreement, defeat, and tedium. The full pool with its fielding protocol and its governance clause is in the design record's sources as CAM-Item-Pool-v0.1.md, and the forty core items are these.

Believing it matters: Decisions made in my neighborhood affect my daily life. What happens in my neighborhood is partly my responsibility. My participation in neighborhood decisions can make a difference. Ordinary residents can change how things are done here. When something in my neighborhood bothers me, I assume something can be done about it. I know the names of several of my neighbors. I pay attention to what is happening in my neighborhood.

Knowing and being confident: I know where to find out what is being decided in my neighborhood. I know who makes decisions about my street and neighborhood. I know how to contact the people who represent my neighborhood. I know how to find the agenda for a public meeting that affects my neighborhood. Taking part: I ask my neighbors for help when I need it. I offer help to neighbors without being asked. I talk with neighbors about issues that affect us. I attend meetings where neighborhood decisions are discussed. I speak up in meetings when I have something to say. I contact officials about neighborhood issues when they arise. I volunteer time to groups working in my neighborhood. I help organize neighborhood activities or gatherings. When I disagree with a proposal, I say so in the meeting rather than only complaining afterward. I bring neighbors who are not yet involved into neighborhood activities. Staying woven through conflict, the hard end: I keep participating even when meetings are slow or boring. I seek out neighbors who disagree with me to understand their reasons. I can state the strongest version of a position I disagree with. I stay in the room when a discussion turns against me. I can work productively with a neighbor I personally dislike. After a proposal of mine is voted down, I keep working with the people who voted against it. I have publicly changed my position when someone persuaded me. I help carry out group decisions I argued against. When a group decision goes badly, I help fix it rather than saying "I told you so." I help groups turn a conflict into a proposal better than either side started with. I keep showing up after the group has disappointed me. I take responsibility for outcomes of decisions I shared in, even ones I opposed.

The stratum labels are scaffolding; Winsteps hands back the real ladder, and where its order disagrees with the drafted one is exactly where something is learned. The governance clause is non-negotiable: item bank, calibration data, control files, and scoring tables are published openly and versioned in the RCN commons, with no exclusive license, ever. That clause is a model for every scorecard dimension developed across neighborhoods.

##### Using e-VSM to validate a scenario

Marc found the idea of validating a scenario with an e-VSM survey interesting and asked how it would work, in detail. Here is how, with the school-refugee-transition scenario from Section 8 as the case.

A scenario makes claims. The interlocking-needs version claims that a particular need exists, for particular people, and that it is compound: that it lives across several systems at once. The school-refugee scenario claims that a refugee student's first year goes badly because the school, the family, the resettlement agency, the clinic, and the cultural community do not coordinate, and that the family's first instinct when something goes wrong is not to contact the school. Those are assertions about the neighborhood's connective tissue, and e-VSM is a survey of exactly that.

Validation means fielding an e-VSM survey to the people the scenario is about and the people it names as the ecosystem, and seeing whether the survey says what the scenario says. The survey is the standard eleven-sphere instrument, with its vernacular labels set for this neighborhood, plus a handful of scenario-specific items written for this scenario, for instance a relationship item, "The school and the resettlement agency share what they know about a new family," and a sphere item, "New families know where to turn when something goes wrong at school." Respondents self-select their worlds: refugee parent, teacher, school nurse, caseworker, CHW, community leader, longtime resident.

Four readings then decide whether the scenario survives. First, does the need appear? If refugee parents mark the coordination relationships Disagree and write evidence that matches the scenario's story, the interlocking needs are real. If they mark them Agree, the scenario is describing a problem the people it is about do not have, and it goes back for revision or is archived with that learning. Second, is it compound? If the Disagree marks cluster on relationships between systems, school-to-agency, clinic-to-family, rather than inside one sphere, the scenario is correctly a between-institution scenario. If they cluster inside one sphere, it is one institution's problem, and an ME is the wrong form. Third, whose experience does it reflect? The aggregator's world-set comparison shows whether parents and teachers see the same broken relationships or different ones. Where they diverge, the scenario is reflecting one world's experience and missing another's, and the Experience-ME goes back to zero-distance work with the world it missed. Fourth, what does it miss? Respondents' free-text evidence will name relationships and spheres the scenario did not, and Claude's synthesis surfaces them. A scenario that names the school and the agency but whose respondents keep writing about the landlord has a missing role.

A scenario survives validation when the first two readings come back yes and the third and fourth have been absorbed into a revised scenario. Only then does it invite a Solution ME.

Marc suggested that proving this is worth significant effort, and that generating a body of plausible data and working through the idea would be the way to do it. The design commits to that experiment as a Claude Code exercise: sixty synthetic respondents across five worlds for the school-refugee scenario, with deliberately planted agreements and divergences, run through the actual aggregator and its synthesis, to see whether the four readings can be made from the tool's output as it stands or need the survey to change. Whatever the experiment teaches feeds the Rasch-and-e-VSM project above, since a validated scenario is also a baseline measure.

##### A callout on Markov blankets and free energy

Marc asked for an explanation of these somewhere, since Section 3 uses them for e-VSM's three layers and this section uses e-VSM to measure. In plain terms: any system that keeps itself going in a changing world has a boundary through which it senses the world and acts on it, and an inside that maintains itself behind that boundary. Karl Friston calls the boundary a Markov blanket, sensory states coming in and active states going out, and the inside the internal states. The free energy principle says such a system survives by keeping the mismatch small between what its internal model expects and what its senses deliver, either by changing the model or by acting on the world. A neighborhood is such a system. e-VSM's Sensory and Interface layer is its blanket, awareness within, awareness beyond, resources crossing the boundary; the Internal Core and Alignment layers are its inside. S4, in Section 3's terms, is where the model is kept and revised. A before-and-after e-VSM survey measures whether an ME changed how well the neighborhood senses, acts, and keeps itself coherent, which is a more fundamental question than whether the ME hit its targets.

Claude's operational role across all these uses is interpretation and integration. Claude reads the survey evidence and surfaces suggestions; the convener, the Platform actors, and the neighborhood participants use those interpretations as inputs to their own judgment. Claude does not substitute for the neighborhood's own recognition; it makes that recognition more visible than raw survey data would.

#### Recognition is something a person does

Marc asked for this subsection to be rewritten so that "recognition" is clear, with examples. Here it is.

A measure produces a number. Recognition is something a resident does. The two are related, and the design keeps them apart because a number can appear without anyone having recognized anything.

A resident recognizes value when three things are true together. They have experienced the value in their own life or in the life of someone they count as a neighbor. They understand what happened as connected to specific work, an ME's activity, a scenario being addressed. And they can say so in their own words. All three matter, and each can be present without the others.

Take the refugee family from Section 8. If the mother says, "This year when my son had trouble at school, the CHW called me the same day, and the school waited for us instead of deciding without us," that is recognition: experienced, connected to the ME's work, articulated. If the school's data show attendance went up and the family says nothing, that is a number; it may or may not turn out to be recognized value, and nobody yet knows. If a neighbor who never met the family says the neighborhood feels friendlier this year, that is goodwill, real and worth having, and not recognition of this ME's value, because it is not connected to the work. If the mother says the year went well and attributes it to her son growing up, the value may be real and the ME's part in it is not recognized, and the settlement is correspondingly small.

Recognition happens in conversation, in survey responses in the respondent's own words, in retrospective participation, and in whether people show up again when invited. It also shows up by its absence: work that produced no recognition, however the numbers look, has produced no recognized value.

The design's commitment is to keep recognition as something residents do, supported by measures and never replaced by them. The scorecard's numbers, the e-VSM syntheses, and the retrospective's narratives are all inputs to a determination that residents did or did not recognize value from this ME's work. The neighborhood makes that determination. Platform compensation and the gates of Section 9 follow the determination, not the numbers alone. And the measures are built to make recognition visible rather than to manufacture its appearance, which is what psychometric honesty means in practice: an item that produces high scores without corresponding lived experience is a broken item, not evidence.

#### The anti-capture properties

The value definition's structural anti-capture properties are what make it different from ordinary outcome-based funding, and they are worth naming explicitly. Marc has asked that these be revisited after the field guide is created, since by then they will have been tested against real cases; they are listed here as a checklist for WWHA's charter and for Platform actor training in the meantime.

Outsiders cannot originate the scorecard. WWHA's board, funders, state agencies, evaluators, and academic partners can contribute methodological help, suggest possible questions, and offer to fund neighborhoods whose scorecards they find credible. They cannot substitute their scorecard for the neighborhood's judgment of what has been received. This is a governance constraint on WWHA itself and belongs in WWHA's charter as a founding limit.

Grantmakers who dislike a neighborhood's scorecard have one legitimate response: decline to fund. They do not get to negotiate the scorecard toward their own preferences as a condition of funding. That negotiation would be the capture pattern the design exists to prevent, and the ability to walk away is what protects the neighborhood's authorship.

Neighborhoods with scorecards that funders reject may still function. Diversified funding means no single funder's rejection can starve a neighborhood whose scorecard the funder disagrees with. The Platform's portfolio economics, Section 9, must maintain enough diversity of capital sources that any single source's departure is survivable.

Scorecard revisions cannot be triggered by funder pressure. The neighborhood revises its scorecard on its own timeline, informed by what it learns from its own experience. Funder feedback may be one input; the decision to revise is the neighborhood's alone.

Recognition cannot be manufactured through survey design. Items that produce apparent recognition without corresponding lived experience are failures of item design. The design record's Rasch commitment includes a commitment to psychometric honesty: items must measure what they claim to, and the yardstick must reach the hard end.

The neighborhood's "no" is respected. A neighborhood that determines an ME's work produced no recognized value has produced a legitimate assessment, even where evidence of outputs and the process of work suggest the ME did its job. The ME cannot appeal that determination to any higher authority; the residents' determination is final within the value definition's own terms. This is what distinguishes recognition from evaluation.

#### What Section 10 commits the design to

Value throughout the design record means what this section has defined: value created by residents for residents and their neighbors, with neighbors defined by the value creators themselves, recognized by the residents and measured by their own measures. No looser or more inclusive definition is available for use anywhere in the design.

Three tools do three jobs. The value network drawing shows the structure of value, where it moves and where returns are missing, at chartering and at retrospective. The neighborhood-authored balanced scorecard measures value on dimensions discovered from the neighborhood's own survey responses by Rasch calibration; each neighborhood constructs and maintains its own; the Platform helps and does not substitute its judgment. The e-VSM survey, before and after, measures whether the neighborhood's connective tissue changed, and validates a scenario before an ME forms around it.

Rasch measurement is the method underneath. Rating-scale responses are its raw material; summed Likert scores are not measures and are not used. The CAM is the precedent, unfielded; its governance clause, everything published in the commons, applies to every scorecard dimension. Two projects are committed: integrating Rasch with e-VSM results, including revising the survey's questions for measurement in neighborhoods; and the scenario-validation experiment on synthetic data.

Recognition is something residents do, supported by measures and never replaced by them; the neighborhood's determination is what settles compensation and gates.

The six anti-capture properties stand as a checklist for WWHA's charter and are to be revisited after the field guide exists.

Section 11 addresses the prior work this design record rests on and carries forward: CMG's 2016 methodology, the Medford and Spokane implementations, the thirty-year arc of Marc's related work, and the specific tools and frameworks, Ackoff's Idealized Design, Beer's VSM and Marc's e-VSM, Ostrom's polycentricity, Vester's biocybernetics, Meadows' leverage points, McKnight's associational-versus-service distinction, Flores' speech acts, Rasch measurement in the Wright and Mahoney line, that constitute its intellectual foundation.
