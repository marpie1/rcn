# Section 1 — Origin and Purpose

## What this document is

This is the RCN Design Record. It holds the reasoning behind a field guide that helps a neighborhood run an Idealized Design cycle at neighborhood scale, produce projects that improve how existing institutions and small groups work together for the neighborhood's residents, and do so in a form that structurally resists two failure patterns that reliably corrupt this kind of work. The projects can include creating new associations, whose work is volunteered, and new businesses, whose work is paid for by paying customers, as well as new coordination among the institutions and groups already there.

RCN is the ReLocalize Creativity Network, a network of people and neighborhoods sharing a pattern language, a set of foundational tools, and a peer network of conveners. WWHA is the Whatcom Wealth and Health cooperative in Whatcom County, Washington, whose granting arm is the first Neighborhood-Catalyzing Industry Platform this record describes. Both are introduced properly in Sections 6 and 11; they are named here so a reader who has never heard of either is not lost in the first page.

The field guide itself is a separate artifact, drafted against this record. This document holds the reasoning: why the field guide is shaped as it is, what it rests on, what patterns it exists to interrupt, what corrections its design absorbed as it took form. A reader who wants only the operational method should read the field guide. A reader who wants to understand why the field guide is that way, or who is joining the work and needs the thinking that made the field guide possible, should read this record.

Both documents are meant to be gardened rather than followed. Neighborhoods vary; the four conveners currently in position each operate in materially different contexts; the RCN foundational tools (SODOTO, CfA-dSC, the Overall Schema, the RCN Graph Tool, FedWiki, CAM, e-VSM) are themselves in ongoing development. The RCN Design Record will change as the work teaches us more. That is a feature.

## What the field guide exists to do

A neighborhood that is functioning at all already has small groups getting things done outside of business and government, usually with business owners and government officials appearing in peripheral roles, which is a sign of health rather than of institutional capture. The field guide exists to help such neighborhoods:

- Convene those small groups and help them create visible relationships, in diagrams and maps, with each other and with a granting arm capable of supporting their work
- Construct a shared picture of how the neighborhood's institutions and groups currently interact around the residents' lives
- Design what the neighborhood would want if it were unconstrained by history, which is Ackoff's Idealized Design adapted to neighborhood scale, bounded only by currently feasible technology and operational possibility
- Select projects, using a weighted-selection method that distributes decision-making across the participants rather than concentrating it in a facilitator or a small circle
- Charter those projects with commitments made as explicit speech acts, with catalytic seed capital from the granting arm
- Execute the projects, reflect on what happened, and support another cycle for a new round of projects where that is within the scope of the funder

The field guide's phases descend from a five-phase Linkage Mapping playbook designed for county-scale Accountable Community of Health work in Jackson County, Oregon, in 2014–2016. That county-scale work produced real between-institution projects. It also demonstrated that when the facilitation depended on a paid external team, the community could not sustain the practice after the team's engagement ended. The field guide is the neighborhood-scale adaptation of that playbook, with structural changes intended to keep that failure from repeating.

## The two failure patterns the field guide resists

The first pattern is external facilitator dependency. In Medford and Spokane, a skilled external team ran the process and produced concrete between-institution projects with measurable win-win-win outcomes. The next year, without the external team, the local organizations could not do it themselves. This was not for lack of intelligence, will, or skill on the local side. It was because the requisite variety needed to see between-organization opportunities, in Ashby's sense of the range of responses a regulator must have to match the system it regulates, is exactly what organizations do not have in themselves, and the external team had been carrying it. The field guide's design constraint is sharper than replacing the facilitator: reduce the facilitator variety that is required to a level distributable across the neighborhood, permanently. The tools themselves must do what the external, episodic facilitators did. That is a demanding constraint and it shapes many of the field guide's specific choices.

The second pattern is what John McKnight named the counterfeit of community: the philanthropic-industrial replacement of associational gift with professional service. A paid program officer whose salary flows from a foundation regardless of whether the funded work creates value for residents will, over time and regardless of individual intention, produce work shaped for the funder's satisfaction rather than for the residents' benefit. Technical assistance dumped on a neighborhood's lawn regardless of whether the neighborhood asked for it, and regardless of whether it produces value the neighborhood recognizes, is the mechanism. Making everything volunteer would be a purity that ignores that people doing real work deserve real compensation. The field guide's constraint is that compensation for the facilitation and granting roles must flow from value created by residents for residents and their neighbors, measured by measures the neighborhood itself constructs, rather than from any outsider's assessment.

Haier's RenDanHeYi provides the working model: an Industry Platform whose operators are compensated as a direct result of the successes of the microenterprises they seed and support, with the money flowing from the users and beneficiaries of what those microenterprises create. WWHA's granting arm is being designed as a Neighborhood-Catalyzing Industry Platform in this pattern. The four conveners, Carl in East County, Washington; Chris in Superior, Arizona, at Leo's; Jerry in Lansing, Michigan, at The Fledge; and Brent in southwest Lansing, operate or will operate as Industry Platform actors at the scale of the grouping of neighborhoods each serves. Their compensation, in money and in reputation, will flow through the value chain from the microenterprises and small groups they seed, whose value creation is recognized by residents and neighbors. Who counts as a beneficiary is defined by the value creators, and is never imposed by the grantors.

## Who this is for

The primary users of the field guide are Carl, Chris, Jerry, Brent, and any conveners who follow them as new neighborhoods engage with the work. Each already occupies the convener position in their neighborhoods and in nearby contexts. Each is doing something recognizable as elder-giving-back work, with real portfolios of what they have already accomplished. Chris and Jerry have founded commons: spaces created by and for the community for collaborative work, safe and culturally appropriate, that the neighborhood recognizes as its own. The field guide does not create conveners; it equips conveners who already exist.

The secondary users are WWHA's designers (Marc Pierson, Dave Chase, Gil Lund, Elizabeth Hampton, and collaborators) shaping the granting arm and its Neighborhood-Catalyzing Industry Platform function; future conveners who take up the field guide in neighborhoods that grow into readiness over time; and Ward Cunningham's Federated Wiki community, who created the open-source technology infrastructure the field guide is delivered on.

The reader of this design record, specifically, may be any of the above, or someone new to the work asking whether this is worth taking seriously. The record is written for that last case, the newcomer who needs the reasoning complete rather than compressed. Readers who already know the foundations can skim.

## What this is not

The field guide is not a program to be adopted, a curriculum to be delivered, a service to be rendered, or a template for standing up a new nonprofit sector. It is not a replacement for existing community organizations, though it may catalyze new relationships among them. It is not a route to grant funding for existing operating budgets; its funding logic is catalytic seed capital for new work. It is not an evaluation framework operated by outsiders on the neighborhood's behalf. And it is not compatible with paid facilitator or paid program-officer roles whose compensation is independent of resident-recognized value, which rules out funding for "technical assistance."

Neighborhoods that cannot sustain the field guide under these constraints are neighborhoods the field guide is not designed for. This is a hard filter. A neighborhood without a founded commons, or without small groups already getting things done, or without elders willing to take up the convener role, needs to get these in place first. Section 4 says what that looks like and what patience means while it happens.

## How this design record is organized

The remaining eleven sections work through the design in the order the reasoning arrived rather than in strict logical dependency, because the reasoning arrived by iteration and correction and the design record honors that history.

Section 2 lays out the four dependency-elimination principles: facilitator variety distributed rather than replaced; elder-driven Industry Platform actors compensated through the value chain; catalytic seed capital; and value created by residents for residents and their neighbors.

Section 3 describes the Viable System Model, with Idealized Design as its S4 activation, at neighborhood scale, and holds the shared reference for the e-VSM survey suite.

Section 4 develops the founded commons: what it is, why it is a hard filter, and what its succession looks like.

Section 5 introduces the customer scenario as the origin object, the durable thing the field guide's phases produce and MEs form around, with the Story Structure model as the connective vocabulary for writing one.

Section 6 works through the Neighborhood-Catalyzing Industry Platform pattern: what its actors do, how they are compensated through the value chain in four currencies, and how it differs from RCN itself.

Section 7 places moods and speech acts as the working layer running through every phase, including the nine states of a conversation for action.

Section 8 describes the between-institution project, the operational form a Solution ME takes, with the Ackoff Bell Labs case and the school-refugee-transition worked example.

Section 9 develops the catalytic seed capital principle in operational detail.

Section 10 defines value in the form settled through the design work, and the neighborhood-authored balanced scorecard that measures it.

Section 11 references the prior work this record rests on: CMG, Medford, Spokane, the Ripple ReThink Model, Highlander 3.0, and the thirty-year arc of related work.

Section 12 names the questions still open, including the ones RCN is actively grappling with, so the reader knows what is settled and what is not.

The five-phase structure the field guide is organized around, Linkage Mapping adapted to neighborhood scale, is referenced throughout and is the field guide's own spine rather than a section of this record. Cross-references among sections use FedWiki internal links where the design record is rendered as a FedWiki site. Where diagrams help, and several sections have them, they are drawn in the RCN Graph Tool and described in text so a reader without the picture still has the structural claim.
