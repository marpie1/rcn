# Section 8 — The Between-Institution Project

## What the between-institution project is

For most of his consulting life Marc treated every institution as a given. If one failed, the market would replace it. What harmed patients was the absence of support between institutions. Between the hospital and the clinic, between the clinic and the pharmacy, between any of them and the school or the landlord, people were on their own until they crossed the threshold of a healthcare establishment. Marc was well versed in the Toyota Production System and in Deming's teaching, and he knows how to improve the processes inside an institution. That was never the focus of his community-scale work in Whatcom County, in the Medford area (Jackson County, Oregon), or across the five eastern Washington counties convened in Spokane. The focus was the space between.

The between-institution project is the operational form a Solution ME takes when it addresses a customer scenario at neighborhood scale. It is a specific kind of work, different from a project inside a single organization, different from a partnership between two organizations, different from a program run by a coalition. It is distinctive enough to deserve its own section, and stable enough across Marc's Medford, Spokane, and earlier work that the pattern can be described operationally rather than in the abstract.

The between-institution project sits in the connective tissue among institutions, small groups, and residents. No single institution or small group owns it. It exists because a scenario requires an ecosystem to address, and the ecosystem requires work between the parties that none of them would do on their own. The value the project produces could not have been produced by any participant working alone; that is why the project exists.

Thirty of Marc's Spokane linkage maps document this form in its canonical pattern. Each map has an Improvement node at the center, connected by WORKS ON edges to several Institution nodes, with further relational edges among the institutions: SHARE_CLIENT, REQUEST_COORD, PRESENTS_TO, LEARNS_FROM, TRANSPORTS_CLIENT, MEMBER_OF. The Family & Individual node, the resident whose life the improvement serves, anchors most of the maps. The pattern is remarkably consistent across the thirty despite wide variation in subject: acute-care pharmacy transport, school-refugee transition, integrated addiction care, community-based screening for prevention, and so on. The Medford GGCS backlog holds sixty more scenarios and project stubs of the same form. GGCS is the Guidance Group and Communication Space, one of the seven Confluence work streams in the 2016 Medford architecture; its Jira project carried sixty stories under Epic GGCS-8, "All the feasible projects that could both improve population health and lower costs," the between-institution scenarios and project stubs that came out of Medford's Idealized Design work. Section 11 treats it in full.

That consistency across a large body of real work is evidence that the form is a stable pattern and not an artifact of one project's design choices. The RCN Design Record can name the form confidently and describe how MEs form to occupy it.

Two things are honestly unknown, and this section says so rather than papering over them. The first is how far the Medford and Spokane work translates to neighborhoods. Those were county-scale efforts among institutions with staff, budgets, and formal authority. A neighborhood's equivalent may be how its associations coordinate or even cooperate, and the role of process work at that scale is not yet clear. The two founded commons the design record leans on point in different directions: Jerry at The Fledge in Lansing has focused on helping locals create many new small businesses, and the need for RenDanHeYi-style support MEs there is plain; Chris at Leo's in Superior has focused on social activities, mutual support, and neighborhood improvement, gardens, home repair, fire prevention. Both are between-institution work in the sense this section means, and neither looks much like a Spokane linkage map. The second unknown is Marc's own vocabulary shift. He used to think in metasystems, as in his talk on emergent meta-organization at marc.relocalizecreativity.net/view/emergent-meta; he now tries to think in RenDanHeYi terms and in network terms. How best to help a neighborhood's already-existing self-organizing, without imposing a metasystem on it, is a question this section holds open.

## Forms of neighborhood value creation

Marc's neighborhood thinking has imagined three movements: more associations form; existing associations become more effective in their volunteer mode; and some associations, or people within them, create local businesses. The design record has so far treated the Solution ME as the one form value creation takes at neighborhood scale. It is one of several, and the others deserve their own treatment.

The four forms this section can name are these. An association that forms or strengthens around something residents care about, in McKnight's associational sense, with care as gift rather than service. An ME that forms around a scenario, does chartered work, and dissolves. An ME that finds its work requires a durable institutional form and becomes one: a business, a cooperative, a standing service. And an existing institution that changes how it works because an ME between institutions showed it something it could not see from inside. The between-institution project is the second and third of these, and it touches the fourth. The first is where the founded commons of Section 4 lives.

Whether these forms need their own think pieces, and whether the design record should carry a general account of neighborhood value creation, is open. This section flags it and moves on.

## Why between-institution work seldom works in the standard model

Between-institution work is famously difficult. Each participating institution has its own S3, day-to-day management that resists work exceeding its scope; its own S5, identity and purpose that shapes what it will and will not join; its own compensation structures, in which staff participation in joint work is unpaid overhead; its own accountability structures, which make joint work politically expensive when it fails; and its own risk-aversion, which treats participation in something it does not control as a threat.

The standard response is to create a new organization to hold the between-institution work: a secretariat, an integrator, a network administrative organization, a backbone team. The reasoning is sound as far as it goes. Between-institution work does need someone to hold it. The trouble is what the new organization becomes. It acquires its own S3, S5, compensation, accountability, and risk-aversion, and the between-institution work now has one more institution to work between rather than one fewer. This can work. Marc's judgment, from thirty years of doing this work in several places, is that it seldom does, and the RCN Design Record does not draw on the literature that promotes it.

The correction that matters here is about institutions themselves, and an earlier draft of this section had it wrong. Institutions are welcome in this design. Haier creates new institutions at a remarkable rate, and it does not hold on to average or underperforming ones. The failure pattern is holding on to an institution after it has stopped earning its place. Some MEs will and should become durable institutions when the work they do requires durable institutional form; the design record wants new institutions to replace outmoded ones. What RCN adds to Haier's practice is the evaluation. Haier uses business metrics almost exclusively. RCN intends to use balanced scorecards with tailored social measures, developed as needed through Rasch modeling, so that a neighborhood can tell which of its institutions deserve to persist by measures the neighborhood recognizes as its own. That is Section 2's fourth principle at work: value created by residents for residents and their neighbors, recognized by residents, measured by their own measures.

Marc's diagnosis, then, is that the standard response misidentifies what has to persist. Between-institution work needs four things to persist, and none of them has to be a new institution. A durable object, the customer scenario, that outlives any single attempt to address it. A compensation mechanism, the Platform's value-chain-embedded pay, that aligns the doers' interests with the value residents receive. A temporary self-organizing team, the Solution ME, that forms to do specific work and dissolves or continues on the merits. And a founded commons, Section 4, where the shared work can happen on the neighborhood's own ground. All four can be provided by the RCN foundational tools and by Platform actors working under the four principles of Section 2: facilitator variety distributed rather than replaced; elder-driven Platform actors compensated through the value chain; catalytic seed capital that departs; and value created by residents for residents and their neighbors.

This is the structural claim the between-institution project rests on. The form is workable at neighborhood scale under RenDanHeYi mechanics because those mechanics let between-institution work persist without requiring a new institution to hold it. The scenario persists in the FedWiki repository. The value chain persists in the CfA-dSC ledger. The people's histories persist in their SODOTO portfolios. The Platform persists as a position in the value chain. And when an ME does need to become an institution, it does so on evidence, with a scorecard that can later say whether it should continue.

## How the Platform persists

The earlier draft said the Platform "persists as a set of relationships in the value chain," and that did not parse. Here is what it means.

Individual MEs come and go. Each forms around a scenario, charters itself, does its work, and either dissolves or continues. What stays constant across many ME cycles is the Neighborhood-Catalyzing Industry Platform's position: it sits between the neighborhoods where scenarios emerge and the MEs that form to address them. From that position it holds four standing relationships. With the neighborhoods it serves, whose residents recognize value and whose scorecards settle what was created. With the MEs it has seeded, each of which it has a VAM stake in and each of which enriches the portfolios and scenarios the Platform will draw on next time. With other Platforms across RCN, from which it learns patterns and to which it lends elders. And with the RCN foundational tools, the scenario repository, the CfA-dSC ledger, the SODOTO portfolios, the e-VSM surveys, in which every completed cycle leaves a trace.

So "the Platform persists" means: while no ME lasts, the memory does, and the Platform is the actor whose job is to carry that memory into the next ME. It is persistent the way a river's bed is persistent while the water changes. It is a standing set of commitments, records, and relationships that make the next ME faster to form than the last, rather than an office that runs projects. Section 6 describes what the Platform's actors do from that position and how they are paid downstream of value received.

## Who is in a Solution ME, and how institutions commit

A Solution ME formed around a scenario draws its participants from wherever the scenario requires them. Some come from institutions: a school nurse, a social worker at a family services agency, a nurse practitioner from a community health center, a peer support specialist from a recovery organization. Some come from small groups already at work in the neighborhood: a community health worker network, a doula collective, a food bank's core volunteers, a mutual aid group's coordinators. Some come as individuals: a retired teacher, a family member of someone the scenario serves, a person with lived experience of the compound need.

The design record keeps two things apart here that are easy to confuse, and both are needed.

The first is how a person participates. Participants participate as themselves, contributing their capabilities, their portfolio, and their zero-distance knowledge. A school nurse in an ME brings what happens with children at the school in the first weeks. She does not join as the school's representative with authority to bind it. Treating a participant as if their presence commits their institution is one of the reliable ways between-institution work fails: the institution later declines to honor a commitment nobody authorized, and the ME's plan was built on it.

The second is how an institution commits. Institutions must still be able to make commitments to solutions, and the design record needs a way for them to do it. The way is a separate speech act by someone authorized to make it. If the school as an institution needs to change its intake process, lend a counselor's time, or share data, that is a Request from the ME to the school's leadership, answered by a Promise, a Counteroffer, or a Decline, and recorded in the charter alongside the participants' own promises. Section 7's conversation-for-action structure applies to institutions exactly as it applies to people; the only difference is who has standing to make the Promise.

Marc's expectation is that institutions will often commit by lending. An institution "loans" an executive or manager to an ME, frequently without monetary compensation, betting that the outcome will be worth the investment in less waste, delay, and frustration for the institution itself. That is a real institutional participation mode, and the charter should name it as such: this person participates as themselves, and this institution has separately committed their time. Both tracks then show up in the value chain. The person's contribution earns them a portfolio entry and, where the VAM says so, compensation. The institution's loan is a contribution the VAM can recognize too, in whatever currency the institution and the ME agreed, including none.

## How MEs form, last, and end

MEs form quickly. A Haier ME in China can form in two or three days when someone grabs a scenario from the platform. A neighborhood ME will form more slowly at first, while portfolios accumulate and trust mechanisms mature, but the design commits to fast formation as the mature state.

MEs may dissolve when the chartered work is done, and many will. Some will not. When an ME's work turns out to need a durable form, the ME becomes an institution: a business, a cooperative, a standing service in the neighborhood. That is a success, provided the new institution keeps earning its place. What the design record warns against is the standing committee, the permanent working group, the long-lived task force that persists because nobody declared it finished, with no scorecard anyone consults. The balanced scorecard and the e-VSM survey are how a neighborhood tells a living institution from a lingering one.

The Platform seeds and supports; it does not run. The Platform actor who helps an ME form, described in Section 6, is the EMC organizer in Haier's sense. The Platform provides mentorship, seed capital, contract help, and connection to other MEs and institutions. The ME's members govern the ME. The Platform is a supporting node in its value chain, with a VAM stake in the outcome and no authority over the work.

## Compensation, value chains, and value networks

When the ME's work delivers value that residents recognize, the Value Added Mechanism specifies how compensation flows across the ecosystem community: to the ME's core participants, to ecosystem members who contributed components or services, and to the Platform for its seeding role. Institutional employees participating in an ME may receive compensation through the ME in addition to whatever their employer pays them for their regular role; the two are distinct and both are legitimate. Small-group participants and individuals receive theirs directly. The four currencies of Section 6 all apply: fiat, time, gifts, and reputation. This alignment of everyone's compensation with the value residents receive is what makes between-institution work sustainable in a way unpaid coalition participation is not.

Three clarifications about the words, because three different traditions use "value chain" and they do not mean the same thing.

The value chain this section means is the RenDanHeYi one: the sequence of contributions from primary-ME through EMC members to value received by residents, with VAM allocations at each link. Value Stream Mapping from lean and Toyota is a different thing: it maps the flow of material and information through a single production process to find waste. Marc knows that tradition well; it is a tool for inside an institution, and the naming clash is unfortunate but real.

Verna Allee's Value Network Analysis is a third thing, and it is already built into the RCN foundational tools. Allee draws a value network as roles, not people, with every arrow naming the deliverable that moves and whether it is tangible or intangible, and with a transaction counting as complete only when the receiving role accepts it. The RCN Graph Tool carries this as a legend preset over its existing machinery plus two rules its validator enforces: every arrow names its deliverable, and every arrow declares its evidence as observed or asserted. The decision record is in the repo at docs/value-network-notation.md, the contract for anyone drawing one is in tools/schemas/graph-tool-v22.md, and six drawings exist, among them the Whatcom cooperative as a value network, the SODOTO acceptance rule, a teach chain read for dead ends and bottlenecks, and a hybrid cooperative showing where value goes versus where money goes.

The compare-and-contrast is short. VNA maps value flows that exist, for analysis; VAM specifies value flows that do not exist yet, for contracts. VNA answers "what actually moves between these roles today, and what ought to move and does not?" by looking at a drawing. VAM answers "when this ME succeeds, who gets what?" by negotiation before the work starts, with lower and upper limits per party, settled after value is delivered and recognized. They belong together in sequence. The ecosystem sketch of the formation sequence below produces a VNA drawing of the ecosystem as it is, with the missing returns drawn in red; the VAM is written against that drawing, turning each missing return into a specified future flow with a named party and a share. That is the integration Marc asked for, and it is untested. The test belongs on the Claude Code side: take one real scenario, sketch its ecosystem on the EIP Stage, draw its value network under the two rules, and write a VAM from the drawing. What survives that exercise is the method; what does not was invention.

The research and design work to use VAM contracts in neighborhoods at all is real and is not done. Haier's VAMs are negotiated among businesses with revenue. A neighborhood's VAM will settle in four currencies, most of them not money, on a scorecard the neighborhood built. That is why the CfA-dSC enhancement is its own work session and not a paragraph here.

## The scenario-to-ME formation sequence

The path from an articulated scenario to an ME doing work follows a pattern the design record can name step by step. Tools and methods that serve each step are named where they apply and listed at the end of the section; this is the footnote convention Marc proposed, adopted from here on.

**Scenario visibility.** A scenario lives in the FedWiki repository, articulated by an Experience-ME as a compound-need scenario or by a primary-ME as a vision-blueprint, as Section 5 distinguishes them, and validated through e-VSM survey work. It is visible to anyone who browses the repository: Platform actors, potential participants, other Experience-MEs, other neighborhoods considering a fork.

**Interest formation.** People who see the scenario and recognize their own capacity to contribute begin conversations with each other and with the Platform. Interest is not yet commitment; it is the moods of ambition and curiosity taking shape around a specific scenario.

**Ecosystem sketching.** The interested parties, often with a Platform actor's help, sketch what an ecosystem community would need to look like to address the scenario. Which capacities are required? Which institutions or small groups have them? Which specific people bring what? The tool for this is Marc's EIP Stage Sketch: a wall-sized drawing on the RCN Graph Tool's Environment / Institutions / Politics stage, made in front of the people concerned, with the essential functions the scenario touches placed by hand and the missing arrows drawn in as questions rather than findings. It is deliberately small, fifteen to twenty nodes, because it has to be read from across a room. The sketch is disposable; the learning is not. This step also answers Marc's question about whether Allee's method and the stage sketch are being combined: yes, in that order. The stage sketch says which roles are on the stage; the value network drawn from it says what moves between them and what ought to.

**Value chain specification.** The sketched ecosystem specifies the value chain: how the ME's work will produce value residents can recognize, whose contributions are needed at which stages, what the Leading Targets will be, how the VAM will share compensation across contributors. This is the negotiation that CfA-dSC contracts will eventually instrument formally. In the transitional state it is handled by hand with the same structural properties preserved: named parties, named shares with limits, settlement on recognized value.

**Chartering.** With the ecosystem sketched and the value chain specified, the ME charters itself. Chartering is a declarative act in Section 7's sense: the ME declares itself into being, named participants make specific promises to specific work, ecosystem members make specific offers, institutions make their separate commitments through someone authorized, the Platform commits a specific amount of seed capital, the scenario is declared as the object being addressed, and the CfA-dSC contract records all of it. Speech-act discipline at chartering is load-bearing. Vague chartering produces broken promises later.

Marc asked who the promise is made to, and who declares satisfaction or dissatisfaction, and said it is important to get right. The design record's answer is this. The primary Customer, in the conversation-for-action sense, is the resident whose life the scenario touches. The resident's declaration of satisfaction is registered through the neighborhood's balanced scorecard and the e-VSM survey; those are the resident's voice in a form the value chain can settle on. The Platform is a secondary Customer: its actors' compensation depends on resident-recognized value, so it has standing to ask and to declare, but it does not outrank the resident. Inside the ME, the EMC members are Performers to the primary-ME, which holds the Customer role on the residents' behalf and is answerable to them for it. That gives every Promise in the charter a named recipient, and every Declaration of satisfaction a named speaker. It has not yet been exercised in a live neighborhood, and the first charter will teach the design record whether the roles hold.

**Execution.** The ME does the work. Speed and specificity vary with the scenario; some can be addressed in weeks, others take years. The ME stays in contact with the residents whose lives the scenario touches throughout, at zero distance, and adjusts as what it learns changes what needs doing. The Platform is available for mentorship on request and does not manage.

**Retrospective and settlement.** When the work reaches the completion the charter specified, the retrospective assesses what happened: what residents actually received, measured through the neighborhood's own scorecard and the e-VSM survey; what the ME learned that others should know, fed back into the scenario's record of existing attempts; and what the value chain settles as compensation to each party. Settlement is a declarative act. The ME declares what has happened and what it means, the residents' scorecard says whether that declaration stands, and the scenario returns to the repository enriched. Marc's proposal for the retrospective's evidence is a before-and-after pair of e-VSM surveys, and a series of EIP Stage Sketches kept as a storyboard showing how the ecosystem changed over the life of the ME. Both are speculative and neither has been done in this fashion; the storyboard is on the EIP Stage's own to-do list as a distinct object from versioning a single sketch.

**Dissolution or reconstitution.** The ME dissolves, or declares that its work requires a durable form and becomes an institution, or charters a second cycle. Participants return to their base contexts with their SODOTO portfolios enriched by the work. If the scenario needs more work, the same people can charter afresh or a different ME can form around the enriched scenario. What persists is the scenario, the contracts, the portfolios, the scorecard, and the Platform's memory of all of them.

One piece of this sequence depends on work not yet done. When bids go out for an ME, the work history of each potential participant has to be available to the people forming it, and that history has to be more than a list of badges. SODOTO today carries teaching and learning credentials. Carrying ME participation, scenario authorship, and resident attestations of value is the SODOTO portfolios extension, and it is the first of the two Claude Code sessions this section commits to.

## A forty-year scenario: Ackoff at Bell Labs

Marc asked whether Russ Ackoff's Bell Labs story might be read as the creation of a set of customer scenarios that took the next forty years to deliver on, and whether placing it in the RenDanHeYi frame would help, since so many people know the story. It does help, and the story is worth telling straight before reading it that way. What follows is from Ackoff's own telling in a recorded lecture, the transcript of which is in the design record's sources.

In 1951 Ackoff, newly on the faculty at Case Institute of Technology, happened to be visiting a friend at Bell Telephone Laboratories in Murray Hill on a morning when the vice president in charge of the laboratories had called every section head to a meeting. The friend smuggled him in. The vice president arrived late, visibly distressed, and told the room: "Gentlemen, the telephone system of the United States was destroyed last night." When they protested that they had used the phone that morning, he told them that if they did not believe it by noon they were fired. Then he grinned, and explained.

He had made a list of the laboratory's greatest contributions and asked the room for theirs. The dial: introduced in the early thirties, invented around 1890. Multiplexing, which put six conversations on one wire and raised the system's capacity sixfold: introduced between the wars, invented before 1900. The coaxial cable: 1882. "The three most important things ever done for this company by this laboratory were all done before anybody in this room was born. What the hell have you guys been doing?" His answer: identifying the system's deficiencies one by one and correcting them, without improving the system at all. Treating the parts, not the system.

So the system was destroyed, and they would spend a year designing the one they would replace it with right now if they were free to, subject to two constraints. It had to be technologically feasible: no telepathy, but a communications satellite was allowed because they knew they could build one. And it had to be operationally viable, which did not mean reachable from where they were, since they were nowhere, but meant able to survive in the current environment: legal, financial, and the rest. He split the room into six teams by subsystem, with a weekly meeting of team leaders so that the year would end with one design and not six. Ackoff landed on the team redesigning the telephone itself.

That team's first instinct was to list what was wrong with the telephone, and someone pointed out that a nonexistent thing has no deficiencies. So they wrote specifications instead, the way you would for a house. Ninety-two properties, over two weeks, with no disagreement about any of them: no wrong numbers; know who is calling before you answer; use it without hands; have it come to you when it rings. Then they took the first property and designed for it. Ackoff phoned the head of the lab's psychology department to ask about wrong numbers and found a man who had been researching them for seventeen years and had never once been asked; four in five were the right number dialed wrong. The team put a register and ten buttons where the dial had been, so you could see the number before the call went through. They called in two young engineers to ask whether it was feasible. The engineers whispered to each other, lost interest in the wrong-number problem, and walked out without a word. Three weeks later they came back to apologize: the buttons had been the exciting part, they had built a push-button telephone, tested it on two thousand people, found it saved twelve seconds a call, which was a twenty percent increase in the capacity of the whole system, and had started a project that week. Its code name was Touch Tone.

Over the year the teams designed telephones that met every one of the ninety-two properties, all feasible, and all of them have since been built. The video telephone, teleconferencing, call forwarding, the pocket telephone that tells you who else is calling while you talk: Ackoff's claim in the lecture was that every major technological change in the Bell System since 1953 came out of that project, and that most of what would happen between then and 2000 would still be coming out of it, because it would take that long to use everything it produced. He called the method idealized design, and it became the core of his interactive planning. Its first consequence, he said, is that everybody can participate, because there is no such thing as an expert on an ought question.

Now the RenDanHeYi reading, which is close to what Marc guessed.

The ninety-two properties are a customer scenario, and a nearly complete one. It is a vision-blueprint in Section 5's terms, written by a primary-ME, the telephone team, against a compound need the whole room had been living with without seeing. It was compelling enough to make people want in, and specific enough to be bid against. It lived in the repository, which at Bell Labs was the laboratory itself, and it outlived every team that worked on it.

The two young engineers are the clearest ME in the story. They grabbed one piece of the scenario, the buttons, formed in an afternoon, built and tested a solution, and came back with a settled result and a project. Their leaving without explanation is what an ME grabbing a scenario looks like from the outside when nobody has instrumented it: the bid, the promise, and the settlement all happened, but none was declared, and the telephone team spent three weeks angry. RenDanHeYi mechanics would have made the grab a visible Request and Promise with a VAM stake, and the anger would not have happened. The engineers were right to run. The lesson for neighborhoods is that the running should be recorded.

The forty years are the point Marc was after. A nearly complete scenario can be delivered piecewise, by successive MEs and EMCs, over a span that no single team, budget, or institution could hold. What held it was the scenario itself, persistent and specific, and an institution, Bell Labs, that functioned as the Platform: it seeded the scenario, set the two constraints, structured the teams, and then did not run them. That is the between-institution project's answer to the question of what persists.

Two smaller readings. The psychologist who had studied wrong numbers for seventeen years without being asked is zero-distance knowledge sitting inside an institution that nobody had reached across a boundary to collect; Section 5's Experience-ME exists to make that reaching routine. And the vice president's "everybody can participate" is Section 2's fourth principle stated in 1951: the people affected by a system are the authority on what it ought to be, whatever they know about how it works.

What Bell Labs had that a neighborhood lacks is one payroll. Everyone in that room was paid by the same employer, so no VAM was needed to align the contributors. A neighborhood ME's contributors come from many institutions and from none, which is why the VAM, the four currencies, and the resident-held scorecard are not optional extras in the RCN version of the story. They are the substitute for the single payroll.

## The school-refugee-transition worked example: a network of nested systems

Section 5 named the school-refugee-transition scenario as the design record's worked example, because a refugee student is simultaneously a student in the school system, a child in a family with its own cultural and legal navigation to do, a resident of a neighborhood, a member of a diaspora community with its own internal support, and a client of health and social services. The structure lives in the fact pattern; the frame surfaces it.

An earlier draft called that structure polycentric. Marc prefers "a network of nested systems," and the reason is worth stating because it is a reason about how people see. "Polycentric" brings sets to mind, overlapping circles with the student in the intersection. That is one true picture. The other true picture is a network: the student as a node with a membership edge into each system, and the systems with coordination edges among themselves. The design record uses both, and the diagram that accompanies this section draws both at once. Each system is drawn inside the larger system it sits in, which is the nesting: the school inside its district, the household inside its extended family and kin, the local cultural community inside a diaspora, the clinic and the resettlement agency inside health and human services, the neighborhood and its CHW network inside town and county. The student sits at the center with a blue arrow into each. The grey dashed arrows among the systems are the coordination that exists today, taken from Spokane linkage map #7. The green dotted arrows are where the Solution ME draws its participants from. The diagram is in the RCN Graph Tool's format, so it can be edited, argued with, and redrawn for a different scenario; it is at Design Record/diagrams/refugee-student-network-of-nested-systems.rcn.json. "Polycentric" is reserved from here on for the case Section 6 names, two Platforms with overlapping constituencies, where the word means what Ostrom meant by it.

Spokane map #7 documents the between-institution form for this scenario. Its Improvement node, "School-Refugee Transition," connects by WORKS ON to five Institution nodes: Schools, World Relief, CHW Network, Family & Individual, and by implication the health providers the CHWs coordinate with. The relational edges include SHARE_CLIENT between institutions serving the same family, PRESENTS_TO from the family to the schools and to World Relief, and REQUEST_COORD among the institutions and from the CHW Network to each of them.

In ME terms, the scenario invites a Solution ME to form around the compound question: what does the first year in a US school look like for a refugee student, and what would it take across school, family, cultural community, health, and social services for that first year to go well for the student and the family? The ME's composition would depend on the neighborhood, but plausibly includes a school nurse or counselor with zero-distance knowledge of enrollment and the first weeks; a resettlement caseworker who knows the family's arrival context; a community health worker from the neighborhood's CHW network with existing relationships in the refugee community; a parent from the same or an earlier cohort whose lived experience says what actually helped; a teacher or two from the school; a cultural or religious community leader whose network is where the family will actually seek support; and Platform-actor support from whoever is convening the neighborhood the school serves. Alongside them, the school district, the clinic, and the resettlement agency each make their separate institutional commitments, most likely by lending the time of the people above.

The ME's Leading Targets would be specific to what the ecosystem can actually deliver: a coordinated intake that captures the family's context without re-traumatizing; a designated navigator, probably the CHW, whose role every participating institution understands; check-ins at named intervals; a shared understanding of what school-family communication requires when the family's English is limited and the school's cultural competence is uneven; and a specific commitment about what happens when something goes wrong at school, since the family's first instinct may not be to contact the school.

The VAM would specify how compensation flows when the year goes well, as measured by the neighborhood's scorecard with input from the family, the school, the resettlement agency, and the cultural community: to the CHW who did the navigation, to the school nurse whose knowledge shaped the design, to the community leader whose network held the family, to the Platform actor whose seeding made the ME possible, in whichever of the four currencies each party agreed to.

If the year goes badly, if the family withdraws, if the student's grades suggest untreated difficulty, if the family reports feeling isolated or misunderstood, the settlement is correspondingly small, and the scenario returns to the repository with what was learned. The next ME to form around it, whether substantially the same people chartered afresh or different people entirely, starts from the enriched scenario rather than from zero.

Marc wants to go to the trouble of actually writing this scenario, and one or two others, rather than describing them, and to produce a playbook for how an Experience-ME writes one. Section 5 holds the material for the playbook: the two scenario levels, the fourteen-element template, the Story Structure model, and Marc's own FedWiki pages on the customer scenario template and on writing one. Writing the first real scenario is a next work item for the design record, and it is the natural first test of the ecosystem-sketch-to-value-network-to-VAM sequence described above.

The network of nested systems is what makes this work hard and what makes the RenDanHeYi mechanics necessary. The student is at the intersection of the school as a viable system with its own S1 to S5 at the district's recursion level, the family as a viable system at household scale, the cultural community as a viable system at the scale of a community of affiliation, the neighborhood as a viable system at the geographic level the field guide operates on, and the provider ecosystem at whatever institutional level each provider occupies. No single institution's operational logic can hold the whole scenario, so the ME has to form specifically to be the connective tissue none of them can be.

## The RCN Workbench

Section 7 promised that this section would develop the RCN Workbench, the proper name for the integrated tool suite that instruments the formation sequence at operational scale. It is the analog of Haier's Workbench, the internal platform on which scenarios are posted, MEs bid, EMCs form, and VAMs settle. The RCN version is in design, with pieces built and the whole not yet assembled, and this is a statement of what it is meant to be.

At its base is one graph: the Neo4j database into which the RCN foundational tools already write. Scenarios from the FedWiki repository, charters and VAMs from CfA-dSC, portfolios and badges from SODOTO, surveys from e-VSM, and drawings from the Graph Tool all land in it as nodes and relationships. The Workbench is the set of views onto that graph that the formation sequence needs, each of which is a lens and none of which owns the data; there is no new tool underneath it.

Five views are planned. A scenario browser, where a resident, a Platform actor, or a potential participant reads the scenarios the neighborhood holds, their existing attempts, and their current state. An ME chartering view, where the ecosystem sketch, the value network, the VAM, and the named promises are entered and the CfA-dSC contract is produced. A portfolio viewer, where a person's history across MEs, scenarios, teaching, and resident attestations can be read when a bid goes out. A convener facilitation view, for the moods-and-speech-acts work of Section 7 in a room, including the shared background of understanding that Section 7 identified as something the Workbench should make explicit. And a Platform dashboard, where the Platform's standing relationships, the neighborhoods, the seeded MEs, the settled and unsettled VAMs, are visible as the persistent structure described earlier in this section.

What the Workbench instruments, step by step, is the sequence above: visibility through the scenario browser; interest formation through the conversation records; ecosystem sketching through the EIP Stage and the value network drawing; value chain specification and chartering through the chartering view and CfA-dSC; execution through the conversations for action that the contract tracks; retrospective and settlement through the scorecard, the e-VSM pair, and the VAM settlement; dissolution through the portfolio entries that result. Every step leaves a record in the graph that the next ME can read.

Two design commitments carry over from elsewhere in the design record. The Workbench does not route decisions through Claude or through any single gate; Claude's role, where it has one, is as an advisor that reads the graph and surfaces suggestions, not as a step every action must pass through. And the views are cave drawings in the sense Section 4 and the Graph Tool's own design goal intend: images a group can read, point at, and argue about untrained, with vocabularies kept small on purpose.

## What Section 8 commits the design to

Between-institution work is the operational form Solution MEs take when they address customer scenarios. It is a distinctive form, documented across Marc's Spokane and Medford work, stable enough to describe as a canonical pattern, and honestly untested at neighborhood scale.

Creating a new organization to hold between-institution work can work and seldom does. The design record holds the work instead with durable objects, the scenarios; with structural mechanisms, the Platform, the CfA-dSC contracts, the SODOTO portfolios, the resident-held scorecard; and with temporary self-organizing teams, the MEs. Institutions are welcome to arise from this work when the work requires them, and are evaluated by balanced scorecards with Rasch-developed social measures rather than kept by inertia.

Solution ME composition draws from wherever the scenario requires: institutional participants, small-group members, individuals. People participate as themselves. Institutions commit separately, through an authorized speech act, often by lending people. MEs form quickly and dissolve, continue, or become institutions on the merits. The Platform seeds and supports and does not run; it persists as a position in the value chain and as the memory carried from one ME to the next.

The scenario-to-ME formation sequence, visibility, interest, ecosystem sketching, value chain specification, chartering, execution, retrospective and settlement, dissolution or reconstitution, is the operational pattern the field guide's phases produce and the RCN Workbench will instrument. The resident is the primary Customer of every promise in a charter; the Platform is a secondary Customer; the EMC members are Performers to the primary-ME. A curriculum in SODOTO for this sequence and its tool skills is needed and not yet designed.

The Ackoff Bell Labs case shows a nearly complete scenario delivered piecewise over forty years by successive MEs and EMCs, held by the scenario and by an institution acting as Platform, and shows what a neighborhood must supply, the VAM and the resident-held scorecard, in place of the single payroll Bell Labs had.

The school-refugee-transition example illustrates the form in a network of nested systems, drawn both as nesting and as network, and is the first scenario the design record intends to write in full.

Two Claude Code sessions are committed as separate pieces of work with distinct design tensions: the SODOTO portfolios extension, so that a participant's work history is available when bids go out; and the CfA-dSC enhancement for chartering, VAM specification in four currencies, speech-act instrumentation, and gated funding. The prep for the second is work Marc and Claude do together before the session starts.

Section 9 develops catalytic seed capital, Section 2's third principle, in operational detail: how much, on what basis, deployed by whom, evaluated against what, and recovered how, as the form that principle takes when it meets the formation sequence described here.
