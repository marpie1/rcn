# Section 9 — Catalytic Seed Capital

## What catalytic capital is and is not

Money enters the work described in this design record in one form only: catalytic seed capital that funds new work at its launch and departs when the new work has become self-sustaining or when it has failed. This form is distinct from the several other forms money commonly takes in philanthropic and public-sector work, and the distinctions matter because the failure modes of the other forms are exactly what the design exists to prevent.

The diagram that accompanies this section, a value network in Verna Allee's notation drawn with the RCN Graph Tool, shows where the money goes in one neighborhood ME. Nine roles, fifteen arrows. Every arrow names what moves. The one thick amber arrow is catalytic seed capital, flowing once from the Platform to the ME at launch. Every other money arrow is drawn thinner on purpose: diversified capital from funders to the Platform; fees or payments from residents to the ME where the work earns them; VAM shares from the ME to its members after recognition; a return share from the ME to the Platform where revenue exists; the Platform actors' share, paid only after residents recognize value. Beside the money are the flows that are not money: loaned staff time from institutions, recognition and reputation from residents, enrichment back to the scenario and the portfolios. And one dotted red arrow, operating subsidy in year three, is drawn as the flow the design refuses, so that if it ever shows up in a real drawing everyone can see it. Nothing in the drawing is measured. It is a structure to argue with, and the first real ME should be drawn from what actually moved.

Catalytic seed capital is not operating subsidy. Operating subsidy funds ongoing work at whatever level it takes to keep the work going. It creates dependency by design: the recipient becomes dependent on the subsidy's continuation, and the funder acquires ongoing power over the recipient's decisions. Operating subsidy is what most philanthropic grant-making effectively becomes over time, whatever its original framing. The pattern is well documented and reliable. The design's opposite is independence by design.

Catalytic seed capital is not project funding in the standard sense. Standard project funding pays for a discrete piece of work, usually scoped, budgeted, and time-limited by the funder, or by the recipient in compliance with the funder's priorities. It funds work that would happen more or less the same way whether this funder chose it or a different one. It selects among existing candidates for work that could happen anyway; it catalyzes nothing new.

Catalytic seed capital is not investment in the venture-capital sense. Venture investment expects financial return proportional to risk taken, and the return flows to the investor rather than to the residents whose lives the venture affects. The venture's success is measured in the investor's terms; the community whose problem the venture addresses is treated as a market rather than as the beneficiary.

What catalytic seed capital is: money that enables an ME to try something it otherwise could not, sized to make the launch possible, structurally shaped so that it departs when the ME either succeeds and becomes self-sustaining or fails and dissolves. The catalyzing happens because a launch that could not have happened without the capital now happens. The departure happens because the capital's job is done at launch, and needs no external judgment about when to withdraw.

## The catalyzability test

The Industry Platform, when considering whether to seed a Solution ME forming around a scenario, applies a specific test: is this catalyzable? Whether it is a good project, whether it addresses a real need, whether it would create value if it worked are all easier questions and all less useful. The catalyzability test asks: will the seed capital do its work and then be able to step away, or is this a request for ongoing subsidy dressed as a launch?

Answering the question requires distinguishing between two situations that look similar on the surface.

In the first, the ME needs capital to acquire capabilities, relationships, tools, or presence that, once acquired, become part of the ecosystem's ongoing operational capacity. A CHW network that receives seed capital to hire and train its first cohort of workers, whose ongoing work then generates the fees or grants or referral revenue that sustains the network, has been catalyzed. The seed capital enabled the acquisition; the acquired capacity now runs on its own metabolism. The Platform's role in that ME is done at launch.

In the second, the ME needs capital to do work that has no self-sustaining metabolism, and the work will need capital continuously in order to continue. A program that provides a specific service to residents and requires ongoing funding to keep providing it is being subsidized, whatever the seed framing says. The seed framing is aspiration; the operational reality is dependency.

The distinction is often unclear at the moment of ME formation. Some MEs that appear catalyzable turn out to be subsidy-shaped once execution begins; some that appear subsidy-shaped develop an unexpected self-sustaining metabolism. What matters is that the Platform asks the question honestly at formation and re-asks it during execution. The catalyzability test is a discipline, and there is no formula.

### Gates: what Haier does, and what RCN will do instead

Marc asked for research on how Haier's Industry Platforms stage their investments, since the staging is where the catalyzability discipline becomes mechanism. What Haier does is documented in Hamel and Zanini's account in the Harvard Business Review (November–December 2018) and is worth stating precisely, because it is more layered than "three phases."

Haier funds a new ME in stages, each conditional on the last. The Thunderobot gaming-laptop ME received a first tranche of internal seed money "with the understanding that further funding was conditional on a successful market test"; when the first 500 units sold out in five days and the next 3,000 in twenty minutes, the team wrote a business plan and received 1.2 million RMB more, to which the team members added 400,000 RMB of their own money for a 15 percent stake, after which outside venture firms joined. Haier's leaders, "recognizing they may not be well placed to judge the merits of a new idea, often require a start-up team to obtain outside funding from one of the company's venture capital partners before agreeing to contribute internal resources"; in one period nine of fourteen new MEs got external investment before Haier's own. So the first gate is often somebody else's judgment, and the second is the team's own money.

Once an ME is running, compensation is tied to three performance thresholds, and these are the gates Marc had in mind. Baseline: when quarterly sales and earnings growth exceed a base target, the team gets a bonus proportionate to the excess. VAM, the value-adjusted mechanism: if the ME reaches a midpoint between baseline and its leading target, the bonus doubles, and team members may put their own money, typically 15,000 RMB a quarter, into a special account that pays a 100 percent dividend if the VAM target is hit again the next quarter. VAM annual: beating the VAM target four quarters running makes the team eligible for profit sharing, 20 percent of net profit above the goal, rising toward 40 percent as the ME closes on its leading target. And money is withdrawn when gates are missed: if an ME fails its baseline for three months in a row, a leadership change is triggered automatically; if it meets baseline but misses VAM, two-thirds of the members can vote the leader out; and any ME that consistently underperforms is restructured or shut down.

All of it is profit- and market-based, as Marc says. RCN needs its own phases and gates, and this is critical enough that the design record names the shape now and commits to working it out with the first real ME rather than leaving it to the field guide.

The RCN gates should keep three things from Haier and change three. Keep the staging: seed capital is released in tranches, and each tranche is conditional on the last having done its work. Keep the outside judgment before the Platform's own: a first gate that someone other than the Platform has to pass, which at neighborhood scale is the residents' own recognition on their scorecard rather than an outside investor. Keep the team's own stake: ME members commit something of their own, which in four currencies can be time and reputation as easily as money. Change the measures: RCN's thresholds are resident-recognized value on the neighborhood's balanced scorecard, in the four currencies, with Rasch-modeled social measures where the neighborhood has them, and never profit alone. Change the response to a missed gate: the seed stops, the scenario is enriched with what was learned, and no one is discarded. Change the pacing: a neighborhood's supply of enthusiasm is limited, and a Platform that takes wild bets with it, the way venture capitalists take bets with an endless supply of supplicants they discard like trash, creates the frustration and resentment that ends neighborhood work. The gates exist as much to protect that supply as to protect the money.

A first shape, offered for argument: gate one, launch, the charter is complete and the first tranche is released; gate two, first recognition, residents have recognized something on their own scorecard within the charter's stated window, and the second tranche is released; gate three, metabolism, the ME can show how it continues without the Platform's money, and the last tranche is released or, more often, is not needed. Missing gate two or three ends the seed and settles what was created so far. The end-to-end process diagram that accompanies this section places these gates in the whole sequence.

### Judgment, and how it fails

Platform actors who have seen the pattern many times are better at the catalyzability discipline than newcomers. Marc's thirty years of work produce catalyzability judgments that a first-year foundation program officer cannot match, because the pattern is only visible through many repeated cases. This is one of the specific reasons the Platform actors must be people with that history: the judgment they bring cannot be substituted by training or process.

They also fail this test in specific ways worth naming. They can become attached to particular kinds of work and want to seed instances of it whether or not the specific instance is catalyzable. They can become resigned about a domain and refuse to seed real opportunities because they have been burned before. They can lose the distinction between the pattern they know and the specific case in front of them. The Platform's compensation structure, outcome-dependent, downstream, variable, is what may correct for these tendencies over time. A Platform actor whose seeding judgments reliably fail the catalyzability test will see their compensation and reputation decline; that signal is more honest than any peer review process would be.

## Sizing

The size of catalytic seed capital is set by what the launch requires, bounded by what the Platform can deploy without over-committing to any single ME, and shaped by the specific structure of the scenario the ME is addressing.

The lower bound is what the ME actually needs to launch. Under-seeding to spread capital across more MEs is a failure mode; MEs that launch under-capitalized fail more often, and each failure represents lost work, lost portfolio accumulation, and lost resident-received value that a well-seeded ME might have produced. The Platform's compensation and reputation depend on ME success; under-seeding to appear frugal actively harms the Platform's own long-term position.

The upper bound is what the launch actually requires. Over-seeding creates its own failure mode: the ME comes to treat the seed capital as ongoing budget rather than as launch fuel. Once an ME's operations scale to consume a specific level of continuous funding, the ME cannot easily contract back to a self-sustaining scale; the over-seeded ME either becomes a subsidy case or dissolves painfully. Over-seeding also concentrates the Platform's capital in fewer MEs, reducing the diversity of scenarios that can be seeded and the diversity of learning that returns to the repository.

Between the bounds, Christopher Alexander's Oregon Experiment gives the Platform a rule for the portfolio as a whole. Alexander's principle of piecemeal growth, worked out for the University of Oregon in the 1970s, divides a year's funds so that many small projects, a good number of medium ones, and a few large ones all happen, on the order of a thousand small, a hundred medium, and ten large, with roughly equal money going to each size class. Otherwise the large projects take all the funding, and the small, fast, user-driven work that teaches the most never gets done. Marc's memory of it as "thirds" is the working version: the Platform sets the split among small, medium, and large seeds in advance and holds to it, which also gives the neighborhood many small chances to learn the pattern before anyone bets big.

Within a size class, the specific amount depends on the scenario, the ME's composition, the ecosystem community's other contributions, and the expected timeline to self-sustainability or completion. Some MEs need substantial capital because their launch involves acquiring durable infrastructure: equipment, physical space commitments, technology systems. Others need modest capital because their launch is mainly assembling relationships and negotiating agreements, with the operational work happening through participants' existing roles and contributions.

Choosing among candidate MEs is where the weighted-selection matrix from the 2014 Linkage Mapping material belongs, distributing the decision across participants rather than concentrating it in the Platform. RCN has no tool for it yet; building one is on the list. Vester's sensitivity model, with its impact matrix of what drives what, is the other candidate for selecting projects to fund, and Section 7 already contrasts it with the CMG weighted-selection matrix.

There is no universal formula. The Platform actor's judgment, informed by pattern recognition across many MEs, is what sizes the capital. Over time, portfolio data accumulated in SODOTO makes the pattern visible: MEs of form X seeded at size Y in context Z tend to produce outcome W. Platform actors calibrate against that data. The judgment does not become mechanical, but it becomes better informed.

## Deployment mechanics

The mechanics of how seed capital moves from the Platform to the ME depend on RCN foundational tools that are in active development. In the mature form:

The ME's charter, Phase 4 in the field guide's structure and described in Section 8, includes the Value Added Mechanism specifying how compensation will flow across the ecosystem community when the work delivers resident-recognized value. The seed capital's deployment is the first commitment in the VAM, the Platform's contribution enabling the launch, and its gates and its recovery are specified alongside.

CfA-dSC instruments the deployment as a smart contract. The contract encodes the promises (the ME's commitments to specific work, the Platform's commitment of specific capital in specific tranches), the offers (ecosystem members' contributions), the declarations (the scenario being addressed, the Leading Targets being aimed for), and the settlement conditions (how the VAM will divide value when it arrives, how residents' recognition will be assessed).

The Overall Schema tracks the ME as a node in the graph, with edges to the scenario it addresses, the Platform actors seeding it, the ecosystem members participating in it, the founded commons where the work happens, and the residents whose lives the scenario touches. Graph queries surface patterns across MEs, help Platform actors see cross-ME learning opportunities, and hold the material for the retrospective at Phase 5.

SODOTO portfolios attach to individual participants, ME core members, ecosystem contributors, Platform actors, and accumulate their traces of work: what they contributed, what value flowed, what they learned. The portfolios are the persistent record of who has done what over time, and they feed the trust mechanisms that eventually make fast ME formation possible. This is more than SODOTO does today, which is teaching and learning credentials; adapting it to carry this Workbench function is the first of the two Claude Code sessions Section 8 commits to.

### What "fully operational" means, and running it by hand

Marc asked for a clear statement of what "the foundational tools fully operational" currently means, with a drawing. The second diagram accompanying this section is that statement: one ME end to end, sixteen steps from a scenario to an enriched scenario, each colored by the state of the tool that carries it on the day of writing. Green is built and usable: the scenario pages in FedWiki, the e-VSM survey before and after, the value network drawing. Amber exists and needs extension for this use: the EIP Stage Sketch, CfA-dSC for a multi-party charter and for the ME's web of promises, the scorecard with Rasch, SODOTO for ME portfolios, the Neo4j graph the Workbench will read. Red is not built and is done by hand: weighted selection, the VAM, gated seed deployment, settlement. The design is fully operational when every box is green.

The same drawing answers Marc's demand that all of this be done in a very small test, end to end, as soon as possible, creating or simulating the missing pieces as we go. Nothing in the red boxes is beyond doing by hand for one ME: a VAM on one page, a gate agreed in a conversation, a settlement declared in a meeting and written into a FedWiki page. In the interim before the tools are fully operational, deployment happens through manual coordination that preserves the structural properties: outcome-dependence, downstream flow, the ability to return small or zero, alignment of Platform compensation with ME success, alignment of ME compensation with resident-received value. Running one ME by hand, with the red boxes simulated, is the fastest way to learn which of them the tools should carry first.

## Recovery and reuse

Catalytic seed capital that has done its work becomes available for the Platform to deploy again to seed new MEs. This is what makes the Platform sustainable across the neighborhoods it serves: capital cycles through, catalyzing successive waves of new work rather than being deployed once and then requiring replenishment from outside.

The specific recovery mechanism depends on the ME's form. Some MEs, once launched, generate revenue streams (fees, service payments, grants for continuing operations) that can include a return-to-Platform component as part of the VAM. Others produce value that residents recognize but that generates no financial revenue; in those cases the recovery is portfolio and scenario enrichment rather than capital return, and the Platform's compensation comes through its VAM proportion rather than through capital recovery.

"VAM proportion" means the Platform's share of whatever value the ME's work is settled at, as agreed in the charter before the work began, in whichever currencies the settlement is in. If the settlement is money, the Platform's proportion is money. If it is time, gifts, and reputation, the Platform's proportion is in those, which is why Section 6 insists that the Platform's compensation be understood in four currencies and not one.

Marc's edits add a second currency of return that the first draft missed, and it belongs here as a design commitment. The spread of desirable skills through SODOTO portfolios is itself an outcome of the investment: an ME that leaves five people able to do something the neighborhood could not do before has repaid part of its seed whether or not money came back. So is the completion of part of an important scenario: a nearly complete scenario, in the Bell Labs sense of Section 8, is repaid piece by piece. And SODOTO's own pay-it-forward rule, that whoever learns must teach, suggests an exponential form of repayment worth seeking and acknowledging on purpose: not only did this ME's members learn, but whom did they then teach, and whom did those teach. The Platform's model should count all three as return, and the scorecard should be able to see them.

MEs that fail do not return capital. That is by design. Failure is expected and priced into the Platform's portfolio economics. The failed ME's contribution is the enrichment of the scenario in the repository: what was tried, what did not work, what the next team should know. That contribution has real value even when no financial recovery is possible, and the Platform's model accounts for it.

The Platform's portfolio economics, taken across all the MEs it seeds over time, some succeeding and some failing, some returning money and some returning only skills and learning, must work out so that successful MEs return enough capital in total, across enough time, to fund continuing seed deployments plus the Platform's own compensation. The first draft called this "the aggregate model," which named nothing; portfolio economics is the plainer phrase, and it is what the first paragraph of this subsection was reaching for. The precise economics depend on ME success rates, capital sizing, VAM specifications, and the mix of financial-recovery and enrichment outcomes. The design record cannot specify them because WWHA's initial deployment will be the first real test, and the model will develop empirically.

Two design commitments constrain the portfolio economics without specifying them. First, the Platform cannot become dependent on any single funding source, foundation, government grant, individual donor, in a way that gives that source control over which MEs get seeded. Diversified funding is structurally necessary to prevent capture, and not merely prudent. Second, the Platform's own compensation must remain outcome-dependent even when capital sources are guaranteed for a period. Guaranteed capital can be used to seed more MEs; it cannot be used to pay Platform actors on a fixed basis, because that would violate Principle Two from Section 2 and would stop the learning that variable compensation forces.

A third commitment follows from Marc's edits. If part of the capital the Platform receives must be spent non-catalytically, as the cooperative's reinvestment in Relocalize Health may have to be, then the proportion of that capital going to each category, catalytic seed, non-catalytic reinvestment, the Platform's operations, is established in advance on stated reasoning and held to. Alexander's rule for project sizes and this rule for capital categories are the same discipline: decide the proportions before the requests arrive, because once they arrive the large and the urgent will take everything.

## The catalytic principle at other scales

Marc questioned whether the first draft's long treatment of the catalytic principle across scales earned its place, and asked how a founded-commons scale differs from a neighborhood scale. It does not; that was one scale counted twice. What survives is a short paragraph, and the justification for keeping even that is that Section 2's Principle Three, catalytic seed capital, applies to the field guide's own presence in a neighborhood and to the Platform's own launch, and a reader who sees the principle only as a rule about money will miss both.

The field guide is catalytic in the same sense as the money: conveners support a neighborhood through the phases and then step back, and if the neighborhood cannot continue without the convener, the field guide has failed the catalyzability test. WWHA itself is a first-generation Neighborhood-Catalyzing Industry Platform, and whatever RCN provides for its launch is seed that departs; WWHA becomes self-sustaining through its own compensation mechanism and diversified capital, or it fails and enriches the RCN scenario of how to launch a Platform. Leo's and The Fledge are analogous instances at their own recursion levels. And the people who hold Platform, convener, and ME-leader roles eventually release them, usually by mentoring the younger neighborhood people who take them up; holding on past the release point is the same failure as year-three subsidy. That is Vester's biological logic: new things emerge from old through variation and selection, and each scale's success is measured by what it lets grow rather than by what it holds in place.

## What Section 9 commits the design to

Money enters the work as catalytic seed capital, and never as operating subsidy, standard project funding, or venture investment. The catalyzability test distinguishes real launches from subsidy requests dressed as launches, and it is applied by Platform actors whose pattern recognition is the specific capacity they bring.

Seed capital is released in gates. RCN's gates keep Haier's staging, outside-judgment-first, and the team's own stake, and replace Haier's profit thresholds with resident-recognized value on the neighborhood's own scorecard in four currencies. A missed gate stops the seed, settles what was created, and discards no one. The gates are to be worked out with the first real ME.

Capital sizing is set by what launches actually require, between under-seeding and over-seeding, with the Platform's portfolio split among small, medium, and large seeds fixed in advance after Alexander. Selection among candidate MEs uses a weighted-selection matrix, a tool RCN still has to build.

Deployment happens through CfA-dSC contracts in the mature form and by hand in the interim, with the structural properties preserved. What "fully operational" means is stated in the end-to-end diagram, and the design commits to running one ME by hand, end to end, at small scale, as soon as possible.

Recovery returns to the Platform for reuse, in money where the ME earns it and in skills spread, scenario parts completed, and pay-it-forward teaching where it does not; all three count. Failed MEs return no capital and still return learning. The Platform's portfolio economics must work across successes and failures, and will be learned empirically.

The Platform cannot depend on any single funding source, its actors' compensation stays outcome-dependent even when capital is guaranteed, and any non-catalytic category of spending has its proportion fixed in advance and held to.

Section 10 develops the value definition, value created by residents for residents and their neighbors, recognized by them, measured by their own measures, in operational detail: how the neighborhood-authored balanced scorecard is constructed, how residents' recognition is assessed, and how the e-VSM survey layer supports the assessment.
