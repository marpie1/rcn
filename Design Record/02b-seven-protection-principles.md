## Section 2b — The Seven Protection Principles

### Why these seven, together

Section 2's four principles close the doors that outside dependency walks through. These seven close the doors that harm walks through. They are a separate set because they answer a separate failure, and because the design had, until they were written, one of the two halves of the picture Marc drew for the Paris safety meeting: an environment that is complex, dynamic and interdependent, a set of principles, and beneath them two ovals side by side. The right-hand oval is continuous improvement of processes, effectiveness, efficiency, timeliness, managing predictable situations. The left-hand oval is the high-reliability organization, safety, managing uncertainty, the unexpected and the unpredictable. RCN grew the right-hand oval in depth: Deming, API, statistical process control, A3, value stream mapping, the linkage of processes, Rasch, the cycle, conversations for action. It grew almost nothing of the left. Before this section, the words high reliability, operational risk, just culture, latent condition, barrier and breakdown appeared in the design record and the field guide not at all, or once.

Harm walks through two doors. The first is Reason's: every organization runs on two currents, production and protection, and protection loses by default, because production's return is visible this month and protection's return is an accident that did not happen. On his own copy of the picture Marc wrote, in the margin, "1° protection, 2° production." The second door is Amalberti's: protection imported from the wrong model, aviation's rules laid on adaptive work, either strangles the work or is ignored, and either way teaches people that safety is someone else's paperwork. Amalberti's own account of what a system must give up to become ultra-safe, limits on maximum performance, professional autonomy, the craftsman's mindset, local arbitration, and complexity itself, is a list of exactly what a neighborhood must not give up at the level of the people doing the work. So the first principle cannot mean avoid risk. It means the trade-off is named, by the people who bear it, at the level that can decide it.

The seven arrived in the order below and are kept in it: what to protect, which model, who decides, what a barrier is, what a breakdown is, what makes people speak, and what to read first. Shortest form: protection named first, model matched to the work, decisions at the level that bears them, barriers as promises, breakdowns blameless, speaking up built and measured, latent conditions read before incidents.

### Principle One — Protection is named before production

Every scenario and every charter declares what must not be lost before it declares what will be produced: the people doing the work, the residents it touches, the relationships and commons it depends on, the volunteer's evenings, the neighborhood's trust in the next attempt. The Platform accepts no unnecessary risk to what is named, and accepts necessary risk only where the benefit is stated against it. A charter with no protection clause is not complete.

This is the Navy's first two principles of Operational Risk Management, accept risk when benefits outweigh the cost and accept no unnecessary risk, turned to face the neighborhood rather than the mission. It is also Reason's two-currents argument written as a rule rather than a warning. The failure it closes is the one every funded initiative knows: the report was delivered, the volunteers were used up, the relationship with the school did not survive, and nobody had written down that the volunteers and the relationship were the point.

### Principle Two — The safety model matches the work, and the match is declared

A scenario carries its domain, in Snowden's terms, known, knowable, complex or chaotic, and its safety model, in Amalberti's, adaptive, high-reliability or ultra-safe. Work done by an ME is adaptive by default and is governed by practice and judgment, not by procedure. The Platform's money, the Shared Care Plan's health data, and anything that can injure a body are high-reliability or ultra-safe and are governed accordingly, with procedure, supervision and audit. Neither model is applied to the other's work.

The failure this closes runs both ways. A neighborhood that borrows the hospital's incident forms for a community kitchen will stop running the kitchen. A neighborhood that runs a person's health record on the trust that runs the kitchen will lose the record, and the person. Marc's Paris deck put Snowden's ontologies third among its three suggestions and drew the Stacey diagram and Cynefin beside Weick's arch of wisdom between overconfidence and paralysis; the design's Choose phase had no such sorting question until now. The declared domain also decides which of the tools downstream apply: procedure and checklist where cause and effect are known, barrier analysis where they are knowable, probe-sense-respond and the Vester model where they are complex.

### Principle Three — Risk decisions are made at the level that bears them, and the level is visible

Every hazard on a charter names which band decides it: operational, the ME itself; collective choice, the neighborhood's rules; constitutional, who has standing at all. Risk that arrives at the operator without a decision having been made above is the design's definition of risk transfer. An undecided hazard found in a retrospective is a finding about the band that should have decided it, not about the person who absorbed it.

This is the Navy's fourth principle, make risk decisions at the right level, made concrete by the three IAD bands the design already uses. Pieper's smokejumpers said what it looks like when the principle is absent: "that's all we do is transfer the risk and it always transfers to the operator"; "between getting that order at dispatch and launching that jump plane there should be some level of analysis and risk management, even if it's quick, and that has to happen in operations right now, because it's not happening in strategic." His practitioners could see the empty middle level on a map and could not see it in a thirty-page report. The design makes the level a field on the charter so that its emptiness is visible before the work, not after.

### Principle Four — Barriers are promises, and promises are barriers

In a neighborhood the defences are commitments, roles and rules, not hardware. Every barrier a charter relies on is a signed speech act with a named holder and a condition of satisfaction. Every promise on a charter is read as a barrier, asking what it protects and what is exposed if it fails. A barrier nobody has promised is a missing barrier, and is recorded as one before the work starts.

This joins Reason's defences-in-depth to Flores's conversations for action, which the design already runs. Tripod's barrier logic was built for valves and guards; here the guard is the neighbor who promised to be at the door, the rule that two people count the money, the institution's authorized person who promised the room. Reading the charter's promises as barriers is what turns Section 7's speech acts into a safety instrument without adding a form. It is also the forward-looking use of the NRM drawing: a barrier map made before the ME bids, with the missing barriers shown in their own color.

### Principle Five — Breakdowns are blameless by default and are the unit of learning

A declared breakdown is a gift to the work. Before any judgment of an act, the retrospective applies Reason's substitution test: would a peer with the same skills, in the same situation, plausibly have done the same? Only sabotage, reckless violation and repeated negligence leave the blameless zone. Declaring a breakdown never costs the person who declared it. The design makes that a rule, not a hope.

On his copy of Reason's culpability tree Marc wrote, beside the blameless-error box, "breakdown for learning." That is the bridge between the two disciplines this section draws on. Flores's breakdown, the moment the flow of action stops and something is revealed, and Reason's blameless error are the same event seen from two sides. The design already has the conversation for a breakdown; it now has the rule that governs what happens to the person who declares one. Without the rule, Principle Six cannot be built, because nobody speaks up in a place where speaking up has cost someone.

### Principle Six — Speaking up is produced by engagement, and both are measured

People report danger because they belong to a team and a culture that wants to hear it, not because leadership has said that safety matters. In Mahoney's PeaceHealth model, 6,751 employees, Rasch-calibrated items, structural equations, engagement predicted "regardless of the situation I feel perfectly free to speak up if I see something I think is a threat to patient safety" at .65, and safety-culture leadership predicted it at .18. The design therefore builds engagement as the safety mechanism and measures verbal vigilance directly, before and after, with calibrated items. A neighborhood whose people will not speak up is not safe whatever its rules say.

The items exist: the six on Mahoney's slide, with one substitution. Where the hospital items said "management/leadership," the neighborhood items say the outside, the municipality, the funders, the professionals providing services, McKnight's question exactly. Engagement is asked about the inside, the small groups, the ME, the neighbors. The same respondents answer both, and the survey's Worlds carry the difference between a resident's answer and an institution employee's. Section 10's Rasch tool calibrates them; the calibration Mahoney ran is gone, and is re-derivable from the neighborhood's own responses.

### Principle Seven — Latent conditions are read before incidents, not reconstructed after

Incidents at neighborhood scale are rare and slow; the conditions that produce them are present and surveyable now. The neighborhood reads its latent-condition profile in Phase 1, alongside e-VSM, and every incident analysis afterwards is compared against that reading. Learning from incidents is the last of the safety moves, not the first.

This is Tripod Delta rather than Tripod Beta. Shell built both: Beta is the incident tree, and it is what the NRM tool imported; Delta is the proactive half, a questionnaire of observable yes/no conditions put to a sample of the workforce, aggregated into a Failure State Profile of the eleven Basic Risk Factors, run before anything has gone wrong. The design's version puts the eleven factors into neighborhood terms, six of them, incompatible goals, communication, organization, training, procedures and defences, translating almost word for word and mapping onto e-VSM's homeostats; the other five, hardware, design, maintenance, housekeeping and error-enforcing conditions, becoming the physical and informational commons and the strain on the people. Everyone answers every question; the Worlds a respondent chooses, resident, small-group holder, institution employee, service recipient, funder, tell the aggregator whose corner each answer comes from. The profile is a Phase 1 reading, re-run at every retrospective, and the NRM drawing of any later incident is read against it.

### How the seven hold together

Principle One says what is protected. Principle Two says how tightly, and forbids borrowing the wrong tightness. Principle Three says who decides and makes an empty decision visible. Principle Four says what a defence is in a place with no hardware, and makes the charter the safety instrument. Principle Five says what happens when a defence fails and a person is standing there. Principle Six says what produces the report of the failure in the first place, and measures it. Principle Seven says read the conditions now, so that the rare incident is compared to a baseline rather than reconstructed from memory.

Any one of these without the others invites the familiar outcome: protection declared and then traded away unnoticed; the wrong model imported and resented; risk pushed down to whoever is holding the work; promises made without anyone asking what they guard; the person who reported the breakdown quietly leaving; a survey of latent conditions nobody could answer honestly. Together they let a neighborhood do adaptive work, which is risky by nature, with the trade-off in view and the people who bear it in the room.

The seven land in the field guide as follows. Principle One and Principle Four in Phase 4, the charter: a protection clause and the reading of each promise as a barrier, with ORM's five steps, identify, assess, decide at the named level, implement the controls, supervise, run inside the chartering evening. Principle Two in Phase 3, Choose: the domain and safety model declared on the Customer Scenario and a protection row on the Weighted Selection Matrix. Principle Three across the charter and the retrospective, as the band named against each hazard. Principle Five and Principle Six in Phase 5, Look: the substitution test in the Retrospective and the vigilance items in the before-and-after survey. Principle Seven in Phase 1, Understand: the latent-condition survey run with e-VSM. And in the substrate, hazard, barrier, latent condition and breakdown become things the Overall Schema can hold, so that a promise can be a barrier and the NRM label stops being the only place protection lives.
