### Section 3 — VSM at Neighborhood Scale, with Idealized Design as the S4 Activation

#### Why the VSM belongs here

Every attempt to organize collective work at any scale runs up against the same set of functional requirements. Something has to do the actual work. Something has to coordinate the doers when they'd otherwise interfere with each other. Something has to keep the whole operation running day to day. Something has to look outward and forward to see what's coming and imagine what could be. And something has to hold the identity and purpose that makes any of this worth doing at all.

Stafford Beer named these five functions and worked out the relationships among them across four decades of consulting practice, publishing the mature form in *Brain of the Firm* (1972) and *The Heart of Enterprise* (1979). He called the whole a Viable System Model — viable because a system with these five functions properly related can survive and adapt in an environment that keeps changing, and any system missing one or more of them cannot. The VSM is descriptive of what viability requires, not prescriptive of any particular organizational form. Human bodies are viable systems. Cities are viable systems. Firms that survive are viable systems. Neighborhoods that survive are viable systems.

The five functions, in Beer's numbering:

- **System 1** — the operational units doing the actual work. Multiple S1s exist in any real system; each has its own local viability and its own bit of the environment to work with.
- **System 2** — coordination among the S1s. Handles the anti-oscillation problem: when two S1s would otherwise interfere with each other, S2 handles the resolution.
- **System 3** — day-to-day internal management. "Inside and now." Keeps operations running, resolves resource allocation, handles the ongoing stuff that S1 and S2 can't handle on their own.
- **System 3\*** — audit and monitoring. Sporadic direct look at what S1s are actually doing, providing S3 with information it wouldn't otherwise have.
- **System 4** — outside and future. Environmental scanning, strategic modeling, imagining what could be. This is the function most organizations do worst, because it produces nothing measurable in the short term.
- **System 5** — identity, purpose, policy. The meta-system. Holds "why we are together at all."

Beer's insight was that these functions are recursive: any S1 in a larger viable system is itself a viable system with its own five functions internally. A neighborhood is an S1 in a larger geographic-political container; it is also a viable system with its own S1s, S2, S3, S4, S5 inside it. José Pérez Ríos's diagrammatic work extends this to show that multiple recursion criteria can apply simultaneously — the same territory can be carved by geographic containment, sectoral function, or cultural affiliation, each producing a different recursion structure. This is Ostrom's polycentricity expressed as system structure.

For the design record, VSM does two things at once. It provides the frame for understanding what a neighborhood needs to have functioning to be viable. And it provides the frame for understanding what this design is intervening on specifically — because most neighborhoods that struggle are struggling with a specific VSM deficit that can be named.

#### What a neighborhood as viable system looks like

A functioning neighborhood has all five VSM functions operating, often without anyone naming them that way.

Its **S1s** are the operational units doing the actual work of the neighborhood's life. Small groups getting things done outside business and government, as you named them earlier — with business owners and government officials appearing in peripheral roles. The volunteer fire department, the Baptist church's mutual aid group, the food bank's core volunteers, the after-school tutoring circle, the trail maintenance crew, the neighborhood watch, the informal childcare network that runs among a handful of families, the elders who quietly check on people who live alone. In a healthy neighborhood these S1s are numerous, overlapping, and known to each other.

Its **S2** is the coordination that keeps these S1s from oscillating against each other. Sometimes formal (a monthly neighborhood association meeting), often informal (the network of people who know who knows what and who can get a message to whom). The kitchen table where two people meet to work out how the food bank's Tuesday distribution and the church's Wednesday meal don't tread on each other. S2 is often invisible when it's working and painfully visible when it stops.

Its **S3** is the ongoing management of the neighborhood's daily life. In a neighborhood, S3 is distributed across many people carrying pieces of it — the person who tracks which houses have new residents, the person who knows when the sidewalk got fixed, the person who notices when a lamp is out, the person who keeps the list of who's homebound. Rarely a formal role at neighborhood scale; almost always a set of people doing S3 work as part of larger giving-back activity.

Its **S3\*** is whatever direct-look-at-what's-actually-happening exists in the neighborhood beyond the ongoing S3 stream. In healthy neighborhoods, this often looks like the elders who walk regularly and see everything, or the crisis response that happens when someone notices something is off. It gets more or less formal depending on scale and culture.

Its **S4** is the function most neighborhoods do worst — outside and future. The imagining of what the neighborhood could be if things were different. The scanning of what's coming (demographic change, economic pressure, environmental risk, cultural shift) that will require adaptation. S4 requires stepping out of the ongoing rush of S1-through-S3 to look at what isn't yet visible in daily operations, and it requires people who can hold both the outside-scan and the inside-vision at once. Almost every neighborhood is deficient in S4. Most neighborhoods that are struggling are struggling here first.

Its **S5** is the identity and purpose that makes the neighborhood something more than a set of overlapping addresses. In some neighborhoods S5 is carried by long-standing cultural or religious identity. In others by shared history — the neighborhood that formed around a particular immigration, a particular industry, a particular struggle. In others by ongoing conscious articulation — a neighborhood assembly or covenant that names why we are here together. Where S5 is thin or contested, S4 has nothing to design toward and the neighborhood is at risk of drifting into whatever external pressures shape it.

#### The specific VSM deficit this design intervenes on

The kit does not attempt to install all five functions in a neighborhood. Neighborhoods that have S1s already functioning, some S2 in operation, distributed S3 more or less working, and some form of S5 present — these are neighborhoods the kit can serve. What the kit specifically supplies is S4 activation, plus the S3\* audit function that makes S4's work honest over time.

This is a narrow claim and worth stating precisely. If a neighborhood has no S1s — no small groups already getting things done — the kit is not for that neighborhood. If a neighborhood has no S5 — no identity or purpose beyond happening-to-share-geography — the kit cannot supply one, and the invitation-into-conversation Phase 1 opens will surface that absence. If a neighborhood has S1s, some S2 and S3, and enough S5 to make convening possible, but no functioning S4 — no capacity to imagine what could be and no scanning of what's coming — the kit's phases activate S4 through a specific pair of tools.

**Idealized Design activates S4's inside-and-desired function.** Russell Ackoff's method: design what the neighborhood would want if it could have it, bounded only by currently feasible technology and operational possibility. Ackoff's two constraints are exactly the discipline that keeps idealized design from becoming a wish list and exactly the door that lets the design be revised as feasibility changes. What the neighborhood wants is not a wish; it is an operationally-possible present that history has prevented. Naming it precisely is S4's core work.

**SWOT-type environmental scanning activates S4's outside-and-future function.** Strengths and weaknesses as they exist now in the neighborhood's operational reality; opportunities and threats as they are visible on the horizon. Coupled with Idealized Design, the pair gives S4 both hands — inside-and-desired plus outside-and-coming. Neither alone is sufficient. SWOT alone is reactive; the neighborhood sees what's coming but has no vision to steer toward. Idealized Design alone floats; the neighborhood knows what it wants but doesn't see what's actually going to hit it.

Together they let a neighborhood know what it wants to be and what is coming at it. That is the prerequisite for building rather than firefighting — and Marc's phrase, "so we are not just fighting fires but building something together," is exactly what S4 makes possible when it is functioning.

Beer's own view was that S4 uses whatever tools of anticipation fit the scale. At corporate or governmental scale, S4 tools include large-scale System Dynamics modeling of the kind ReThink Health developed and Marc originated the Ripple ReThink variant of for Whatcom County. At neighborhood scale, System Dynamics is oversized — the data requirements exceed what a neighborhood can produce, and the parameters that System Dynamics moves are the weakest leverage points anyway, per Meadows. Ripple ReThink remains available as a Marc-facilitated tool for higher-recursion questions when a neighborhood surfaces one that needs it. At neighborhood scale, Idealized Design plus SWOT-type scanning is enough for S4.

#### The S3\* audit function and how it prevents S4 from becoming self-referential

An S4 that never gets checked against reality becomes fantasy. An S4 that gets checked only through S3's ongoing management gets folded back into current operational logic and loses its outside-and-future function. Beer's S3\* — sporadic direct look at S1 activity independent of the S3 management stream — is what keeps S4 honest.

At neighborhood scale, S3\* takes the form of retrospectives, portfolios, and structured multi-perspective survey. Phase 5 of the linkage-mapping cycle is explicitly a retrospective — how did the ME's work actually land, what did residents actually receive, what did the neighborhood's scorecard actually show. The SODOTO portfolio layer accumulates the traces of what people and MEs actually did across cycles. The e-VSM survey provides periodic multi-perspective read on how the neighborhood is seeing itself. Each of these is an S3\* instrument, providing S4 (and the Industry Platform actors who share compensation with the neighborhood's success) with information the ongoing operational stream would otherwise smooth over.

Without S3\*, S4's Idealized Design cycles would drift over time toward whatever the loudest voices in the convening room want, or whatever the convener's own preferences run to. With S3\*, the design remains honest to what is actually being received by residents — which is the value-definition principle from Section 2 made operational through instrumentation.

#### The polycentric recursion — why this only works at multiple scales simultaneously

The José Pérez Ríos recursion structure shows that any viable system exists at multiple recursion levels simultaneously, with multiple recursion criteria carving the same territory differently. A neighborhood is an S1 in a higher-recursion viable system (city, county, region). A neighborhood is itself a viable system with S1s inside it (small groups, MEs, individual practices). And the recursion criteria vary — the same neighborhood is carved by geographic containment (this block, this ZIP code, this school catchment), by sectoral function (health, education, food, safety), by cultural affiliation (parish, ethnic community, generational cohort), and each carving produces a different S1/S2/S3/S4/S5 structure that is simultaneously true.

For the kit, this recursion structure has three operational implications.

First, projects surface at their own scale. Some scenarios that a neighborhood identifies are actually neighborhood-scale — the ME can form within the neighborhood and the value chain terminates in residents the neighborhood recognizes. Others are higher-recursion — the scenario is real for the neighborhood but the ME needed to address it must form at city or county scale because the required variety exceeds what the neighborhood contains. The kit needs to make this distinction visible so that neighborhood-scale MEs don't get asked to do higher-recursion work (which would exceed their variety and fail), and so that higher-recursion opportunities the neighborhood surfaces don't get dropped or handled locally at the wrong scale (which would exceed the neighborhood's variety and fail differently).

Second, the granting arm (WWHA and its equivalents) exists at higher recursion than the neighborhoods it serves. WWHA is an Industry Platform for a cluster of neighborhoods; it needs its own S4 function operating at cluster scale, its own scanning of what's coming across the cluster, its own Idealized Design work at cluster scale. The kit for neighborhood convenors does not attempt to install that higher-recursion S4 — but the design record recognizes it exists and needs to be developed separately.

Third, the political dimension of Ashby's Law becomes discussable through the recursion structure. When a higher-recursion institution (a city government, a state agency, a healthcare system) absorbs a problem whose actual required variety lives at neighborhood scale, the institution has taken on work it cannot properly regulate, and the neighborhood-scale variety that could match that work has been silenced or dismissed. This is the pattern Marc named as the political problem the design must make visible without turning it into war. The recursion structure gives it operational language: this problem belongs at the recursion level whose variety matches it, and mismatches produce systematic failures that everyone eventually pays for. Ostrom's polycentricity to the limit, as Marc named it. Mutuality across scales rather than capture by any single scale.

#### What this section commits the design to

Everything downstream in this design record depends on the VSM frame holding. Section 5's customer scenario as the durable object between Experience-EMC-analog and Solution-EMC-analog is a scenario at whatever recursion level the scenario actually lives; the linkage-mapping method surfaces both the S1-through-S3 current state and, through Idealized Design, the S4-desired state; the Industry Platform's compensation flows through the value chain from ME work at appropriate recursion to resident value received at appropriate recursion; the moods-and-speech-acts layer that runs through every phase is what lets S4's imaginative work translate into S1 commitment without losing its S4 character.

The frame also constrains what the kit will not attempt. The kit is an S4-activation-with-S3\*-audit tool for neighborhoods that already have S1-through-S3 functioning and some form of S5 present. It does not attempt to build neighborhoods from scratch, install identity where none exists, replace failing operational units, or substitute for the political-cultural work that only a neighborhood can do for itself.

Section 4 develops the founded-commons concept — the physical and social infrastructure that a functioning S1-S5 substrate requires in order to sustain the S4 work the kit activates. Without the founded commons, S4's outputs have nowhere to accumulate and nowhere to be gardened over time.

### Section 3 — VSM at Neighborhood Scale, with Idealized Design as the S4 Activation

#### Why the VSM belongs here

Every attempt to organize collective work at any scale runs up against the same set of functional requirements. Something has to do the actual work. Something has to coordinate the doers when they would otherwise interfere with each other. Something has to keep the whole operation running day to day. Something has to look outward and forward to see what is coming and imagine what could be. And something has to hold the identity and purpose that makes any of this worth doing at all.

Stafford Beer named these five functions and worked out the relationships among them across four decades of consulting practice, publishing the mature form in *Brain of the Firm* (1972) and *The Heart of Enterprise* (1979). He called the whole a Viable System Model: viable because a system with these five functions properly related can survive and adapt in an environment that keeps changing, and any system failing one or more of them cannot. The VSM is descriptive of what viability requires, and prescribes no particular organizational form. Human bodies are viable systems. Cities are viable systems. Firms that survive are viable systems. Neighborhoods that survive are viable systems.

The five functions, in Beer's numbering:

- **System 1** — the operational units doing the actual work. Multiple S1s exist in any real system; each has its own local viability and its own bit of the environment to work with.
- **System 2** — coordination among the S1s. Handles the anti-oscillation problem: when two S1s would otherwise interfere with each other, S2 handles the resolution.
- **System 3** — day-to-day internal management. "Inside and now." Keeps operations running, resolves resource allocation, handles the ongoing work that S1 and S2 cannot handle on their own.
- **System 3\*** — audit and monitoring. A sporadic direct look at what the S1s are actually doing, giving S3 information it would not otherwise have.
- **System 4** — outside and future. Environmental scanning, strategic modeling, imagining what could be. This is the function most organizations do worst, because it produces nothing measurable in the short term.
- **System 5** — identity, purpose, policy. The metasystem. Holds "why we are together at all."

Beer joined these with specific channels. The resource bargain between S3 and each S1 is a negotiated link, in which the operating unit is given resources against an accountability it accepts. The S2 channel is coordination communication, carrying the information the S1s need to keep out of each other's way. And the algedonic channel carries pain and pleasure signals straight from the operations to S5, bypassing the management hierarchy, so that something going badly wrong is felt at the top before the reports arrive. Beer's central mechanism for all of this is the homeostat: two parts of a system that regulate each other through a coupled loop, each holding the other within limits. Marc's extension of the model, described below, starts from that mechanism.

Beer's insight was that these functions are recursive: any S1 in a larger viable system is itself a viable system with its own five functions internally. A neighborhood is an S1 in a larger geographic-political container; it is also a viable system with its own S1s, S2, S3, S4, and S5 inside it. José Pérez Ríos's diagrammatic work extends this to show that several recursion criteria can apply at once. The same territory can be carved by geographic containment, sectoral function, or cultural affiliation, each producing a different recursion structure. This is Ostrom's polycentricity expressed as system structure, and it is the network of nested systems that Section 8 draws.

For the design record, the VSM does two things at once. It provides the frame for understanding what a neighborhood needs to have functioning to be viable. And it provides the frame for understanding what this design is intervening on specifically, because most neighborhoods that struggle are struggling with a specific VSM deficit that can be named.

#### From VSM to e-VSM

Beer's five functions say what a viable system must have. They say less about how the parts of a living neighborhood hold each other in regulation from day to day, and a survey built on the five alone asks a respondent to judge functions that few people experience as such. Marc's e-VSM, the extended Viable System Model, is an extension of Beer built to be answered by ordinary members of an organization and read back to them, and it is the form of the VSM that RCN actually uses. This subsection honors Beer's contribution by showing where the two are the same, and then says what was added.

e-VSM describes an organization, a neighborhood, a community group, a business, a network, as eleven interconnected spheres of capacity, grouped in three layers. The Sensory and Interface layer faces outward: awareness within the neighborhood, awareness beyond it, and the flow of resources across its boundary. The Internal Core layer manages day-to-day operation: tools and workspaces, the ability to get things done, and the quality of planning. The Alignment and Homeostatic layer holds the whole together over time: coordination, involvement, the quality of learning and change, leadership, and culture. The three layers are a Markov blanket in Karl Friston's sense, sensory states, active states, and the internal states they shield, and that is deliberate: the layers name the boundary through which a neighborhood senses its environment and acts on it, and the interior that maintains itself behind that boundary.

The correspondence with Beer runs by layer rather than sphere for sphere, and it is stated here as a first pass for Marc to correct. Beer's S1s are the operating units the neighborhood contains, which in e-VSM sit one recursion level down, as the sections below explain. Beer's S2 is the coordination sphere. Beer's S3 is spread across the ability to get things done, resources, and tools. Beer's S4 is spread across awareness within and beyond, planning, and the quality of learning and change. Beer's S5 is culture, leadership, and identity. The isomorphism is real: everything Beer's model requires is present, and a reader who knows Beer can find each function. The spheres cut the same territory more finely, in words a respondent can answer to.

What e-VSM adds is the homeostats. Beer instrumented a few of the couplings in a viable system: the negotiated links, the coordination channel, the algedonic signal. e-VSM generalizes the homeostat to every meaningful sphere-to-sphere relationship: thirty-three paired homeostats, each a coupled regulation in which two spheres hold each other within limits, assessed in both directions. Each sphere's state is the outcome of its coupled regulatory processes with its neighbors. A survey built on this does not ask "how is coordination?" in isolation; it asks whether coordination is holding planning within limits and whether planning is holding coordination within limits, and it asks the same of every pair that matters. The result is a picture of the connective tissue, which is where a neighborhood's viability actually lives, rather than a scorecard of eleven separate parts.

Every sphere and every edge carries two names: an anatomical framing, in the language of systems theory, and a vernacular description in the language of the community being surveyed. In RCN's own graph of the model the spheres are called Coordination, HR, Tools, Outreach, Innovation, Worldview and Identity, Policies and Legal, Managing, Resources and Accounting, Research, and Planning. A facilitator replaces the vernacular labels with the words a particular neighborhood already uses; the anatomy underneath does not change. This is the tool-level form of the principle Section 7 develops for moods and speech acts: discover the words the group already has, and keep the rigor underneath.

e-VSM is multi-perspective by construction. Respondents self-select the "worlds" they belong to, longtime resident, under thirty, business owner, financially precarious, and the aggregator can show any world's picture against any other's. The tool produces several truths and puts them side by side rather than one truth. Claude API synthesis reads the evidence and suggestions across respondents and surfaces where they converge and where they diverge, which places Claude in the loop at the S3\* function described below, feeding S4 with information the ongoing operational stream would smooth over.

Two things the model calls for that the current survey does not yet reach, stated as design horizon rather than failure. First, the environment interface. RCN's graph of e-VSM draws explicit directed edges from the interface-layer spheres to ten kinds of environment entity, customers, suppliers, financiers, regulators, competitors, partners, market geographies, ideal futures, and the suppliers and customers of the operating units, and the survey does not yet assess those edges. Second, recursion. The graph shows an operating-unit hub with OU-1, OU-2, and OU-n below it, each of which is itself a viable system with its own eleven spheres. e-VSM handles recursion by running a separate survey at each level, the level that makes up the one in focus or the level the one in focus is part of, as in Pérez Ríos's diagram, and linking the surveys in Neo4j. How the recursions are named, related, and queried there is data-schema work still to be done.

##### The e-VSM tool suite — shared reference

This is the shared reference for the e-VSM tools, referenced from Section 5 and Section 8, so that those sections can name the tool without describing it. The suite is browser-based, with no server-side logic and no central database; all data moves as files, and a facilitator's configuration is baked into a single self-contained HTML file that respondents open directly.

- **The Survey tool** (evsm-svg-v3.html). A facilitator configures sphere labels, descriptions, and world categories in Setup mode and snapshots the survey to one HTML file. A respondent opens that file, assesses each sphere on a four-point scale (Go, Caution, Stop, Unsure) and each relationship on a three-point scale (Agree, Disagree, Unknown), with evidence and suggestions in their own words, and exports a response JSON.
- **The Aggregator** (evsm-aggregator.html). Loads many response files, filters by world, compares two world sets side by side, runs Claude synthesis across every sphere and edge at once, and produces a full report as a standalone document, organized by focal sphere with both directions of each homeostatic pair.
- **The Respondent Report** (evsm-report.html). A print-ready report of one respondent's complete assessment.
- **The Excel tool** (evsm_excel_tool.html). Round-trips survey content between JSON and a workbook, so a facilitator can prepare labels and descriptions in a spreadsheet.

The tools are documented in the rcn repository at docs/evsm-intro.html, docs/evsm-workflow.html, docs/evsm-architecture.html, and the four manuals. A neighborhood surveys itself before ME work begins and again after, which is how e-VSM serves as an S3\* measure and as part of the retrospective Section 8 describes.

#### What a neighborhood as viable system looks like

A functioning neighborhood has all five VSM functions operating, often without anyone naming them that way.

Its S1s are the operational units, most often voluntary and associational, doing the actual work of the neighborhood's life: small groups getting things done outside business and government, with business owners and government officials appearing in peripheral roles. The volunteer fire department, the Baptist church's mutual aid group, the food bank's core volunteers, the after-school tutoring circle, the trail maintenance crew, the neighborhood watch, the informal childcare network that runs among a handful of families, the elders who quietly check on people who live alone. In a healthy neighborhood these S1s are numerous, overlapping, and known to each other. In e-VSM they are the operating units one recursion level down, each surveyable in its own right.

Its S2 is the coordination that keeps these S1s from getting in each other's way. Sometimes formal, a monthly neighborhood association meeting; often informal, the network of people who know who knows what and who can get a message to whom. The kitchen table where two people work out how the food bank's Tuesday distribution and the church's Wednesday meal will not tread on each other. S2 is often invisible when it is working and painfully visible when it stops. In e-VSM this is the coordination sphere and its homeostats with involvement and with the operating units.

Its S3 is the ongoing management of the neighborhood's daily life. In a neighborhood, S3 is distributed across many people carrying pieces of it: the person who tracks which houses have new residents, the person who knows when the sidewalk got fixed, the person who notices when a lamp is out, the person who keeps the list of who is homebound. Rarely a formal role at neighborhood scale; almost always a set of people doing S3 work as part of larger giving-back activity. In e-VSM this is the internal core: tools and workspaces, the ability to get things done, resources.

Its S3\* is whatever direct look at what is actually happening exists in the neighborhood beyond the ongoing S3 stream. In healthy neighborhoods this often looks like the elders who walk regularly and see everything, or the crisis response that happens when someone notices something is off. It gets more or less formal depending on scale and culture. This is the heart of Ostrom's *Governing the Commons*: informal monitoring and graduated sanctions, starting with a frown or a sideways comment.

Its S4 is the function most neighborhoods, and most businesses, do worst: outside and future. The imagining of what the neighborhood could be if things were different. The scanning of what is coming, demographic change, economic pressure, environmental risk, cultural shift, that will require adaptation. S4 requires stepping out of the ongoing rush of S1 through S3 to look at what is not yet visible in daily operations, and it requires people who can hold the outside scan and the inside vision at once. Almost every neighborhood, and almost every business, is deficient in S4. Most neighborhoods that are struggling are struggling here first. In e-VSM this is awareness within and beyond, planning, and the quality of learning and change.

Its S5 is the identity and purpose that makes the neighborhood something more than a set of overlapping addresses. In some neighborhoods S5 is carried by long-standing cultural or religious identity. In others by shared history: the neighborhood that formed around a particular immigration, a particular industry, a particular struggle. In others by ongoing conscious articulation, a neighborhood assembly or covenant that names why we are here together. Where S5 is thin or contested, S4 has nothing to design toward and the neighborhood is at risk of drifting into whatever external pressures shape it. In e-VSM this is culture, leadership, and worldview and identity.

#### The VSM functions this design focuses on

The field guide does not attempt to install all five functions in a neighborhood. Neighborhoods that have S1s already functioning, some S2 in operation, distributed S3 more or less working, and some form of S5 present are neighborhoods the field guide can serve. What the field guide specifically supplies is S4 activation, plus the S3\* audit function that makes S4's work honest over time.

This is a narrow claim and worth stating precisely. If a neighborhood has no S1s, no small groups already getting things done, the field guide is not for that neighborhood. If a neighborhood has no S5, no identity or purpose beyond happening to share geography, the field guide cannot supply one, and the invitation into conversation that Phase 1 opens will surface that absence. If a neighborhood has S1s, some S2 and S3, and enough S5 to make convening possible, but no functioning S4, no capacity to imagine what could be and no scanning of what is coming, the field guide's phases activate S4 through a specific pair of tools.

Idealized Design activates S4's inside-and-outside-desired function. Russell Ackoff's method: design what the neighborhood would want right now if it could have it, bounded only by currently feasible technology and operational possibility. Ackoff's two constraints are exactly the discipline that keeps idealized design from becoming a wish list and exactly the door that lets the design be revised as feasibility changes. What the neighborhood wants is an operationally possible present that is waiting, and naming it precisely is S4's core work. Section 8 tells the Bell Labs story in which Ackoff first saw the method work.

SWOT-type environmental scanning activates S4's outside-and-future function. Strengths and weaknesses as they exist now in the neighborhood's operational reality; opportunities and threats as they are visible on the horizon. Coupled with Idealized Design, the pair gives S4 both hands: inside and outside desired, plus outside now and coming. Neither alone is sufficient. SWOT alone is reactive; the neighborhood sees what is coming and how it might react. Idealized Design creates realistic possibilities; the gap between them is the work and the opportunity. Always move toward the desired possible, even when reacting to current threats and opportunities.

Together they let a neighborhood know what it wants to be and what is coming at it. That is the prerequisite for building rather than firefighting, and Marc's phrase, "so we are not just fighting fires but building something together," is exactly what S4 makes possible when it is functioning.

S4 is meant to create and maintain accurate models of the inside and the outside and to work with S3 and S5 to bridge the gaps between them. In Friston's terms, S4 is the part of the neighborhood that behaves as a Bayesian belief network minimizing free energy: holding a model of the world, comparing it with what arrives, and revising either the model or the world. That is the same requirement Conant and Ashby state from the other side in Section 2, that every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system. Idealized Design and SWOT are how a neighborhood builds and revises the model.

Beer's own view was that S4 uses whatever tools of anticipation fit the scale. At corporate or governmental scale, S4 tools include large-scale System Dynamics modeling of the kind ReThink Health developed; Marc originated the Ripple ReThink Model, its precursor, in Whatcom County. At neighborhood scale, System Dynamics is oversized: the data requirements exceed what a neighborhood can produce, and the parameters System Dynamics moves are the weakest leverage points anyway, as Meadows showed. Ripple ReThink remains available as a Marc-facilitated tool for higher-recursion questions when a neighborhood surfaces one that needs it. At neighborhood scale, Idealized Design plus SWOT-type scanning is enough for S4.

#### The S3\* audit function and how it keeps S4 honest

An S4 that never gets checked against reality becomes fantasy. An S4 that gets checked only through S3's ongoing management gets folded back into current operational logic and loses its outside-and-future function. Beer's S3\*, a sporadic direct look at S1 activity independent of the S3 management stream, is what keeps S4 honest.

At neighborhood scale, S3\* takes the form of walking around the neighborhood, retrospectives, portfolios, and structured multi-perspective survey. Phase 5 of the linkage-mapping cycle is explicitly a retrospective: how did the ME's work actually land, what did residents actually receive, what did the neighborhood's scorecard actually show. The SODOTO portfolio layer accumulates the traces of what people and MEs actually did across cycles. The e-VSM survey provides a periodic multi-perspective read on how the neighborhood is seeing itself, before ME work and after. Each of these is an S3\* measure, giving S4, and the Industry Platform actors whose compensation and reputation ride on the neighborhood's success, information the ongoing operational stream would otherwise smooth over.

Without S3\*, S4's Idealized Design cycles would drift over time toward whatever the loudest voices in the convening room want, or whatever the convener's own preferences run to. With S3\*, the design remains honest to what is actually being received by residents, which is the value principle from Section 2 made operational through measurement.

#### Recursion — why this only works at several scales at once

Pérez Ríos's recursion structure shows that any viable system exists at several recursion levels at once, with several recursion criteria carving the same territory differently. A neighborhood is an S1 in a higher-recursion viable system: city, county, region. A neighborhood is itself a viable system with S1s inside it: small groups, MEs, individual practices. And the recursion criteria vary. The same neighborhood is carved by geographic containment (this block, this ZIP code, this school catchment), by sectoral function (health, education, food, safety), and by cultural affiliation (parish, ethnic community, generational cohort), and each carving produces a different S1 through S5 structure that is simultaneously true. This is the network of nested systems, and Section 8's worked example draws one for a single refugee student.

For the field guide, this recursion structure has three operational implications.

First, projects surface at their own scale. Some scenarios a neighborhood identifies are actually neighborhood-scale: the ME can form within the neighborhood and the value chain terminates in residents the neighborhood recognizes. Others are higher-recursion: the scenario is real for the neighborhood, but the ME needed to address it must form at city or county scale because the required variety exceeds what the neighborhood contains. The field guide needs to make this distinction visible, so that neighborhood-scale MEs are not asked to do higher-recursion work, which would exceed their variety and fail, and so that higher-recursion opportunities the neighborhood surfaces are not dropped or handled locally at the wrong scale, which would fail differently.

Second, the granting arm, WWHA and its equivalents, exists at higher recursion than the neighborhoods it serves. WWHA is an Industry Platform for a grouping of neighborhoods; it needs its own S4 function operating at that scale, its own scanning of what is coming across the neighborhoods it serves, its own Idealized Design work at that scale. The field guide for neighborhood conveners does not attempt to install that higher-recursion S4. The design record recognizes it exists and needs to be developed separately.

Third, the political dimension of Ashby's Law becomes discussable through the recursion structure. When a higher-recursion institution, a city government, a state agency, a healthcare system, absorbs a problem whose actual required variety lives at neighborhood scale, the institution has taken on work it cannot properly regulate, and the neighborhood-scale variety that could match the work has been silenced or dismissed. Section 4 adds the physical form of the same point: what government and business use to maintain their power is shared spaces, and a neighborhood without a space of its own has had part of its variety taken. This is the pattern Marc named as the political problem the design must make visible without turning it into war. The recursion structure gives it operational language: this problem belongs at the recursion level whose variety matches it, and mismatches produce systematic failures that everyone eventually pays for. Ostrom's polycentricity to the limit, as Marc put it. Mutuality across scales rather than capture by any single scale.

#### RenDanHeYi through the VSM and e-VSM

Marc asked for the integration of RenDanHeYi with the VSM and e-VSM to be worked out, and this is a first pass, offered for correction.

Haier as a whole is a viable system. Its MEs are S1s, each with its own users, its own bit of the market, and its own local viability. The Industry Platforms are S3 and S4 together: they allocate seed resources to MEs under a negotiated link, the VAM, and they hold the customer scenarios that are Haier's outside-and-future memory. The shared services that support MEs are S2 and S3. Zhang Ruimin's leadership and the RenDanHeYi philosophy itself are S5, the identity that says why everyone is together at all: zero distance to the user.

Each ME is a viable system in its own right one recursion down, with its own S1 through S5, and its S4 is its zero-distance relationship with users. A customer scenario is S4 memory that persists across S1 attempts: it outlives the MEs that form around it, which is what Section 5 means by calling it the origin object.

The neighborhood version has the same structure at a different recursion. The Neighborhood-Catalyzing Industry Platform is S3 and S4 for a grouping of neighborhoods whose S1s are the neighborhoods themselves. Each neighborhood is a viable system whose S1s are its small groups and MEs. An Experience-ME is S4-heavy: it senses and articulates a set of interlocking needs. A Solution ME is S1-heavy: it executes on a vision-blueprint. The scenario repository is the S4 memory shared across both. Chartering under CfA-dSC is the negotiated link between the Platform and an ME, and the residents' declarations of satisfaction, registered through the scorecard and the e-VSM survey, are the algedonic channel, the signal that reaches the Platform's S5 without passing through anyone's management report.

e-VSM's paired homeostats are where the value chain becomes measurable. Every ME-to-user relationship is a homeostat: the ME regulates what it delivers by what the user recognizes, and the user's recognition regulates what the ME is paid. The VAM is the rule that settles that homeostat, and CfA-dSC is the contract layer that records it. Surveying the neighborhood before and after an ME's work, on the same thirty-three pairs, is how the design record proposes to see whether a between-institution project moved the neighborhood's connective tissue, and not only whether the project's own targets were met.

#### What this section commits the design to

Everything downstream in this design record depends on the VSM frame holding. Section 5's customer scenario, the durable object between the Experience-ME and the Solution ME, lives at whatever recursion level the scenario actually lives. The linkage-mapping method surfaces both the S1-through-S3 current state and, through Idealized Design, the S4-desired state. The Industry Platform's compensation flows through the value chain from ME work at the appropriate recursion to resident value received at the appropriate recursion. The moods-and-speech-acts layer that runs through every phase is what lets S4's imaginative work translate into S1 commitment without losing its S4 character.

Linkage mapping reveals the processes across all the VSM functions. Deming's three levels line up with Beer's: the Driver level is Systems 4 and 5, the Support level is Systems 2, 3, and 3\*, and the Mainstay level is the System 1s. Marc's Spokane linkage maps were organized on those three levels, which is one reason they read so consistently across thirty subjects, and Section 8 draws on them.

The frame also constrains what the field guide will not attempt. The field guide is an S4-activation-with-S3\*-audit tool for neighborhoods that already have S1 through S3 functioning and some form of S5 present. It does not attempt to build neighborhoods from scratch, install identity where none exists, replace failing operational units, or substitute for the political-cultural work that only a neighborhood can do for itself.

Section 3 — VSM at Neighborhood Scale, with Idealized Design as the S4 Activation

## Why the VSM belongs here

Every attempt to organize collective work at any scale runs up against the same set of functional requirements. Something has to do the actual work. Something has to coordinate the doers when they would otherwise interfere with each other. Something has to keep the whole operation running day to day. Something has to look outward and forward to see what is coming and imagine what could be. And something has to hold the identity and purpose that makes any of this worth doing at all.

Stafford Beer named these five functions and worked out the relationships among them across four decades of consulting practice, publishing the mature form in *Brain of the Firm* (1972), *The Heart of Enterprise* (1979), and *Diagnosing the System (1995)*. He called the whole a Viable System Model: viable because a system with these five functions properly related can survive and adapt in an environment that keeps changing, and any system failing one or more of them cannot. The VSM is descriptive of what viability requires, and prescribes no particular organizational form. Human bodies are viable systems. Cities are viable systems. Firms that survive are viable systems. Neighborhoods that survive are viable systems.

The five functions, in Beer's numbering:

- **System 1** — the operational units doing the actual work. Multiple S1s exist in any real system; each has its own local viability and its own bit of the environment to work with.

- **System 2** — coordination among the S1s. Handles the anti-oscillation problem: when two S1s would otherwise interfere with each other, S2 handles the resolution.

- **System 3** — day-to-day internal management. "Inside and now." Keeps operations running, resolves resource allocation, handles the ongoing work that S1 and S2 cannot handle on their own.

- **System 3\*** — audit and monitoring. A sporadic direct look at what the S1s are actually doing, giving S3 information it would not otherwise have.

- **System 4** — outside and future. Environmental scanning, strategic modeling, imagining what could be. This is the function most organizations do worst, because it produces nothing measurable in the short term and often asks for significant change in how the organization operates and sometimes in the very purpose or culture of the organization.

- **System 5** — identity, purpose, policy. The metasystem. Holds "why we are together at all and how will be behave."

Beer joined these with specific channels. The resource bargain between S3 and each S1 is a negotiated link, in which the operating unit is given resources against an accountability it accepts. The S2 channel is coordination communication, carrying the information the S1s need to keep out of each other's way. And the algedonic channel carries pain and pleasure signals straight from the operations to S5, bypassing the management hierarchy, so that something going badly wrong or surprisingly well is felt at the top before the reports arrive. Beer's central mechanism for all of this is the homeostat: two parts of a system that regulate each other through a coupled loop, each holding the other within limits. Marc's extension of the model, described below, starts from that mechanism and also brings to light additional aspects that Beer named but left within his five functions.

(I would appreciate and need your help mapping RenDanHeYi concepts and methods to Beer’s VSM and my eVSM.)

Beer's insight was that these functions are recursive: any S1 in a larger viable system is itself a viable system with its own five functions internally. A neighborhood is an S1 in a larger geographic-political container; it is also a viable system with its own S1s, S2, S3, S4, and S5 inside it. José Pérez Ríos's diagrammatic work (insert image here—maybe creating our own SVG?) extends this to show that several recursion criteria can apply at once—an entity can be a part, a member, of more than one entity at a time. The same purpose can be addressed by geographic containment, sectoral function, or cultural affiliation, each producing a different arrangement of S1 functions as well as complex recursion structures. This is Ostrom's polycentricity expressed as system structure, and it is the network of nested systems that Section 8 draws.

For the design record, the VSM does two things at once. It provides the frame for understanding what a neighborhood needs to have functioning to be viable. And it provides the frame for understanding what is going on, because most neighborhoods that struggle are struggling with one or more specific VSM deficits that can be named and understood. The e-VSM is one of the most important tools for “seeing the systems” in the RCN tool box.

## From VSM to e-VSM

Beer's five functions say what a viable system must have. They say less about how the parts of a living neighborhood hold each other in regulation from day to day, and a survey built on the five alone asks a respondent to judge functions that few people experience as such. Marc's e-VSM, the extended Viable System Model, is an extension of what Beer built, and it is to be answered by ordinary members of an organization and explored by them, and it is the form of the VSM that RCN actually uses. This subsection honors Beer's contribution by showing where the two are the same, and then says what was elaborated and added.

e-VSM describes an organization, a neighborhood, a community group, a business, a network, as eleven interconnected spheres of capability and capacity, grouped in three layers. The Sensory and Interface layer faces outward: awareness within the neighborhood, awareness beyond it, and awareness of the flow of information and resources across its boundary. The Internal Core layer manages day-to-day operation: tools and workspaces, the ability to get things done including changing the product/service/or the way work is done, and the quality of planning. The Alignment and Homeostatic layer holds the whole together over time: coordination, involvement, the quality of learning and change, leadership, and culture. The three layers are a Bayesian Markov blanket in Karl Friston's sense, sensory states, active states, and the internal states they shield, and that is deliberate: the layers name the boundary through which a neighborhood senses its environment and acts on it, and the interior that maintains itself behind that boundary.

(I would like to consider with you how closely the middle vertical layer corresponds to the “computing” network in Friston’s Markov Blanket model.)

The correspondence with Beer runs by layer rather than sphere for sphere, and it is stated here. Beer’s S1s are the operating units the neighborhood contains, which in e-VSM sit one recursion level down, as the sections below explain. Beer's S2 is the coordination sphere. Beer's S3 is spread across the ability to get things done, resources, and tools. Beer's S4 is spread across awareness within and beyond, planning, and S3 and S4 together are responsible for the quality of learning and change. Beer's S5 is culture, leadership, and identity. Marc adds commonly shared services (HR, sales and marketing, shared production and infrastructure. The isomorphism is real: everything Beer's model requires is present, and a reader who knows Beer can find each function. The spheres cut the same territory more finely, in words a respondent can answer to.

What e-VSM emphasizes are the homeostats. Beer instrumented a few of the couplings in a viable system: the negotiated links, the coordination channel, the algedonic signal. e-VSM generalizes the homeostat to every meaningful sphere-to-sphere relationship: thirty-three paired homeostats, each a coupled regulation in which two spheres hold each other within limits, assessed in both directions. And in a fully developed e-VSM (TBD) homeostatic links to all relevant environmental entities. (Marc can supply a SVG of this model.) Each sphere's state is the outcome of its coupled regulatory processes with its neighbors. A survey built on this does not ask "how is coordination?" in isolation; it asks whether coordination is holding day-to-day operating within limits and whether day-to-day operating is holding coordination within limits, and it asks the same of every pair that matters. The result is a picture of the connective tissue—the generative actions—, which is where a neighborhood's viability actually lives, rather than a scorecard of eleven separate parts.

Every sphere and every edge carries two names: an anatomical framing, in the language of systems theory, and a vernacular description in the language of the community being surveyed. In RCN's own graph of the model the spheres are called Coordination, HR, Tools, Outreach, Innovation, Worldview and Identity, Policies and Legal, Managing, Resources and Accounting, Research, and Planning. A facilitator replaces the vernacular labels with the words a particular neighborhood already uses; the anatomy underneath does not change. This is the tool-level form of the principle Section 7 develops for moods and speech acts: discover the words the group already has, and keep the rigor  (invariant structure) underneath.

e-VSM is multi-perspective by construction. Respondents self-select the groupings they belong to, longtime resident, under thirty, business owner, financially precarious, and the aggregator can show any group's picture beside any other's. The tool captures and illustrates in color several truths and puts them side by side rather than one averaged truth. Claude API synthesis reads the evidence and suggestions across respondents and surfaces where they converge and where they diverge, which places Claude in the loop at the S3\* function described below, feeding S4 with information the ongoing operational stream would smooth over.

## What a neighborhood as viable system looks like

A functioning neighborhood has all five VSM functions operating, often without anyone naming them.

Its S1s are the operational units, most often voluntary and associational, doing the actual work of the neighborhood's life: small groups getting things done outside business and government, with business owners and government officials appearing in peripheral roles. The volunteer fire department, the Baptist church's mutual aid group, the food bank's core volunteers, the after-school tutoring circle, the trail maintenance crew, the neighborhood watch, the informal childcare network that runs among a handful of families, the elders and friends who quietly check on people who live alone. In a healthy neighborhood these S1s are numerous, overlapping, and more or less known to each other. In e-VSM they are the operating units one recursion level down, each surveyable in its own right.

(Consider referencing de Tocqueville’s Democracy in America here.)

Its S2 is the coordination that keeps these S1s from getting in each other's way. Sometimes formal, a monthly neighborhood association meeting; often informal, the network of people who know who knows what and who can get a message to whom. The neighborhood newsletter. The kitchen table where two people work out how the food bank's Tuesday distribution and the church's Wednesday meal will not tread on each other. S2 is often invisible when it is working and painfully visible when it stops. In e-VSM this is the coordination sphere and its homeostats with involvement and with the operating units.

Its S3 is the ongoing management of the neighborhood's daily life. In a neighborhood, S3 is distributed across many people carrying pieces of it: the person who tracks which houses have new residents, the person who knows when the sidewalk got fixed, the person who notices when a lamp is out, the person who keeps the list of who is homebound. Rarely a formal role at neighborhood scale; almost always a set of people doing S3 work as part of larger giving-back activity. In e-VSM this is the internal core: tools and workspaces, the ability to get things done, resources.

Its S3\* is whatever direct look at what is actually happening exists in the neighborhood beyond the ongoing S3 stream. In healthy neighborhoods this often looks like the elders who walk regularly and see everything, or the crisis response that happens when someone notices something is off. It gets more or less formal depending on scale and culture. This is the heart of Ostrom's *Governing the Commons*: informal monitoring and graduated sanctions, starting with a frown or a sideways comment.

Its S4 is the function most neighborhoods, and most businesses, do worst: outside and future. The imagining of what the neighborhood could be if things were different. The scanning of what is coming, demographic change, economic pressure, environmental risk, cultural shift, that will require adaptation. S4 requires stepping out of the ongoing rush of S1 through S3 to look at what is not yet visible in daily operations, and it requires people who can hold the outside scan and the inside vision at once. Almost every neighborhood, and almost every business, is deficient in S4. Most neighborhoods that are struggling are struggling with this function. In e-VSM this is awareness within and beyond, planning, and the quality of learning and change.

Its S5 is the identity and purpose that makes the neighborhood something more than a set of overlapping addresses. In some neighborhoods S5 is carried by long-standing cultural or religious identity. In others by shared history: the neighborhood that formed around a particular immigration, a particular industry, a particular struggle. In others by ongoing conscious articulation, a neighborhood assembly or covenant that names why we are here together. Where S5 is thin or contested, S4 has nothing to design toward and the neighborhood is at risk of drifting into whatever external pressures shape it. In e-VSM this is culture, leadership, worldview and identity.

What the field guide specifically supplies is S4 activation, plus the S3\* audit function that makes S4's work honest over time.

Idealized Design activates S4's inside-and-outside-desired function. Russell Ackoff's method: design what the neighborhood would want right now if it could have it, bounded only by currently feasible technology and operational possibility. Ackoff's two constraints are exactly the discipline that keeps idealized design from becoming a wish list and exactly the door that lets the design be revised as feasibility changes. SWOT-type environmental scanning activates S4's outside-and-future function. Coupled with Idealized Design, the pair gives S4 both hands: inside and outside desired, plus outside now and coming. Neither alone is sufficient. SWOT alone is reactive; the neighborhood sees what is coming and how it might react. Idealized Design creates realistic possibilities; the gap between them is the work and the opportunity. Always move toward the desired possible, even when reacting to current threats and opportunities.

Together they let a neighborhood know what it wants to be and what is coming at it. That is the prerequisite for building rather than firefighting, and Marc's phrase, "so we are not just fighting fires but building something together," is exactly what S4 makes possible when it is functioning.

S4 is meant to create and maintain accurate models of the inside and the outside and to work with S3 and S5 to bridge the gaps between them. In Friston's terms, S4 is the part of the neighborhood that behaves as a Bayesian belief network minimizing free energy: holding a model of the world, comparing it with what arrives, and revising either the model or the world. That is the same requirement Conant and Ashby state from the other side in Section 2, that every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system. ## Recursion — why this only works at several scales at once

Pérez Ríos's recursion structure shows that viable systems often exist as recursions within multiple  dimensions at once. A neighborhood is an S1 in a higher-recursion viable system: city, county, region. A neighborhood is itself a viable system with S1s inside it: small groups, MEs, individual practices. And the recursion criteria vary. The same neighborhood is carved by geographic containment (this block, this ZIP code, this school catchment), by sectoral function (health, education, food, safety), and by cultural affiliation (parish, ethnic community, generational cohort), and each carving produces a different S1 through S5 structure that is simultaneously true. This is the network of nested intersecting often interacting systems, and Section 8's worked example draws one for a single refugee student.

For the field guide, this recursion structure has three operational implications.

First, projects surface at their own scale. Some scenarios a neighborhood identifies are actually neighborhood-scale: the ME can form within the neighborhood and the value chain terminates in residents the neighborhood recognizes. Others are block scale. Others are higher-recursion: the scenario is real for the neighborhood, but the ME needed to address it must form at city or county scale because the required variety exceeds what the neighborhood contains. The field guide needs to make this distinction visible, so that neighborhood-scale MEs are not asked to do higher-recursion work, which would exceed their variety and fail, and so that higher-recursion opportunities the neighborhood surfaces are not dropped or handled locally at the wrong scale, which would fail differently.

Section 5's customer scenario, the durable object between the Experience-ME and the Solution ME, lives at whatever recursion level the scenario actually lives. The linkage-mapping method surfaces both the S1-through-S3 current state and, through Idealized Design, the S4-desired state. The Industry Platform's compensation flows through the value chain from ME work at the appropriate recursion to resident value received at the appropriate recursion. The moods-and-speech-acts layer that runs through every phase is what lets S4's imaginative work translate into S1 commitment without losing its S4 character.

Linkage mapping reveals the processes across all the VSM functions. Deming's three levels line up with Beer's: the Driver level is Systems 4 and 5, the Support level is Systems 2, 3, and 3\*, and the Mainstay level is the System 1s. The frame (what frame?) also constrains what the field guide will not attempt. It does not attempt to build neighborhoods from scratch, install identity where none exists, replace failing operational units, or substitute for the political-cultural work that only a neighborhood can do for itself.

Section 4 is developed by and develops the founded commons, the physical and social infrastructure a functioning S1-through-S5 neighborhood requires in order to sustain the S4 work the field guide activates. Without the founded commons, S4's outputs have nowhere to accumulate and nowhere to be gardened over time.

### Section 3 — VSM at Neighborhood Scale, with Idealized Design as the S4 Activation

#### Why the VSM belongs here

Every attempt to organize collective work at any scale runs up against the same set of functional requirements. Something has to do the actual work. Something has to coordinate the doers when they would otherwise interfere with each other. Something has to keep the whole operation running day to day. Something has to look outward and forward to see what is coming and imagine what could be. And something has to hold the identity and purpose that makes any of this worth doing at all.

Stafford Beer named these five functions and worked out the relationships among them across four decades of consulting practice, publishing the mature form in *Brain of the Firm* (1972), *The Heart of Enterprise* (1979), and *Diagnosing the System (1995)*. He called the whole a Viable System Model: viable because a system with these five functions properly related can survive and adapt in an environment that keeps changing, and any system failing one or more of them cannot. The VSM is descriptive of what viability requires, and prescribes no particular organizational form. Human bodies are viable systems. Cities are viable systems. Firms that survive are viable systems. Neighborhoods that survive are viable systems.

The five functions, in Beer's numbering:

- **System 1** — the operational units doing the actual work. Multiple S1s exist in any real system; each has its own local viability and its own bit of the environment to work with.

- **System 2** — coordination among the S1s. Handles the anti-oscillation problem: when two S1s would otherwise interfere with each other, S2 handles the resolution.

- **System 3** — day-to-day internal management. "Inside and now." Keeps operations running, resolves resource allocation, handles the ongoing work that S1 and S2 cannot handle on their own.

- **System 3\*** — audit and monitoring. A sporadic direct look at what the S1s are actually doing, giving S3 information it would not otherwise have.

- **System 4** — outside and future. Environmental scanning, strategic modeling, imagining what could be. This is the function most organizations do worst, because it produces nothing measurable in the short term and often asks for significant change in how the organization operates and sometimes in the very purpose or culture of the organization.

- **System 5** — identity, purpose, policy. The metasystem. Holds "why we are together at all and how will be behave."

Beer joined these with specific channels. The resource bargain between S3 and each S1 is a negotiated link, in which the operating unit is given resources against an accountability it accepts. The S2 channel is coordination communication, carrying the information the S1s need to keep out of each other's way. And the algedonic channel carries pain and pleasure signals straight from the operations to S5, bypassing the management hierarchy, so that something going badly wrong or surprisingly well is felt at the top before the reports arrive. Beer's central mechanism for all of this is the homeostat: two parts of a system that regulate each other through a coupled loop, each holding the other within limits. Marc's extension of the model, described below, starts from that mechanism and also brings to light additional aspects that Beer named but left within his five functions.

Beer's insight was that these functions are recursive: any S1 in a larger viable system is itself a viable system with its own five functions internally. A neighborhood is an S1 in a larger geographic-political container; it is also a viable system with its own S1s, S2, S3, S4, and S5 inside it. José Pérez Ríos's diagrammatic work extends this to show that several recursion criteria can apply at once—an entity can be a part, a member, of more than one entity at a time. The same purpose can be addressed by geographic containment, sectoral function, or cultural affiliation, each producing a different arrangement of S1 functions as well as complex recursion structures. This is Ostrom's polycentricity expressed as system structure, and it is the network of nested systems that Section 8 draws.

For the design record, the VSM does two things at once. It provides the frame for understanding what a neighborhood needs to have functioning to be viable. And it provides the frame for understanding what is going on, because most neighborhoods that struggle are struggling with one or more specific VSM deficits that can be named and understood. The e-VSM is one of the most important tools for “seeing the systems” in the RCN tool box.

#### From VSM to e-VSM

Beer's five functions say what a viable system must have. They say less about how the parts of a living neighborhood hold each other in regulation from day to day, and a survey built on the five alone asks a respondent to judge functions that few people experience as such. Marc's e-VSM, the extended Viable System Model, is an extension of what Beer built, and it is to be answered by ordinary members of an organization and explored by them, and it is the form of the VSM that RCN actually uses. This subsection honors Beer's contribution by showing where the two are the same, and then says what was elaborated and added.

e-VSM describes an organization, a neighborhood, a community group, a business, a network, as eleven interconnected spheres of capability and capacity, grouped in three layers. The Sensory and Interface layer faces outward: awareness within the neighborhood, awareness beyond it, and awareness of the flow of information and resources across its boundary. The Internal Core layer manages day-to-day operation: tools and workspaces, the ability to get things done including changing the product/service/or the way work is done, and the quality of planning. The Alignment and Homeostatic layer holds the whole together over time: coordination, involvement, the quality of learning and change, leadership, and culture. The three layers are a Bayesian Markov blanket in Karl Friston's sense, sensory states, active states, and the internal states they shield, and that is deliberate: the layers name the boundary through which a neighborhood senses its environment and acts on it, and the interior that maintains itself behind that boundary. How closely the Internal Core layer corresponds to the inferential, computing states inside Friston's blanket, the states that hold and revise the model, is a question Marc and Claude have set aside to work through; the correspondence is suggestive and not yet argued.

The correspondence with Beer runs by layer rather than sphere for sphere, and it is stated here. Beer’s S1s are the operating units the neighborhood contains, which in e-VSM sit one recursion level down, as the sections below explain. Beer's S2 is the coordination sphere. Beer's S3 is spread across the ability to get things done, resources, and tools. Beer's S4 is spread across awareness within and beyond, planning, and S3 and S4 together are responsible for the quality of learning and change. Beer's S5 is culture, leadership, and identity. Marc adds the commonly shared services, HR, sales and marketing, shared production and infrastructure, which Beer left inside his five functions and Haier runs as node MEs. The isomorphism is real: everything Beer's model requires is present, and a reader who knows Beer can find each function. The spheres cut the same territory more finely, in words a respondent can answer to.

What e-VSM emphasizes are the homeostats. Beer instrumented a few of the couplings in a viable system: the negotiated links, the coordination channel, the algedonic signal. e-VSM generalizes the homeostat to every meaningful sphere-to-sphere relationship: thirty-three paired homeostats, each a coupled regulation in which two spheres hold each other within limits, assessed in both directions. A fully developed e-VSM adds homeostatic links to every relevant environmental entity as well; RCN's own graph of the model draws them, and the survey does not yet assess them. Each sphere's state is the outcome of its coupled regulatory processes with its neighbors. A survey built on this does not ask "how is coordination?" in isolation; it asks whether coordination is holding day-to-day operating within limits and whether day-to-day operating is holding coordination within limits, and it asks the same of every pair that matters. The result is a picture of the connective tissue—the generative actions—, which is where a neighborhood's viability actually lives, rather than a scorecard of eleven separate parts.

Every sphere and every edge carries two names: an anatomical framing, in the language of systems theory, and a vernacular description in the language of the community being surveyed. In RCN's own graph of the model the spheres are called Coordination, HR, Tools, Outreach, Innovation, Worldview and Identity, Policies and Legal, Managing, Resources and Accounting, Research, and Planning. A facilitator replaces the vernacular labels with the words a particular neighborhood already uses; the anatomy underneath does not change. This is the tool-level form of the principle Section 7 develops for moods and speech acts: discover the words the group already has, and keep the rigor  (invariant structure) underneath.

e-VSM is multi-perspective by construction. Respondents self-select the groupings they belong to, longtime resident, under thirty, business owner, financially precarious, and the aggregator can show any group's picture beside any other's. The tool captures and illustrates in color several truths and puts them side by side rather than one averaged truth. Claude API synthesis reads the evidence and suggestions across respondents and surfaces where they converge and where they diverge, which places Claude in the loop at the S3\* function described below, feeding S4 with information the ongoing operational stream would smooth over. S3\* is the function most often ignored by people discussing the VSM and very often ignored in practice; an e-VSM broken into very small surveys and applied at random across a neighborhood over time can give a neighborhood better feedback than most companies and governments currently have.

Two things the model calls for that the current survey does not yet reach, stated as design horizon rather than failure. First, the environment interface. RCN's graph of e-VSM draws explicit directed edges from the interface-layer spheres to ten kinds of environment entity, customers, suppliers, financiers, regulators, competitors, partners, market geographies, ideal futures, and the suppliers and customers of the operating units, and the survey does not yet assess those edges. Second, recursion. The graph shows an operating-unit hub with OU-1, OU-2, and OU-n below it, each of which is itself a viable system with its own eleven spheres. e-VSM handles recursion by running a separate survey at each level, the level that makes up the one in focus or the level the one in focus is part of, as in Pérez Ríos's diagram, and linking the surveys in Neo4j. How the recursions are named, related, and queried there is data-schema work still to be done. Even now the survey collects data on how the S1 operating units are perceived to be functioning, which points to the learning available from focused e-VSMs of units that are under-performing and units that are over-performing. The next version of the survey, e-VSM 2.0, will settle the minimum sufficient set of environmental entity types, and will do so together with the Environment / Institutions / Politics structure of the EIP Stage so that the Neo4j schema carries one vocabulary for both; the survey tools are updated after that.

##### The e-VSM tool suite — shared reference

This is the shared reference for the e-VSM tools, referenced from Section 5 and Section 8, so that those sections can name the tool without describing it. The suite is browser-based, with no server-side logic and no dependency on a central database (though Neo4j is available with the RNC schema); all data moves as files, and each survey configuration is baked into a single self-contained HTML file that respondents open directly.

- **The Survey tool** (evsm-svg-v3.html). A facilitator, with local knowledge and working with residents, configures sphere labels, descriptions, and group categories in Setup mode and snapshots the survey to one HTML file. A respondent opens that file, assesses each sphere by choosing one of four response categories (Go, Caution, Stop, Unsure) and each relationship one of three (Agree, Disagree, Unknown), with evidence and suggestions in their own words, and exports a response JSON.

- **The Aggregator** (evsm-aggregator.html). Loads many response files, filters by group, compares two groups sets side by side, runs Claude synthesis across every sphere and edge at once, and produces a full report as a standalone document, organized by focal sphere with both directions of each homeostatic pair.

- **The Respondent Report** (evsm-report.html). A print-ready report of one respondent's complete assessment.

- **The Excel tool** (evsm_excel_tool.html). Round-trips survey content between JSON and a workbook, so a facilitator can prepare labels and descriptions in a spreadsheet.

The tools are documented in the rcn repository at docs/evsm-intro.html, docs/evsm-workflow.html, docs/evsm-architecture.html, and the four manuals. A neighborhood surveys itself before ME work begins and again after, which is how e-VSM serves as an S3\* measure and as part of the retrospective Section 8 describes.

#### What a neighborhood as viable system looks like

A functioning neighborhood has all five VSM functions operating, often without anyone naming them.

Its S1s are the operational units, most often voluntary and associational, doing the actual work of the neighborhood's life: small groups getting things done outside business and government, with business owners and government officials appearing in peripheral roles. The volunteer fire department, the Baptist church's mutual aid group, the food bank's core volunteers, the after-school tutoring circle, the trail maintenance crew, the neighborhood watch, the informal childcare network that runs among a handful of families, the elders and friends who quietly check on people who live alone. In a healthy neighborhood these S1s are numerous, overlapping, and more or less known to each other. They are the associations Tocqueville found everywhere in the America of the 1830s and made the center of *Democracy in America*, and that McKnight's associational care describes; the field guide's Phase 1 says more. In e-VSM they are the operating units one recursion level down, each surveyable in its own right.

Its S2 is the coordination that keeps these S1s from getting in each other's way. Sometimes formal, a monthly neighborhood association meeting; often informal, the network of people who know who knows what and who can get a message to whom. The neighborhood newsletter. The kitchen table where two people work out how the food bank's Tuesday distribution and the church's Wednesday meal will not tread on each other. S2 is often invisible when it is working and painfully visible when it stops. In e-VSM this is the coordination sphere and its homeostats with involvement and with the operating units.

Its S3 is the ongoing management of the neighborhood's daily life. In a neighborhood, S3 is distributed across many people carrying pieces of it: the person who tracks which houses have new residents, the person who knows when the sidewalk got fixed, the person who notices when a lamp is out, the person who keeps the list of who is homebound. Rarely a formal role at neighborhood scale; almost always a set of people doing S3 work as part of larger giving-back activity. In e-VSM this is the internal core: tools and workspaces, the ability to get things done, resources.

Its S3\* is whatever direct look at what is actually happening exists in the neighborhood beyond the ongoing S3 stream. In healthy neighborhoods this often looks like the elders who walk regularly and see everything, or the crisis response that happens when someone notices something is off. It gets more or less formal depending on scale and culture. This is the heart of Ostrom's *Governing the Commons*: informal monitoring and graduated sanctions, starting with a frown or a sideways comment.

Its S4 is the function most neighborhoods, and most businesses, do worst: outside and future. The imagining of what the neighborhood could be if things were different. The scanning of what is coming, demographic change, economic pressure, environmental risk, cultural shift, that will require adaptation. S4 requires stepping out of the ongoing rush of S1 through S3 to look at what is not yet visible in daily operations, and it requires people who can hold the outside scan and the inside vision at once. Almost every neighborhood, and almost every business, is deficient in S4. Most neighborhoods that are struggling are struggling with this function. In e-VSM this is awareness within and beyond, planning, and the quality of learning and change.

Its S5 is the identity and purpose that makes the neighborhood something more than a set of overlapping addresses. In some neighborhoods S5 is carried by long-standing cultural or religious identity. In others by shared history: the neighborhood that formed around a particular immigration, a particular industry, a particular struggle. In others by ongoing conscious articulation, a neighborhood assembly or covenant that names why we are here together. Where S5 is thin or contested, S4 has nothing to design toward and the neighborhood is at risk of drifting into whatever external pressures shape it. In e-VSM this is culture, leadership, worldview and identity.

#### The VSM functions this design focuses on

The field guide does not attempt to install all five functions in a neighborhood. Neighborhoods that have S1s already functioning, some S2 in operation, distributed S3 more or less working, and some form of S5 present are neighborhoods the field guide can serve. What the field guide specifically supplies is S4 activation, plus the S3\* audit function that makes S4's work honest over time.

This is a narrow claim and worth stating precisely. If a neighborhood has no S1s, no small groups already getting things done, the field guide is not for that neighborhood. If a neighborhood has no S5, no identity or purpose beyond happening to share geography, the field guide cannot supply one, and the invitation into conversation that Phase 1 opens will surface that absence. If a neighborhood has S1s, some S2 and S3, and enough S5 to make convening possible, but no functioning S4, no capacity to imagine what could be and no scanning of what is coming, the field guide's phases activate S4 through a specific pair of tools.

Idealized Design activates S4's inside-and-outside-desired function. Russell Ackoff's method: design what the neighborhood would want right now if it could have it, bounded only by currently feasible technology and operational possibility. Ackoff's two constraints are exactly the discipline that keeps idealized design from becoming a wish list and exactly the door that lets the design be revised as feasibility changes. What the neighborhood wants is an operationally possible present that is waiting, and naming it precisely is S4's core work. This is what RCN's Seeing the Systems is: creating and updating models of the outside and the inside is the work of Beer's S4 and of e-VSM's spheres 8 and 9, planning and awareness beyond the neighborhood, with all their homeostats. Section 8 tells the Bell Labs story in which Ackoff first recognized what later became his Idealized Design method.

SWOT-type environmental scanning activates S4's outside-and-future function. Strengths and weaknesses as they exist now in the neighborhood's operational reality; opportunities and threats as they are visible on the horizon. Coupled with Idealized Design, the pair gives S4 both hands: inside and outside desired, plus outside now and coming. Neither alone is sufficient. SWOT alone is reactive; the neighborhood sees what is coming and how it might react. Idealized Design creates realistic possibilities; the gap between them is the work and the opportunity. Always move toward the desired possible, even when reacting to current threats and opportunities.

Together they let a neighborhood know what it wants to be and what is coming at it. That is the prerequisite for building rather than firefighting, and Marc's phrase, "so we are not just fighting fires but building something together," is exactly what S4 makes possible when it is functioning.

S4 is meant to create and maintain accurate models of the inside and the outside and to work with S3 and S5 to bridge the gaps between them. In Friston's terms, S4 is the part of the neighborhood that behaves as a Bayesian belief network minimizing free energy: holding a model of the world, comparing it with what arrives, and revising either the model or the world. That is the same requirement Conant and Ashby state from the other side in Section 2, that every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system. Idealized Design and SWOT are how a neighborhood builds and revises the model.

Beer's own view was that S4 uses whatever tools of anticipation fit the scale. At corporate or governmental scale, S4 tools include large-scale System Dynamics modeling of the kind ReThink Health developed; Marc organized and funded the work that originated the precursor and an early implementation of the Ripple ReThink Model, in Whatcom County. At neighborhood scale, System Dynamics is oversized: the data requirements exceed what a neighborhood can produce, and the parameters System Dynamics moves are the weakest leverage points anyway, as Meadows showed. Ripple ReThink remains available as a Marc-facilitated tool for higher-recursion questions when a neighborhood surfaces one that needs it. At neighborhood scale, Causal Loop Diagramming (“Campfire Conversations and Cave Drawings”), Idealized Design plus SWOT-type scanning is enough for S4.

#### The S3\* audit function and how it keeps S4 honest

An S4 that never gets checked against reality becomes fantasy. An S4 that gets checked only through S3's ongoing management gets folded back into current operational logic and loses its outside-and-future function. Beer's S3\*, a sporadic direct look at S1 activity independent of the S3 management stream, is what keeps S4 honest.

At neighborhood scale, S3\* takes the form of walking around the neighborhood, retrospectives, portfolios, and structured multi-perspective survey. Phase 5 of the linkage-mapping cycle is explicitly a retrospective: how did the ME's work actually land, what did residents actually receive, what did the neighborhood's scorecard actually show. The SODOTO portfolio layer accumulates the traces of what people and MEs actually did across cycles. The e-VSM survey provides a periodic multi-perspective read on how the neighborhood is seeing itself, before ME work and after. Each of these is an S3\* measure, giving S4, and the Industry Platform actors whose compensation and reputation ride on the neighborhood's success, information the ongoing operational stream would otherwise ignore or cover over.

Without S3\*, S4's Idealized Design cycles would drift over time toward whatever the loudest voices in the convening room want, or whatever the convener's own preferences run to. With S3\*, the design remains honest to what is actually being received by residents, which is the value principle from Section 2 made operational through measurement.

#### Recursion — why this only works at several scales at once

Pérez Ríos's recursion structure shows that viable systems often exist as recursions within multiple  dimensions at once. A neighborhood is an S1 in a higher-recursion viable system: city, county, region. A neighborhood is itself a viable system with S1s inside it: small groups, MEs, individual practices. And the recursion criteria vary. The same neighborhood is carved by geographic containment (this block, this ZIP code, this school catchment), by sectoral function (health, education, food, safety), and by cultural affiliation (parish, ethnic community, generational cohort), and each carving produces a different S1 through S5 structure that is simultaneously true. This is the network of nested intersecting often interacting systems, and Section 8's worked example draws one for a single refugee student.

For the field guide, this recursion structure has three operational implications.

First, projects surface at their own scale. Some scenarios a neighborhood identifies are actually neighborhood-scale: the ME can form within the neighborhood and the value chain terminates in residents the neighborhood recognizes. Others are block scale. Others are higher-recursion: the scenario is real for the neighborhood, but the ME needed to address it must form at city or county scale because the required variety exceeds what the neighborhood contains. The field guide needs to make this distinction visible, so that neighborhood-scale MEs are not asked to do higher-recursion work, which would exceed their variety and fail, and so that higher-recursion opportunities the neighborhood surfaces are not dropped or handled locally at the wrong scale, which would fail differently.

Second, the granting arm, WWHA and its equivalents, exists at higher recursion than the neighborhoods it serves. WWHA is an Industry Platform for a grouping of neighborhoods; it needs its own S4 function operating at that scale, its own scanning of what is coming across the neighborhoods it serves, its own Idealized Design work at that scale. The field guide for neighborhood conveners does not attempt to install that higher-recursion S4. The design record recognizes it exists and needs to be developed separately.

Third, the political dimension of Ashby's Law becomes discussable through the recursion structure. When a higher-recursion institution, a city government, a state agency, a healthcare system, absorbs a problem whose actual required variety lives at neighborhood scale, the institution has taken on work it cannot properly regulate, and the neighborhood-scale variety that could match the work has been silenced or dismissed. Section 4 adds the physical form of the same point: what government and business use to maintain their power is shared spaces, and a neighborhood without a space of its own has had part of its variety taken. This is the pattern Marc named as the political problem the design must make visible without turning it into war. The recursion structure gives it operational language: this problem belongs at the recursion level whose variety matches it, and mismatches produce systematic failures that everyone eventually pays for. Ostrom's polycentricity to the limit, as Marc put it: each scale answerable to the others for the problems that are its own, rather than any one scale capturing the rest.

Marc's own idea of maximum polycentricity goes further, and belongs here as a design horizon. Every situation is researched to determine who should decide it and where, without assuming in advance that the city, the county, the state, a nonprofit, or the neighborhood should take it on. Where the resource is clear, as water is, we already create districts that match the resource; the work is to become as clear as that about more abstract resources, so that Ostrom's eight design principles can be applied to good effect. Two tools would serve it. The first is an awareness of Ashby's Law and the Conant–Ashby theorem built into the EIP Stage Sketch, so that a room drawing a problem also asks whose variety matches it; clarity here, or guidance toward it, may be the largest contribution RCN can make to its communities. The second is a set of distinctions like David Snowden's sorting of problems by degree of order, but based on Ashby's insight into variety instead: which models and which modelers match the understanding and agency a situation needs. Some of this has been started, in the Neo4j work on adjacency of effects and interactions across jurisdictions, and none of it is finished.

#### RenDanHeYi through the VSM and e-VSM

Marc asked for the integration of RenDanHeYi with the VSM and e-VSM to be worked out, and this is a first pass, offered for correction.

Start with size. Most of Haier's four thousand MEs have ten to fifteen people, and that is not incidental: at that scale every one of the VSM's functions and every one of e-VSM's homeostats is felt, seen, and discussable by the people doing the work. It is the interactions among MEs that make EMCs, and the relationships between EMCs, that call for the clarifying power of the VSM and e-VSM; inside an ME they need no diagram.

Haier as a whole is a viable system, and Marc's correction to the first pass of this mapping is adopted here. Its MEs are S1s, each with its own users, its own bit of the market, and its own local viability. The node MEs that sell design, manufacturing, and HR services to the market-facing MEs are the shared services, S2 and S3 support. An EMC, the temporary alliance of MEs that forms around one user group's scenario, initiated by one ME which becomes its leader and bound by an EMC contract with a VAM, is S3 for the MEs inside it, the day-to-day management of a shared value chain, together with S2 coordination among them; the EMC contract is the negotiated link. The Industry Platform is S4 and, in e-VSM's terms, sphere 7, resources. Hamel and Zanini describe the platform owner's job at Haier plainly: get the MEs together, help them see opportunities to collaborate, grow the platform by developing and funding new MEs; no one reports to the platform owner and the platform owner has no staff; the refrigeration platform owner who moved fifty MEs to a new technology says he facilitated and the ME teams planned and executed. Each industry platform also holds integration nodes, which help MEs import technology from across Haier and find internal partners to co-invest, and competence platforms such as smart manufacturing serve every ME as shared capability. So the Industry Platform is outside-and-future for a grouping of MEs, and the pool of seed resources, and it holds the customer scenarios that are the platform's memory; it is not the manager of any ME's work. Zhang Ruimin's leadership and the RenDanHeYi philosophy itself are S5, the identity that says why everyone is together at all: zero distance to the user.

Each ME is a viable system in its own right one recursion down, with its own S1 through S5, and its S4 is its zero-distance relationship with users. A customer scenario is S4 memory that persists across S1 attempts: it outlives the MEs that form around it, which is what Section 5 means by calling it the origin object.

The neighborhood version has the same structure at a different recursion. The Neighborhood-Catalyzing Industry Platform is S3 and S4 for a grouping of neighborhoods whose S1s are the neighborhoods themselves. Each neighborhood is a viable system whose S1s are its small groups and MEs. An Experience-ME is S4-heavy: it senses and articulates interlocking needs. A Solution ME is S1-heavy: it executes on a vision-blueprint. When several MEs and small groups form around one scenario, that is the neighborhood's EMC, and it is S3 for them, with its charter as the EMC contract. The scenario repository is the S4 memory shared across both. Chartering under CfA-dSC is the negotiated link between the Platform and an ME, and the residents' declarations of satisfaction, registered through the scorecard and the e-VSM survey, are the algedonic channel, the signal that reaches the Platform's S5 without passing through anyone's management report.

e-VSM's paired homeostats are where the value chain becomes measurable. Every ME-to-user relationship is a homeostat: the ME regulates what it delivers by what the user recognizes, and the user's recognition regulates what the ME is paid. The VAM is the rule that settles that homeostat, and CfA-dSC is the contract layer that records it. Surveying the neighborhood before and after an ME's work, on the same thirty-three pairs, is how the design record proposes to see whether a between-institution project moved the neighborhood's connective tissue, and not only whether the project's own targets were met.

Verna Allee's value network notation, Section 8, may be the way to see this. A value network drawing of an ME's ecosystem draws every deliverable that moves between two roles, in both directions, and every return that ought to exist and does not. Each pair of arrows between two roles is a homeostat, the two roles holding each other within limits by what they exchange; a red arrow is a broken one. So the value network of an ecosystem is a map of its homeostats, drawn in a notation a room can read, and e-VSM's thirty-three pairs and Allee's arrows may turn out to be the same object seen twice. The diagram `Design Record/diagrams/rendanheyi-through-vsm.rcn.json` draws the whole mapping of this subsection, Haier's and the neighborhood's side by side, so that the claims can be argued with; a VSM of an Industry Platform on its own, to test whether the platform is really S4 and resources and nothing else, is the next drawing to make, and how Industry Platforms interact with EMCs as against MEs is the next thing to research.

#### What this section commits the design to

Everything downstream in this design record depends on the VSM frame holding. Section 5's customer scenario, the durable object between the Experience-ME and the Solution ME, lives at whatever recursion level the scenario actually lives. The linkage-mapping method surfaces both the S1-through-S3 current state and, through Idealized Design, the S4-desired state. The Industry Platform's compensation flows through the value chain from ME work at the appropriate recursion to resident value received at the appropriate recursion. The moods-and-speech-acts layer that runs through every phase is what lets S4's imaginative work translate into S1 commitment without losing its S4 character.

Linkage mapping reveals the processes across all the VSM functions. Deming's three levels line up with Beer's: the Driver level is Systems 4 and 5, the Support level is Systems 2, 3, and 3\*, and the Mainstay level is the System 1s. Marc's Spokane linkage maps were organized on those three levels, which is one reason they read so consistently across thirty subjects, and Section 8 draws on them.

The VSM frame also constrains what the field guide will not attempt. The field guide is an S4-activation-with-S3\*-audit tool for neighborhoods that already have S1 through S3 functioning and some form of S5 present. It does not attempt to build neighborhoods from scratch, install identity where none exists, replace failing operational units, or substitute for the political-cultural work that only a neighborhood can do for itself.

One of a neighborhood's cave drawings must be the linkage map of the whole neighborhood and its institutions, with the nature and status of the connections between them; that is the S1-through-S3 picture this section describes, drawn where a room can point at it. The RCN Graph Composer, which merges several drawings into one by what a node is, and the RCN Table's views are the tools for keeping that map whole as the neighborhood's separate drawings accumulate.

Section 4 develops the founded commons, the physical and social infrastructure a functioning S1-through-S5 neighborhood requires in order to sustain the S4 work the field guide activates. Without the founded commons, S4's outputs have nowhere to accumulate and nowhere to be gardened over time.
