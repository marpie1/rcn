## Whitcomb background

Boston Herald Traveler → WSJ → International Herald Tribune (finance editor, Paris) → Providence Journal (VP and editorial-page editor; two-time AP New England editorial-writing winner). ## Voice — sentence and paragraph

Mixes long clause-stacking with short punch. "Amazon is an impressive if rather creepy company... Amazonianism's causes? One is in the mirror." Rhetorical questions sparse and used to pivot. Parentheticals liberal, for texture without breaking momentum. Interjections keep it conversational — "Yes, but…", "Well, some are.", "Anyway…". Uses curly quotes ‘’ around cited phrases and idioms — a signature typographical tic.

## Voice — openings

Five patterns observed:
- Direct proposition — "Crowded American cities are generally healthier than less crowded ones..."
- News anchor — "The U.S. government has the stronger argument in its battle with Apple..."
- Personal set piece with implicit thesis — "While driving around Los Angeles's vastness last week, I kept getting a déjà vu feeling..."
- Institutional lead — "Much of American health care's future can be seen in two synergistic kinds of institutions in Rhode Island."
- Quotation/book reference — "In the appendix of Philip K. Howard's mordantly entertaining new book..."

Never a throat-clearing paragraph explaining why the piece exists.

## Voice — closings

A wry aside, an aesthetic detail from the day, or a pointed final observation. Examples: "Lovely dim late-summer light today, and leaves are falling off the plane trees from sheer exhaustion." "Regarding Golden State water-wars, see the movie 'Chinatown'." The Marc profile ends: "The whole community, he says, owns its health and well-being and we must design our futures in that context." Never a summary paragraph.

## Voice — vocabulary tics

Adjectives from a specific stock: "creepy," "cold," "mordantly," "acidly funny," "spanking new," "jalopy," "grimly funny," "capacious." Phrases: "the likes of," "of course," "at least for now," "at least for public consumption," "in droves." "(RIP)" for defunct institutions ("Boston Herald Traveler (RIP)"). "xxx" as section break in multi-topic commentary columns — three x's on their own line between topic segments.

## Voice — stance

Op-ed-page discipline. Takes a clear view; argues by example; doesn't hedge; names things — Apple "hypocritical," Iran a "corrupt theocratic dictatorship," Russia "Putin's mobster Russian regime," "'nonprofit'" hospitals whose profit shows up in executive compensation. Center-left institutional-liberal with a scold's streak against elite pretension and any side ducking responsibility. Lineage he draws on: Jane Jacobs and William H. Whyte on cities; H. Gilbert Welch on evidence-based medicine; the old public-utility model for regulated services. Suspicious of monopolies, "data-driven" management, unregulated freelancing, and consumer-tech privacy invasion. Sympathy for low-wage workers and for practical, local institutions.

## Voice — structural moves

Regional-to-universal arc as the default. Rhode Island Medicaid → national coordinated care. Whatcom County → community-owned health. Providence density → cities generally. Always through a specific place, institution, or figure.

Sources named with credential and role — "led by Merrill Thomas, its CEO, and Jane Hayward, the Rhode Island Health Center Association's president." Quotes cleanly and sparingly; prefers "as X noted" or "X told the Financial Times" to "X wrote." Expert quotes authorize a point rather than carry it.

Friction as an aside — "Yes, but how do you 'factor out' income?" In the Marc profile: "we wondered about whether only the rich would have the finest sort of individualized 'concierge care'." Friction introduced late, answered, moved past.

Multi-topic commentary columns: header ("Whitcomb: X; Y; Z; W"), segments 300–800 words each, "xxx" between them, byline tag at end reciting his affiliations. Standalone essays (like the Marc profile) run 700–900 words as one piece.

Byline tag shape: "Robert Whitcomb (email) is a X, a Y, and a Z. He used to be A, B, and C." Always includes ProJo/WSJ/IHT lineage plus current affiliations. Rotate elements, keep shape.

## Book-length register (Cape Wind)

Reviewers: "acidly funny," authors "rake some fine muck," "written with fervor and aplomb," occasionally "corny when describing new characters." Civic-drama register — protagonists get moral color, opponents get satirized, the thesis (money hijacks democracy on renewable energy) accumulates through narrative rather than direct assertion.

## What to avoid when drafting in this voice

- Throat-clearing preambles
- Breathless praise of the subject
- "On one hand / on the other hand" mushiness — op-ed voice takes a view
- Academic apparatus, footnotes, or over-caveats
- Mid-piece hedging of the subject's ideas
- Adjective-driven admiration — make admiration structural through what you include
