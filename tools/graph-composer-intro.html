A big diagram nobody can read is easy to make. Fifty-four arrows crossing each other is what you get when one person tries to draw a whole system at once. The Composer takes the opposite route: keep the pieces small enough that each one is legible on its own, and let the machine find where they join.

The problem it solves

Ask six people about a neighborhood and you get six partial pictures. The housing person draws housing. The health person draws the clinic. Each picture is small, honest, and readable. The trouble has always been putting them together — that has meant one person redrawing everything by hand, and losing detail every time they do.

The Composer does it mechanically. Load the pieces, tick the ones you want, and it produces the combined graph — fusing any node that appears in more than one piece, and closing loops that no single contributor could see.

Additive, not subtractive

There are two ways to get a drawing a group can talk about. You can build the whole system and then hide parts of it, or you can build small pieces and add up the ones you need. The first leaves you maintaining a monster. The second means you never build the monster in the first place.

This matters for the RCN goal of Cave Drawings — images a group can read, point at, and argue about without training. A reference artifact holding everything is legitimate and useful, but it is not something a group talks about. Composition gives you the talking version on demand, assembled from parts that were each readable to begin with.

How two pieces know they're about the same thing

Everything rests on one question: when two contributors both draw something called "the clinic," is that one node or two?

The Composer answers it with the schema label — the standard name from the neighborhood schema, carried on the node as metadata. Two nodes are the same thing when their schema labels match, plus an instance name when there are several of a kind.

FieldQuestion it answersPart of identity?
labelWhat do we call it on this drawing?No — free to change
schemaLabelWhat is it, in the schema?Yes
props.nameWhich one, when there are several?Yes
extraLabelsWhat else is it called? Aliases.No — data, exported to Neo4j

The consequence is worth stating plainly: the words on the drawing are free. A node can be renamed HOUSING or Housing or the housing we have to suit the audience without changing what it merges with. Legibility and identity stop fighting each other.

No wiki required. The schema labels live in the files. A FedWiki roster is one convenient way to look standard names up — it is not the price of entry. A folder of JSON files works exactly as well.

What it does

Beam

The loaded subgraphs, each with a checkbox and its node/edge count. Tick to include.

Composite

Merge the ticked subgraphs. Nodes matching on schema label + instance name become one.

Shared-node highlighting

Untick­ed subgraphs light up when they overlap your selection — brighter the more of it they touch.

Bridge

The stricter question: which unselected piece would connect two you already have?

Partition

Split a composite into its disconnected parts and push them back into the beam.

Hold a node

Click and hold to light up everything that node touches — its edges thicken, its neighbours stay bright, the rest fades. Release to clear.

Export

The composite as graph JSON for the Graph Tool, or as Graphviz DOT.

The highlight is a promise, not a hint

Because the highlight uses the same key as the merge, a lit row means something specific: compositing that subgraph will fuse those nodes, not set them alongside. Hovering the badge names which ones. The feature stays quiet when there is genuinely no overlap, which is what makes it worth trusting when it does light up.

Worked example — the EIP schema

The RCN EIP schema exists as sixteen curated aspect files: Action, Affect, Asset, Commitment, Conversation, Culture, Function Objective, Motivation, Org, Person, Place, Power, Problem, Purpose, Role, Solution. Each is small enough to read — two to eight nodes.

Ticking all sixteen composites 74 source nodes down to 25, matching the whole-schema reference exactly, with no duplicates. Forty-nine nodes were recognised as repeats of ones already present.

It also showed something the flattened version had lost. The composite carries relations that the single merged diagram does not — Person —experience→ Motivation, Place —support→ Person, Person —make→ Commitment, Conversation —for→ Action — plus edge labels that flattening had fused (create / stimulates was two separate relations). The subgraphs are the better source of truth.

Where it came from

This is a reimplementation of Ward Cunningham's Solo Super Collaborator, whose premise is the one above: individuals with specialised knowledge curate small graphs that later compose into a larger community resource, closing loops where the small graphs intersect at identical nodes.

One idea from Ward's version is not yet here, and is worth knowing about: his converter resolves each node's name against the sitemaps of every site in the page's roster, so identity binds to a real federated page rather than a typed string. That is the federated answer to the same question schemaLabel answers locally.

Composer or Graph Tool?

They are different verbs, deliberately kept apart.

  • Graph Tool Authoring and drawing one graph. Modes, layouts, icons, legend-as-registry, CLD loops, exports. 5,300 lines.
  • Composer Assembling many graphs. Beam, merge, bridge, partition. Deliberately small.

They share a file format. Draw the pieces in the Graph Tool, assemble them in the Composer, export the composite, open it back in the Graph Tool to lay out and dress for an audience.

The one-line version

Keep the pieces small enough to read. Let the machine find where they join. Nobody draws the whole thing, and nobody has to agree on wording first — only on what things are.
