1 · Core ideas in one minute

A timeline is made of intervals (spans of time, drawn as bars) and links (typed relations between them). Three things are worth knowing before you start:

  • Time can be fuzzy. A bar has a solid core (the part you're sure of) and soft shoulders that fade out (the uncertainty).
  • Events are pinned or floating. 📌 Pinned = locked to a real date (a fact, a deadline). Floating = the tool positions it from its links, and you can nudge it.
  • You can say it or drag it. Typing a sentence and dragging a bar make the same underlying fact — use whichever is faster.

2 · The interface

Three regions:

  • Toolbar (top) — document actions, creating intervals and links, the sentence box, zoom, and export. Hover any button for a one-line explanation.
  • Canvas (center) — a horizontal date axis with interval bars stacked in rows and links drawn between them. An issues panel appears at the bottom-right of the properties column when something contradicts.
  • Properties panel (right) — everything about whatever you've selected: label, dates, pinned/floating, fuzz, confidence, colour, provenance, a copy-link button, and delete.

3 · Starting & navigating

Start a timeline

  • New (top-left) clears everything to a blank canvas centred on the current year. This is how you begin from scratch.
  • Demo loads the sample "founding → first report" timeline — handy for exploring, and to bring the sample back after pressing New.
  • Give your timeline a name in the field next to the New button.

Move around

  • Pan: drag an empty part of the canvas left/right.
  • Zoom: scroll the mouse wheel over the canvas (zooms around the cursor), or use ＋ / －.
  • Fit: the Fit button frames every interval.
The date axis is adaptive: zoomed out you see years (2024), zoom in and it becomes months (Jul 2024), zoom further and it becomes days (Jul 15). Dates are never shown as decimals.

4 · Creating intervals

Three ways, all equivalent:

  • Double-click an empty spot on the canvas — makes an interval starting at that date, on the row under your cursor.
  • + Interval button — makes one at the centre of the current view.
  • Sentence box — type new Groundbreaking and press Enter. Naming a not-yet-existing interval in any sentence also creates it.

A new interval is floating by default. Select it (click it) and edit it in the properties panel.

5 · The interval — extent & the four handles

An interval bar has a solid core from its start to its end (the time you're certain about), and soft fuzz shoulders extending outward that fade to nothing (the uncertainty). A bar whose start and end are the same date is an instant, drawn as a dot.

Select an interval and four drag handles appear — this is the "grab-target decides" rule, so there are no modes to switch:

HandleGrabDoes
Bodythe middle of the barMoves the whole interval in time (keeps its length and fuzz). Floating only.
Inner edgethe blue handle at the core start/endChanges the start or end date — i.e. the duration. Hidden when pinned.
Outer edgethe grey handle past the shoulderWidens or narrows the fuzz (how uncertain that end is). Works even when pinned.
The split is deliberate: you can always become less sure about a pinned fact (widen its band), but you can't silently slide the fact to a new date. The record can't be falsified by a stray mouse gesture.

You can also set start, end, and fuzz precisely in the properties panel (fuzz has sliders; start/end are date fields — see §7).

6 · Pinned vs. floating

Toggle it in the panel (the 📌 Pinned / Floating buttons) or press P with the interval selected.

  • 📌 Pinned — anchored to the calendar. Shown with a solid core outline, a pin, and a drop-line to the axis. It will not move when neighbours shift. Use it for facts and hard deadlines.
  • Floating — shown with a dashed core outline. The solver positions it from its links; you can nudge its body within what the links allow. Use it for anything planned, forecast, or not yet firmly dated.

7 · Dates & durations

Dates always display as Mon D, YYYY (e.g. Mar 15, 2021). When you type a date — in the sentence box or a Start/End field — several forms are accepted:

March 15 2021same (comma optional, full or short month)

Durations (a length, not a date — used for fuzz, slack, and "lasts") display as spans like 6 mo or 3 yr 2 mo, never decimals. When typing a duration you can write 6 months, 2 weeks, 90 days, or a bare number for years (3).

8 · Links — before & meets

Links are the typed relations between intervals. v0 has the two that do the most work:

  • before (loose) — A ends no later than B starts, but a gap is allowed. The gap is slack, labelled on the link. Drawn as a dashed arrow. Drag A later and it eventually pushes B; drag B earlier and it stops at A's wall.
  • meets (rigid) — B starts exactly when A ends, welded. Drawn as a solid connector with a weld mark. Move one and the other follows as a block.

Creating a link

  1. Click + before → or + meets ⊣⊢.
  2. Click the source interval, then the target. Done.

Or type it: Pilot before Grant, Grant meets Scale-up. Select a link to change its type, confidence, or provenance in the panel.

Neither link can say during

Both relations are defined against where A ends. That means you cannot link two intervals that overlap — and if you try, nothing complains. The solver simply pushes B, and everything downstream of B, until the overlap is gone. The dates you typed are quietly replaced.

This bites hardest with long-running states. An interval "Building in community ownership, 2022→2030" with before pointing at "Units occupied" does not mean the occupancy follows from the ownership — it means the occupancy cannot begin until 2030.

Two ways out, both legitimate:

  • Bound the interval to the event that actually causes the next thing ("title transferred, Nov 2022 – Feb 2023") rather than to the open-ended state it begins.
  • Don't link it. Overlapping intervals can just carry their own dates and sit side by side. Links are for sequence; dates carry everything else.
The Import report (§14) flags this before you load a file, and it is worth a glance whenever a bar lands somewhere you did not expect: it is almost always a link across an overlap, not a bad date.

9 · The solver & contradictions

After every edit the tool re-solves: it propagates the links forward in time, moving floating intervals so the relations hold. Slack and "critical path" behaviour fall out of this automatically — you don't set them.

Two things it can't satisfy become contradictions, flagged in red (the link, the affected interval, and an issues list at the bottom of the panel):

  • A link would need to move a pinned interval — e.g. a task pushed past a hard deadline.
  • A cycle of links that can never all be true (A before B before A).
Click any issue in the list to jump to the object involved. Contradictions are a feature: the tool is showing you exactly where the plan breaks or where two accounts disagree, instead of hiding it.

10 · Saying it — the sentence box

The sentence box (top toolbar) is a fast, keyboard-only way to assert things. Type and press Enter. Every sentence makes the same kind of object a gesture would.

SentenceEffect
new KickoffCreate an interval named "Kickoff".
Pilot before GrantAdd a loose before link (creating either interval if needed).
Grant meets Scale-upAdd a rigid meets link.
Report = Jan 2026 .. Jun 2026Set an interval's start and end.
Scale-up lasts 18 monthsSet its end to start + that duration.
Founding ~ 2016Make it an instant around that date (with a little fuzz).
pin Report / unpin ReportPin or unpin by name.

Names are matched case-insensitively. If a sentence doesn't parse, a hint shows the accepted forms.

11 · Provenance

Every interval and every link carries provenance, editable in the panel:

  • Asserted by — who is making this claim.
  • Evidence / note — the source or reasoning. Any http(s)://… URL you type here appears as a clickable link just below the field (it opens in a new tab); the note itself stays plain, editable text.
  • Confidence — a slider; lower confidence makes the bar's core render fainter, so shaky claims look shaky.

Hover any interval or link to see its provenance in a frosted tooltip. This is what makes a timeline something a group can refine: each contribution says who, why, and how sure.

12 · Selecting, editing, deleting

  • Select: click an interval or a link. Its handles appear and the panel fills in. Click empty canvas to deselect.
  • Rename / recolour: in the panel. Eight colours are available.
  • Delete: the red button in the panel, or press Delete / Backspace with something selected. Deleting an interval also removes its links.
  • Undo / Redo: the ↶ Undo and ↷ Redo toolbar buttons, or Ctrl/Cmd-Z and Ctrl/Cmd-Shift-Z. Every change is covered — creating, deleting, dragging, pinning, editing a field, running a sentence, loading a demo, and importing. A run of keystrokes or a slider drag on one field folds into a single step, so undo doesn't crawl character by character.

13 · Deep links (Copy link)

Any object can produce a link that reopens this exact timeline and highlights that object.

  • 🔗 Link (toolbar) copies a link to the whole timeline — highlighting the current selection, if any.
  • 🔗 Copy link to this interval / relation (panel) copies the same whole-timeline link, only focused: opening it rebuilds the entire timeline and then centres and selects that one object. It is not a slice of the timeline — there's no server to fetch a slice from, so every link carries the full model; the only difference from the toolbar button is which object opens selected.
Because the tool has no server, the link is self-contained — it carries the whole timeline inside the URL. That means links can get long and won't auto-update if you change the timeline later. It's perfect for "here's the state I'm looking at" sharing into a document, chat, or FedWiki page. (If timelines are later hosted under names, links can become short pointers instead.)

14 · Importing and exporting (JSON · SVG · PNG)

Import is one button and the three exports are another three. They used to share a single box, which showed your current model in a textarea with a Load button beside it — so the thing you were reading and the thing that would overwrite it were the same field. They are separate doors now.

Getting a timeline in

  • Import opens a box with three ways in: drop a .json file on it, click it to pick a file, or paste JSON into the text area below.
  • You can also drop a .json file anywhere on the window — the whole page becomes a target and the import box opens with the file already read.
  • Nothing loads until you press Load. Before then you get a report of what is in the file.

The import report

Every file is checked before it can load, and the result is colour-coded:

  • Green — interval and link counts, the model name, and "checks out."
  • Amber — it will load, but something is worth knowing: a link whose rel is not meets or before (it will silently behave as before), an interval with no end date (it will default to one year long), an unreadable date, or — the one that catches people — a link between two intervals that overlap. Neither relation can say during, so the solver will push the second interval, and everything after it, until the overlap is gone. Your dates go quietly missing. See §8.
  • Red — it cannot load, and Load is disabled: JSON that will not parse, a link pointing at an interval id that does not exist, or duplicate ids.
Importing replaces what is on the canvas. It is undoable (Ctrl/Cmd-Z restores the previous timeline), but exporting first is still the safe habit.

Getting a timeline out

  • JSON downloads the model as a .json file, named after the model. Dates are stored as readable strings; each object keeps a stable id (so a future bridge can match an interval to a Graph node or a Map parcel).
  • SVG downloads a crisp vector image of the whole timeline (fit to content, white background).
  • PNG downloads a high-resolution raster image.

SVG and PNG are the same exports the Graph Tool offers; format types that only make sense for graphs (MDL, Cypher) are intentionally left out.

To share a timeline without a file at all, use 🔗 Link (§13) — it carries the whole model in the URL.

16 · Working patterns

Reconstructing history with a group

Pin the facts you're certain of (dated records). For everything hazy, make a floating interval with a wide fuzz band and set its provenance to who remembers it. Add before links for known orderings. As people contribute more bounds, the bands narrow — you're watching accuracy improve.

Planning & forecasting

Pin the fixed dates (a grant date, a report deadline). Chain the work with meets (must be back-to-back) and before (must precede, slack allowed). Drag a milestone and watch the ripple; if a slip turns a link red, you've found the thing the schedule can't absorb. Widen the end-fuzz of a forecast to show it's a guess, not a promise.

From a Graph Tool trace to a timeline

This is the pattern the two tools were built for, and it is likely to become the common one: see and discuss the logic, then see and discuss the effects across time.

In the Graph Tool, Trace mode tags edges as T1–T4 and animates them outward from their roots in waves. That animation is a partial order — it says this, then this, then this. What it cannot say is when, for how long, or how big the gap is: it runs at a fixed speed in milliseconds, so a step that took a week and a step that took four years look exactly alike.

Bring the same trace here and the sequence acquires dates:

  1. Each node on the trace path becomes an interval. Giving it a duration is the step where you discover whether anyone actually knows one — that discovery is half the value.
  2. Each trace edge becomes a link: meets if the next thing starts as this one ends, before if there is a gap.
  3. Keep the node's label as the interval label, and colour intervals by their legend family, so the two pictures can be read side by side.
  4. Fill in who and conf as you go. The graph has no place to record that a causal claim is contested; here it is a field.

What you get is an argument the graph cannot make. On the graph, two edges out of the same node are drawn identically. On the timeline, one of them may be welded to its cause and the other trailing it by slack 2 yr 2 mo — long enough that everyone had declared the work finished and stopped looking. A causal edge with years of slack is a fundamentally different claim from one with a week of slack, and only the timeline shows the difference.

Worked example, shipped in the repo: the EIP schema's T1 trace as a graph, and the same trace as t1-trace-timeline-demo.json. Open both and compare.

The Graph Tool does this for you. In Trace mode, under "Send trace to Timeline", press ⌛ T1 (or T2–T4) and the trace opens here as intervals and links.

What arrives is deliberately half-finished. Every interval is a one-year placeholder at confidence 0.3 with an empty who — because nobody has dated it yet. The links, by contrast, carry a real who ("T1 trace, <model>") and confidence 0.8, because the order genuinely is attributed: the graph said so. Every link comes in as before, never meets — a trace edge asserts sequence, not adjacency.

So your job on arrival is exactly the useful one: replace the placeholder durations with what you actually know, pin the things that have a document behind them, raise the confidence as you go, and change a link to meets where you establish there is genuinely no gap.
