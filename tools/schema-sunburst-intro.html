The Sunburst turns it into one image a group can point at.

Each lens reads the same substrate and answers a different question. The Sunburst answers the one that has to be settled before the others mean anything: what are we calling things, and where does each of those words actually get used?

Why a sunburst, and not another graph

A node-link diagram is the right picture for a network and the wrong picture for a hierarchy. Draw a strict tree as nodes and arrows and you get a tangle whose shape carries no information — the layout engine's opinion, not the data's.

But the Composer's levels are a strict contraction: zooming out merges; zooming in never invents. Family, schema label and variable label are progressively longer names for the same idea, so every concept has exactly one parent. That is precisely the precondition a sunburst requires, and it was already true of our data before anyone thought to draw it this way.

Ring 2
The schema label — the merge key. Problem, Org, Action
Ring 3
The states of that concept — the ways it can be measured. Seriousness of PROBLEM, Effectiveness of ORG. A concept with several states splits its arc between them

The part that is ours

The form is borrowed — it is the shape of the Python Standard Type Hierarchy diagram. But that diagram gives every leaf an equal slice, because a type either exists or it does not. Our concepts carry something a type system has no equivalent of: evidence.

Each concept knows which of the 16 source drawings contain it. Org appears in nine of them. Asset in one. So the Sunburst sizes each arc by that source count, and the picture stops being a taxonomy and becomes a map of reach: a fat wedge is a concept that turned out to be load-bearing across most of the schema, a sliver is one that matters in a single corner of it.

What a “source” is depends on which graph you opened, and the difference matters. In aspects16 the sixteen sources are topic drawings — action, org, place — so a wide arc means the concept is cross-cutting, turning up in most aspects of one schema. In the neo4j reference the sources are people (merchant, organizer), and there size(c.sources) > 1 is the gold test: two contributors reaching the same concept independently. Same mechanism, two readings — and the wheel will not tell you which one you are looking at.

What it cannot do

There are no causal edges here, and there never can be. A sunburst is a partition of a disc: every child sits inside exactly one parent, and the geometry offers nowhere to put an arrow that returns to where it started. The Fixes-that-Fail loop — Problem raising Motivation raising Action, and address and resolve closing negatively back onto Problem — has no representation in this shape.

That is the price of the clarity, not a gap to be filled later. The causal reading lives in /projection/causal and the Graph Tool, where a loop is drawable and means something. If someone proposes adding edges to this wheel, the geometry will accept them and the picture will lie.

A different wheel could carry causality, and it is worth building. Root a tree on one concept, put its direct causes in ring 1, theirs in ring 2, and outward — that is a tree, and the same concept appearing in several places is the accepted price, exactly as it is in the Graph Tool's driver trees. The rule is not "a sunburst cannot show influence." It is that the rings must be the hierarchy you are actually claiming: naming here, causal distance there. One wheel cannot be both at once.

What makes it different

  • OriginalArc width is provenance, not size. Every sunburst in the wild encodes a quantity — file bytes, revenue, population. This one encodes how many separate source drawings contain the concept, which turns a reference diagram into a record of where a vocabulary is actually load-bearing.
  • DerivedNothing is stored to make the picture. Families, colours, labels and source counts all come out of the live database. There is no second copy to drift from, so the diagram cannot go stale while the data moves.
  • LegibleSearch dims, it does not filter. Removing the non-matches would silently re-widen every remaining arc, and a proportional picture would start answering a question nobody asked. Matches keep full colour; everything else fades but holds its place.
  • LayeredTwo readings of the same wheel. Colour by family for the vocabulary, or by OPM Object/Process for things-versus-happenings — with unclassified shown as itself rather than folded into either.
  • HonestThe label that will not fit goes to hover. Each label is capped by the tangential room its own wedge has. What does not fit is shortened; what cannot fit at all becomes a faint ellipsis — and every segment carries its full text on hover regardless, so nothing is ever merely lost.
  • DumbThe renderer knows no domain. The projection emits start and span as fractions; the tool turns fractions into arcs. Point it at a different graph and it draws that instead, with no code change.
  • PortableWorks with the server off. A snapshot of all three graphs is baked into the file, so it opens from a thumb drive as a handout — and says so, in the footer, rather than pretending to be live.

What you can see in ten seconds

Where the reach is

Person, Institution and Aim take nearly half the wheel between them. That is not a design decision — it is what being needed in two-thirds of the topic drawings looks like.

Where it is thin

Resource is two concepts, one source each. Either the neighborhood genuinely does not think in terms of assets and power, or nobody has asked it to yet.

Whether a family is real

A family whose children are all slivers is a category somebody proposed, not one the group uses. The wheel makes that visible without an argument about it.

Whether the vocabulary is balanced

Eight families should carry roughly comparable weight if the schema fits the domain. Where they do not, the shape asks the question for you.

Comparators

Neo4j Browser / Bloom schema viewDifferent question

Shows node kinds and the relationships between them — five boxes and some arrows. That is the meta-model, and we have it at /projection/schema. It cannot show the 24 concepts inside :Concept, because to Neo4j they are all one label.

D3 / Observable sunburstSame form, no substrate

The form is a solved problem and the libraries are excellent. What they do not give you is a live tree derived from a governed database, a source count to weight by, or a palette that is already the project's. Those are the whole content here; the arcs are the easy part.

Python Standard Type Hierarchy diagramThe direct ancestor

The reference this borrows its shape from, and the proof that the form carries a large vocabulary legibly at a glance. The difference is that a type hierarchy is fixed and equal-weighted; ours is provenance-weighted, and moves whenever the drawings do.

RCN Graph ToolThe other half

Not a competitor — the complement. It holds everything the Sunburst structurally cannot: direction, polarity, feedback. Between them they cover the substrate, and each is honest about which half it is showing.

How to read it with a group

MoveWhat it surfaces
Open on sourcesThe default. Fat means the concept appears in many of the graph’s source drawings; thin means one.
Flip to equalRemoves the evidence. Useful once, to show what the weighting was doing.
Drop to 2 ringsFamilies and schema labels only — the shape for a wall or a slide.
Hover or click a wedgeFull labels, the states, the source count and which sources — including for wedges too thin to carry text.
Switch graphaspects16 is the 16 drawings; composite26 is the signed EIP CLD; neo4j is the n=6 reference.

The productive question in a room is rarely "is this diagram right." It is "why is that one so thin?" — which is a question about the neighborhood, not about the schema.

The bet underneath

A group cannot agree on causes until it agrees on words, and it cannot notice that it disagrees about words while the words are in a table. Put the vocabulary in one circle, size it by how many people actually used each term, and the disagreements become visible — and therefore discussable — before they turn into an argument about something else.
