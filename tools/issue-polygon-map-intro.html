Most civic arguments are really arguments about a boundary. Who is inside the rule and who is outside it; which government's law applies here and not there; how many of the people actually affected want the change. The Issue Polygon Map makes that boundary an object you can draw, share, and reason over — instead of a thing everyone assumes they agree about.

The third kind of region

Every RCN tool is the same shape underneath: regions plus typed relations. In the Graph Tool a region is a node and the relations are causal. In the Timeline a region is an interval and the relations are temporal. Here a region is a parcel or a boundary, and the relations are the ones geography gives you for free — inside, outside, overlapping, adjacent.

An issue, not a map

This is the distinction that matters. A GIS viewer shows you land. This tool shows you one question — and the land is there only because the question has a footprint.

The working example ships with the tool: backyard chicken keeping in Superior, Arizona. The issue file carries the polygon that bounds it, the parcels inside, the two jurisdictions that overlap it, the three laws in play, and the actions nobody has done yet. Open it and the argument is on the screen in one piece.

What makes it different

  • Jurisdiction stack
    The layers are governments, not map furniture. Municipality, county and state boundaries are drawn together with the laws tagged to each, so you can see which rule reaches a given parcel — and where one government's authority has been preempted by another's.
  • Stance, honestly
    Every parcel is Unknown until somebody actually asks. Public record gives you lot size, zoning and ownership. It does not give you what a household thinks. The tool defaults to Unknown and keeps a running tally, so the gap between "parcels affected" and "people we've spoken to" is impossible to hide.
  • No consent needed
    You can build it before you talk to anyone. Everything except stance is public record, so the map is one of the instruments you can prepare in advance — and then use as the reason for the first conversation.
  • Shareable to the parcel
    Every popup has a Copy link. Send someone a URL that opens the map on one property with its popup already open, or on the view you were just looking at.

Why the jurisdiction stack is the point

In Superior the question isn't "are chickens allowed." It's whose rule wins:

State
HB2325 (2024) limits how far a municipality can restrict residential fowl — but only for code adopted after it passed.
County
Pinal County's animal ordinance sets a concrete standard: max 6 hens, 100 ft setback.
Municipality
The Town of Superior code may prohibit fowl outright — and may predate the state preemption, which is exactly the thing nobody has verified.

Three governments, three different answers, one back yard. The map is where that becomes a specific, checkable question — request the code from the Town Clerk — rather than a general grievance.

The unknowns are the deliverable. A finished issue map is not one where every parcel is coloured in. It's one where the open questions have names and owners: which code section applies, whose lot qualifies, who hasn't been asked yet. The tool is built to make those gaps visible rather than to paper over them.

What it looks like

The issue polygon

A gold dashed boundary defining the scope of the question. Everything inside it is in the argument.

Parcel pins

Green for pro, red for con, grey-blue for unknown. Click one to set a stance or edit its details.

Boundary layers

Municipality and county outlines, each with a popup carrying its code status and the law that applies.

Live tally

Pro / con / unknown counts that update as you work — the honest picture of how much you actually know.

Drawn polygons

Sketch a new boundary, name it, then drag its vertices to reshape — insert or remove points as the argument sharpens.

NDC anchor

The Neighborhood Development Cooperative that convenes the issue, ringed on the map with its coordinator.

How it compares

QGIS / ArcGISDifferent

Vastly more capable at geography and vastly worse at this job. They model land; you would have to build the notion of "an issue," "a stance," and "a jurisdiction stack" yourself, and nobody you're organising with can open the file.

Google My MapsOverlaps

Easy and shareable, and fine for dropping pins. But there is no typed data on a pin, no tally, no jurisdictions, and no export anything else can read — so the work stays trapped as decoration.

Canvassing tools (NationBuilder, VAN)Overlaps

They track people and stances at scale, and they are built around a campaign that already knows what it wants. This tool is for the stage before that — when the question itself, and whose rule governs it, is still being worked out.

A shared spreadsheet of addressesThis replaces it

The honest comparator, and what most neighbourhood efforts actually use. The map does everything the spreadsheet does, plus shows you the boundary, the overlapping jurisdictions, and which parcels nobody has visited.

Where it sits in the sequence

The RCN instruments are ordered by how much relationship they require. This one is near the front: it needs nobody's permission, because it is assembled from public record. That makes it a good first artifact — something concrete to bring to a first conversation, rather than a survey you have to earn the right to run.

What it cannot do alone is tell you why the situation persists, or when anything happened. Those are the Graph Tool and the Timeline. An issue that's worth mapping usually wants all three: the map says where, the graph says why, the timeline says whether the timing supports the story.

Share what you make

Export the whole issue as GeoJSON — parcels, the issue polygon and any drawn boundary — and it reloads into the tool intact, or opens in any GIS. Or export the picture as SVG or PNG for a flyer, a council packet, or a page in the wiki. Every parcel and view also has a copyable deep link.

For anyone generating an issue file programmatically, the field-level schema and its traps are documented in tools/schemas/issue-polygon-map.md.

Where this is going

Parcel data imported straight from county assessor records; jurisdiction boundaries pulled from TIGER instead of hand-entered; a view that holds several issues at once so a neighbourhood can see everything in play. The bet underneath is that a boundary drawn in public, with its rules and its unknowns attached, changes the argument more than another opinion does.
