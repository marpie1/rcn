Agreement Checker

Elinor Ostrom's grammar of rules, turned into plain questions a group can ask of any agreement it writes.

Marc Pierson and Claude Opus 5.5 · September 2026

Introduction User Manual Open the checker

Groups write agreements all the time: garden bylaws, co-op rules, house agreements, council charters. Most of them fail in the same few ways. Two rules get packed into one sentence. A permission quietly contradicts a duty. A penalty is written down, but nobody is named to carry it out. The Agreement Checker finds these problems and asks about each one in plain words, so the group can settle it together.

Why it exists

Elinor Ostrom won the Nobel Prize for showing how communities govern shared resources without a boss or a market. With Sue Crawford she wrote a grammar for the rules those communities make. Every rule, they found, answers the same small set of questions: who it is for, whether it is a duty, a permission, or a prohibition, what action it covers, when it applies, how it is carried out, and what happens if it is broken.

That grammar is powerful, and almost nobody outside a university uses it. It arrived wrapped in symbols (ADICO, Deontic, Aim, XOR) and in diagrams that are sparse, use arcane words, and carry no examples. The people who most need it, the ones writing their own agreements, never meet it.

"I have never thought that math, logic, or programming has to be for the initiated only. It would just be much more verbose and illustrated, but then democratic."Marc Pierson

The checker takes Marc at his word. It keeps all of Ostrom's logic and shows none of her symbols unless you ask for them. The questions are in plain English, every part has an example, and the group decides every answer.

Two kinds of agreement, and their questions

Rules about what people do depend on roles and things that someone created first. "The water keeper must suspend watering" needs a water keeper. So there are two kinds of agreement, and the creating kind comes first.

First, create what the rules need

A creating agreement brings a role, group, or thing into being: a position, a council, a membership. For example: "Each spring, a water keeper must be chosen from the garden members." It answers four questions of its own:

Then, rules about what people do

Each rule should say one thing that people must do, may do, or must not do. For that sentence, the checker asks seven questions:

QuestionWhat it asksGarden exampleOstrom's term
WHEN?What has to be true for this to switch onwhen the tank is below halfActivation Condition
WHO?The people this is aboutgardenersAttributes
MUST, MAY, OR MUST NOT?A duty, a permission, or a prohibition. Exactly one per sentence.must notDeontic
DO WHAT?The actionwaterAim
TO WHAT?The thing the action is done totheir plotsObject
HOW?Limits on how, where, or how muchwith sprinklersExecution Constraint
OR ELSE?What the group agreed will happen if it is broken, and who makes it happenthe water keeper suspends watering for a weekOr else

WHO?, MUST, MAY, OR MUST NOT?, and DO WHAT? always need an answer. WHEN?, TO WHAT?, and HOW? are often blank, and that is fine: a rule that always applies has no WHEN.

WHEN? and OR ELSE? belong to both kinds. A creating agreement can have a trigger ("each spring") and an agreed consequence, just like a rule.

What the checker catches

Two rules in one sentence

It splits them apart, one MUST, MAY, or MUST NOT each, so each can be argued about and changed by itself.

A MUST NOT in disguise

"Must water only on assigned days" really means "must not water on other days." It asks which you mean.

A clash

One sentence limits watering, another allows hand-watering any day. It asks whether the second is an exception, and suggests adding "unless …".

A penalty nobody carries out

"Violators lose watering for a week" says what happens but not who makes it happen. It suggests a duty that names someone.

No OR ELSE

A duty with no agreed consequence holds only as long as goodwill holds. It asks whether the group wants one.

A role nobody created

The water keeper enforces the rules, but no sentence says how the keeper is chosen. It asks for one.

Words people read differently

"Promptly", "reasonable", "as needed". It asks for how much, how often, or by when.

Things a rule takes for granted

"Gardeners must water on assigned days" assumes everyone knows who counts as a gardener, and that someone assigns the days. It asks who.

Soft words and missing people

"Should" is softer than MUST. "Fees must be paid" doesn't say who pays. "They" doesn't say who they are.

How strong is each sentence?

The checker labels every sentence by what it has answered. These labels match the companion drawing.

AGREED RULE

MUST or MUST NOT, with an OR ELSE, and someone named to carry it out.

SHARED EXPECTATION

MUST, MAY, or MUST NOT, but nothing backs it. It holds as long as goodwill does.

SHARED HABIT

No MUST, MAY, or MUST NOT at all. Just who does what, and when.

CREATING AGREEMENT

Brings a role, group, or thing into being.

None of these is wrong. Plenty of good agreements are shared expectations. The label shows the group what it has actually agreed to, so nobody is surprised later.

One bylaw, before and after

As written

"Gardeners must water only on assigned days and must not use sprinklers, but may hand-water seedlings any day. Violators lose watering privileges for a week."

The checker splits this into four sentences and asks five questions: is "must water only" a MUST NOT in disguise; is hand-watering seedlings an exception; who takes away the watering privileges; who counts as a gardener; and who assigns the days?

After the group answers

  1. Gardeners must not water on days not assigned to them, unless hand-watering seedlings.
  2. When the tank is below half, gardeners must not water their plots with sprinklers.
  3. Gardeners may hand-water seedlings on any day.
  4. The water keeper must suspend watering for one week for any gardener who breaks these rules.
  5. Each spring, a water keeper must be chosen from the garden members.
  6. Anyone who holds a plot counts as a gardener.
  7. Each spring, the water keeper must assign watering days to every gardener.

Sentences 1 and 2 are now agreed rules, backed by sentence 4, whose keeper is created by sentence 5. Sentences 6 and 7 answer what the rules took for granted: who counts as a gardener, and who assigns the days. The checker leaves one note: if the water keeper doesn't do their duties, nothing happens, except that the group can choose someone else. That may be fine. The group decides.

Who it is for

  • Community gardens, co-ops, tenant groups, and neighborhood councils writing or revising their rules.
  • NDC councils and the RCN Constitution work, where creating agreements set up the positions, councils, and memberships everything else depends on.
  • Facilitators who want a group to read its own agreement closely, one sentence at a time, without a lawyer in the room.
  • Researchers who code rules with Ostrom's grammar: the full coding is one click away on every sentence.

Honest gaps

  • It reads English word patterns and does not understand meaning. Its guesses will sometimes be wrong. Every guess is an editable box, and every question can be set aside with Leave as is.
  • It reads one agreement at a time. It won't notice a clash with an older bylaw or a state law it hasn't seen.
  • It checks whether an agreement is complete and clear, not whether it is fair. Fairness is the group's conversation.
  • It works one sentence at a time and does not yet handle rules that nest inside other rules beyond OR ELSE.
  • The plain names for Ostrom's "Conditions" (here WHEN? and HOW?) and for the four creating questions are still being settled.
  • Your work stays in your own browser. To share it, copy the link, or save the drawing file.
