An A3 is one page, written in pencil by the person who does the work, about one problem seen once. It is a conversation, not a report — it gets carried to the gemba, argued over, and marked up. This tool keeps that shape and refuses to let it drift into a project plan.

Whose A3 this is

A3 problem solving comes out of Toyota, but this tool follows Cindy Jimmerson's healthcare version (A3 Problem Solving for Healthcare, 2007), learned through the Toyota engagement at Community Medical Center in Missoula and taught since to hospitals and clinics across the country.

That matters because most A3 templates you can download are project-management forms with Japanese labels. Hers is not. It is a record of direct observation, and the things it insists on are the things that generic templates quietly drop.

The three rules the sheet enforces

Every A3 template says "define the problem" and "find the root cause." Saying it does not make it happen. These three are built into the shape of the page instead:

  • A problem is a gap. Her definition is the difference between what is supposed to happen and what actually happens. So the Problem Statement is three fields, not one textarea: the standard, the observation, and the gap between them highlighted in amber. If you cannot fill the first two, you do not yet have a problem — you have a complaint.
  • Root cause is a broken process, never a person. The 5 Whys chain runs a live check. Type "the nurse forgot to check the box" and the row turns pink with a prompt: what about the work made that the easy thing to do? That moment is where most healthcare A3s stop being improvements and become disciplinary notes.
  • Countermeasures, not solutions. A countermeasure is a specific change to the work, tried and measured, kept or dropped. Each row carries an Answers column pointing back at the why it addresses — so the right side of the page cannot drift free of the analysis on the left.

What it does

Drawings, not prose

Current Condition and Target Condition are drawing slots, not text boxes. Drop a photo of a pencil sketch, paste a screenshot, or import an SVG from the Graph Tool.

A page whose Current Condition is a paragraph is not an A3.

Storm bursts

Every problem you saw while observing gets named. Click one burst to mark what this A3 is about — exactly one can hold it. The rest stay as candidate A3s, so they survive the walk back to the desk.

It has to fit

Paper clips silently. Every field measures itself as you type and shows a red "too long for the page" badge while there is still time to say it shorter. The one-page constraint is hers, and the sheet holds you to it.

Just do it

Her escape hatch, on the page as a checkbox. A problem you can fix this morning does not get an A3, and marking that is more honest than writing one anyway.

Prints to 11×17

One landscape page at 1:1, with the toolbar and the hints dropped. Print it, take it to the people who do the work, and let them mark it up.

One self-contained file

Save as .a3.rcn.json — drawings embedded, so the A3 travels as a single document. Markdown export ports straight into FedWiki.

The pairing: map the stream, then A3 the worst of it

In her method the A3 does not begin on a blank page. It begins with a value stream map, and the A3's Current Condition is a small sketch of that stream with the storm bursts on it. The two tools are one method, so they are wired together.

  1. Draw the current state in VSM mode in the Graph Tool — one patient, one condition, one observation, times as you saw them.
  2. Place a storm burst at every problem you witnessed, and mark the one worth an A3.
  3. Press VSM→A3.
  4. Open this tool and Load the file it wrote.

The page arrives with the theme taken from the burst, the whole burst list with the chosen one marked, the observed times in the caption and in "what actually happened," and the lead time as the follow-up baseline. What only a person can supply — what was supposed to happen, the gap, the five whys — is deliberately left empty.

And when the A3 asks how you will know it worked: counts and days belong on a run chart. Hand the measure to the SPC tool rather than declaring victory from two numbers.

How it compares

Instead ofWhat this does differently
A Word or PowerPoint A3 template The rules are structural rather than advisory. A template cannot stop you writing a one-line problem statement or blaming a person; this sheet notices both. It also guarantees the page actually fits on one page, which templates famously do not.
A generic Lean / Kaizen SaaS platform No account, no server, no subscription, no data leaving the machine — one HTML file that works offline. Patient-adjacent observation should not require uploading anything to a vendor.
A ticketing or CI system An A3 is not a ticket. It is one page that a group reads together and argues with. There is no queue, no assignee field, no workflow state beyond draft / in test / confirmed / dropped.
A blank sheet of 11×17 paper Honestly — paper is still excellent, and she recommends it. This tool earns its place by carrying the drawing, doing the arithmetic when it comes from a value stream map, keeping the candidate bursts, and making the A3 shareable and printable without losing the pencil-sketch character.

Who it is for

Built for the Whatcom Wealth and Health work, where community health workers, clinic staff and a cooperative are trying to see their own processes clearly enough to change them. The vocabulary is clinical throughout — patient, provider, order, handoff — because that is what the people filling it in actually say.

It is useful to anyone doing improvement work where the problem is a flow between people rather than a defect in a thing: a clinic, a care agency, a co-op, a neighborhood organization.

The point of the format

An A3 is small on purpose. The page is the size it is so that a group can stand around it, so that one person can hold the whole problem in view, and so that the temptation to fix four things at once has nowhere to go. Most of what the format does for you, it does by refusing you room. This tool is built to keep that refusal intact.
