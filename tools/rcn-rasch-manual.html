The tool is one HTML file. Open it in any browser — no install, no server, no account, and nothing you load ever leaves your machine. Everything below assumes you have it open beside this page.

1 · Opening the tool

Double-click rcn-rasch.html, or drag it onto a browser window. The panel down the left side runs top to bottom in the order you will use it: paste data, name what a person and an item are, press Estimate. The results fill the right-hand side, Wright map first.

Nothing is saved automatically. Reloading the page loses your work. Use Save session (§12) before you close the tab.

The tag beside the title in the top bar says which model ran: dichotomous · JMLE for 0/1 data, or Andrich rating scale · 5 categories · JMLE for a scale. You do not choose; the tool reads it from the data.

2 · Loading data

The two demos

Badges loads a SODOTO badge ledger — 24 people, 12 skills, each cell 1 if the person holds the badge — and estimates it immediately. Likert loads a neighbourhood participation questionnaire — 140 residents, 7 statements, answered 1–5. Both are generated deterministically, so they are the same every time, and both have a deliberate flaw built in so that you can see what the tool does when it finds one (§13).

Your own CSV

Paste into the Data box. The first column is the person. Every other column is an item. One row per person, and the header row carries the item names — these become the labels on the map, so write them the way you want to read them. 

person,edit a wiki page,publish a page,stand up a server

CellMeaning
1 / 0Dichotomous: succeeded / did not. Badged / not badged. Yes / no.
0–3, 1–5, …A rating scale. Whole numbers. The tool takes the lowest value present as the bottom category and the highest as the top, and runs the Andrich model. A 1–5 scale is estimated as 0–4 and displayed as 1–5 — the shift is reported on the rating scale card, never applied silently.
emptyMissing. The person simply contributes no observation for that item. Missing is handled, not imputed, and a person with only some items answered still gets a measure — with a larger standard error.
anything elseMissing, loudly rather than coerced. A decimal, a negative number, a value above 20, or text that is not a number all become missing cells. A short row is padded with missing; extra cells are ignored. Check the N column (§9, §10) after estimating if you are not sure what was read.

Column order is item order in every table; row order does not matter. Item and person names may contain spaces and punctuation but not commas. If you have a name with a comma, wrap it in double quotes.

Where a badge ledger comes from. The SODOTO ledger is already persons × skills, dichotomous. Export it with one row per person and one column per badge, 1 where the badge is held, and it is ready to paste. No survey is needed; the observations already exist, and they were made by a mentor rather than reported by the person.

3 · Naming persons and items

Two fields under Labels: What a person is and What an item is. They default to person and skill. Change them and every heading, every note, and both axis labels on the Wright map change with them — resident and statement for a survey, learner and badge for a ledger, site and practice for an audit.

This is not cosmetic. A map labelled PERSONS — more able above and SKILLS — harder above can be read by a group in a room; one labelled theta and beta cannot. The demos set these for you; set them yourself for your own data.

4 · Estimating

Press Estimate. The tool parses the CSV, finds the lowest and highest values, removes any person or item with an extreme score (§11), and runs joint maximum likelihood estimation until the largest change in any estimate is below 0.0001 logits. On a few hundred persons this takes well under a second.

Two things stop it before it starts. If every cell holds the same value there is nothing to measure and the tool says so. If there is no header row, no data row, or no item column, the tool says which. A row with too few cells is not an error — the absent cells are missing.

At the bottom of the Does it hold together? card the tool reports whether estimation converged and in how many iterations. Both demos converge (16 and 28 iterations). If a run does not converge in 300, every number on the page is provisional and the card says so; the usual cause is a matrix with very few persons or a rating scale with a category almost nobody used.

5 · Reading the Wright map

The map is the first card and the one to read first. Persons are on the left of a vertical ruler, items on the right, and the ruler is marked in logits.

MarkMeaning
Blue dot, leftA person. Higher is more able. People within about a quarter-logit of each other are drawn on one row with their names listed beside them.
Amber bar, rightAn item. Higher is harder. Items too close to share a row are nudged apart and a thin grey lead line runs back to the item's true height on the ruler.
Red bar, rightAn item whose infit or outfit exceeds 1.5 — a misfit, labelled as such. See §9.
Hollow dashed dot or barAn extreme score, drawn above the top tick or below the bottom one, with no number. A person who succeeded at everything sits above every item; an item everybody passed sits below every person. See §11.
Dashed blue lineThe mean person measure.
Dashed amber lineThe mean item measure, which is zero by construction — items are centred to give the ruler an origin.

Read across, not down. An item level with a person is one that person has even odds on. An item one logit above a person is one they will succeed at roughly a quarter of the time; one logit below, three-quarters. Items well above everybody are out of reach for this group. Items well below everybody are universal and carry no information about differences between these people — they may still matter for another group.

The two questions the map answers at a glance, before any table is read: do the items reach the people? — is there an item near every cluster of persons, or are there gaps on the ruler where people sit with nothing to measure them by — and is anything red?

6 · Targeting

Under the map, a note reports the mean person measure — since items are centred at zero, this is the gap between where the people are and where the items are. Within ±0.8 logits the note is green: the items are aimed near where the people are. Beyond that the note says which way the gap runs and what to do about it.

TargetingMeaningRemedy
near 0Items reach the people. Most responses are informative.—
strongly positivePeople sit well above the items. The items are too easy for this group and mostly confirm what you already know.Add harder items.
strongly negativePeople sit well below the items. Most of the information is in whether anyone succeeded at all.Add easier items.

The badge demo reports −0.01; the Likert demo −0.19. Both are well targeted, which is not typical of real instruments on their first run.

7 · Does it hold together?

The second card reports how finely the instrument can cut, for persons and for items separately.

StatisticWhat it is
ReliabilityThe share of the spread in measures that is real rather than measurement error: (SD² − MSE) / SD². Runs 0 to 1. The same fact as separation, stated as a ratio. Not the same thing as Cronbach's alpha, though it plays the same role.
SeparationSpread of measures divided by the typical error: sqrt(reliability / (1 − reliability)). A separation of 2 means the measures spread twice as wide as they are uncertain.
StrataSeparation turned into a count of distinguishable levels: (4G + 1) / 3. This is the number in plain language.
SD (logits)Standard deviation of the person measures.
RMSERoot mean square of the person standard errors — the typical uncertainty in one person's measure.

Below the numbers a note says, in words, what the strata licenses. Three or more: enough to sort people into levels, not enough to rank them individually. Two: high from low and nothing finer. Fewer than two: this set of items cannot separate these people; treat any ranking as noise.

Neither reliability nor separation says whether the instrument is good. They say how finely it cuts. A dozen badges cannot cut finely and are not meant to. Reporting a rank order of individuals from an instrument with two or three strata is the standard misuse of a measure, and it is the one this card is there to stop.

Item reliability and separation are reported too. High item separation — the Likert demo has 9.57 — means the items are spread out along the ruler and the sample was large enough to place each one precisely. It says nothing about the people.

8 · The rating scale card

This card appears only when the data have more than two categories. The Andrich model estimates one set of thresholds shared across all items — that sharing is what makes it the rating-scale model rather than partial credit, and it is exactly the assumption a Likert questionnaire makes: every statement uses the same scale, so the categories mean the same thing everywhere.

A threshold is the point on the ruler where the next category up becomes the more likely answer than the one below it. The card shows one row per category with its count, its share of all responses, its threshold, and a reading.

ReadingMeaning
Used as expectedEnough observations and the threshold sits above the one beneath it.
DisorderedThis category's threshold sits below the one beneath it. There is no region of the ruler where the category below it is the most likely answer — respondents are not using the categories in the order they were written. Collapse the two adjacent categories and re-run.
Very thinFewer than 10 observations. The threshold rests on almost nothing; treat it as provisional.
Never usedZero observations. No threshold can be estimated. Collapse the category into a neighbour before reading anything else on the card.

Under the table a note gives the verdict for the scale as a whole — ordered and working as written, or disordered and which category. If your lowest category was not 0, a final line says what shift was applied for estimation; the category numbers on the card are always in your original numbering.

How to collapse categories. Edit the CSV: replace every 4 with 3 (or every 3 with 4 — the direction does not matter) so the scale becomes 1, 2, 3, 5. Re-estimate. The tool will read the gap as four categories and renumber for estimation; the card will show them in your numbering. Compare the thresholds before and after; the collapsed scale should now climb.
Spacing is approximate; sequence is reliable. JMLE spreads its estimates 10–20% wider than the truth with few items. Winsteps applies a correction factor; this tool does not. The disordering diagnostic depends only on the order of the thresholds, which the bias does not affect. And detecting a small disordering takes respondents: an earlier demo with 60 residents and a 0.2-logit reversal correctly reported the thresholds as ordered, because the reversal was smaller than the noise.

9 · The items table

Hardest first. One row per item.

ColumnMeaning
ScoreSum of responses to this item across measurable persons. For 0/1 data, the number who succeeded.
NHow many persons answered this item. Less than the number of persons if there are missing cells or extreme persons.
MeasureDifficulty in logits, on the same ruler as the persons. Items are centred to mean zero. An extreme item shows a grey pill — nobody or everybody — instead of a number.

Fit pills are coloured by the worse of infit and outfit:

Misfit is information. An item that misfits is measuring something else, and finding out what is usually the point. In the badge demo, run a campfire conversation reports infit 2.07 and outfit 11.61 — some of the least able people hold it and some of the most able do not — because the demo built it to depend on temperament rather than on the capability the other eleven skills share. Do not delete a misfitting item without looking at who succeeded on it and who did not; that pattern is the finding.

10 · The persons table

Most able first. Same columns as the items table, without a reading. Score is the person's raw score across measurable items, N is how many items they answered, Measure is ability in logits on the same ruler.

Persons with the same raw score on the same items get the same measure — the raw score is a sufficient statistic, and that is a property of the model, not a limitation of the tool. The same raw score on different items (because of missing cells) gives different measures.

A person with high outfit usually has one anomalous response — a miss on something easy, or a success on something well above their level. In the badge demo Ray holds eleven badges and misses one of the easiest, the campfire badge, for an outfit of 19.69. The question a high-outfit person raises is what happened on that one item, not whether the person is unreliable.

11 · Extreme scores

A person who succeeded at everything, or at nothing, has no finite measure: they are somewhere beyond the items and the data cannot say where. The same is true of an item everybody passed or nobody passed. The tool treats these four cases the same way.

  • They are excluded from estimation, iteratively — removing an extreme item can push a borderline person to an extreme score, so the check repeats until nothing changes.
  • They are listed in the tables with their raw score and a grey pill — maximum, minimum, everybody, nobody — and no measure, SE, or fit.
  • They are drawn on the map, as hollow dashed markers above the top tick or below the bottom one, with their names, and no number.
  • They enter nothing numeric: not the mean, not the reliability, not the exported measures.

Winsteps instead extrapolates a measure by treating a perfect score as a fractional one (EXTRSC, 0.3 by default). That gives a precise-looking number whose position depends on a setting rather than on data. This tool refuses it. A ceiling effect — a third of the group at the top — is one of the most useful things a Wright map can show, and it stays visible here as a pile-up of hollow markers; what it does not get is a fictional measure.

If many persons are extreme, the remedy is more items at the ends of the ruler. If many items are extreme, the remedy is more persons at the ends.

13 · The two worked examples

Badges — the misfit case

24 people, 12 SODOTO skills from comment on a page to stand up an NDC server. True abilities were spread evenly from −3 to +3.2 logits and true difficulties from −2.6 to +2.7; the responses were drawn from the model with a fixed seed. One skill, run a campfire conversation, was generated from a person's temperament instead of their ability.

14 · From measure to control chart

A total of ordinal responses is not on an interval scale, and charting it on a control chart is a known error: the limits assume equal intervals the data do not have. A Rasch measure is on an interval scale and can be charted. This is the intended path for RCN: badge ledger → Rasch measure → RCN Process Behavior Charts.

  1. Estimate the ledger. Check targeting, strata, and misfit. Set aside any item that misfits badly; it is measuring something else and should be charted separately if at all.
  2. Export measures CSV. Keep the person rows; drop the item rows and any row with a word in the extreme column.
  3. Chart the person measures in the SPC tool as one value per person per period, or the group mean per period. Read the subgrouping section of that manual before choosing which — the choice decides what the chart can find.
  4. Keep the item set fixed between periods. If items change, measures from different periods are not on the same ruler unless some items are shared; that is a linking problem this tool does not yet solve.

15 · What this tool does not do

  • Partial credit. Every item shares one set of thresholds. If different items genuinely use different scales, the rating-scale model is the wrong one and the thresholds will look muddled.
  • Many-facet Rasch. No rater facet. If different mentors award the same badge at different standards, that variation lands in the item fit rather than being measured.
  • Differential item functioning. No check for whether an item is harder for one subgroup than another. Run the subgroups separately and compare item measures by hand.
  • Dimensionality. No principal components of residuals. Multidimensionality shows up as misfit, which is the model doing its job, but the tool will not tell you how many dimensions there are.
  • Anchoring and linking. No way to fix item measures from a previous run. Every estimate is fresh.
  • Extreme-score extrapolation and JMLE bias correction. Deliberately absent; see §8 and §11.

All are legitimate; several are important. They are absent because every addition is something a co-op board has to learn, and the first version should be the version the board can read. The estimator underneath has been verified by parameter recovery on simulated data — correlation 0.995 dichotomous and 0.999 polytomous, RMS error about 0.2 logits — so any of these can be added on top of it when a real need appears.

16 · Formulas

Named so they can be checked against Winsteps. B is person ability, D is item difficulty, both in logits.

Dichotomous model

P(x = 1 | B, D) = exp(B − D) / (1 + exp(B − D))

Andrich rating-scale model

P(x = k) ∝ exp( k(B − D) − Σj=1..k Fj ),   F0 = 0,  Σ Fj = 0

Categories 0..m. Thresholds F shared across items. With m = 1 this reduces exactly to the dichotomous model; the tool has one estimator and the equivalence was verified to 1e-15 before the separate dichotomous code was deleted.

Estimation

JMLE (UCON). Log-odds start. Newton-Raphson alternately on person and item margins; items recentred to mean zero each pass; steps clamped to ±2 logits; convergence when the largest change is below 0.0001, up to 300 iterations. Thresholds updated from the count of responses reaching each category or above, then recentred to sum to zero. Extreme persons and items are removed iteratively before estimation, and the margins are recomputed over the surviving submatrix each pass so that observed and expected scores agree at the solution.

Standard error

SE = 1 / sqrt( Σ Var(x) )

where the sum runs over the responses that person (or item) contributed and Var(x) is the model variance of each response — P(1 − P) in the dichotomous case.

Fit

infit = Σ (x − E)² / Σ Var(x)     outfit = mean( (x − E)² / Var(x) )

E is the expected response. Infit is information-weighted; outfit is not. The standardised versions (infit_zstd, outfit_zstd in the export) use the Wilson-Hilferty cube-root transform.

Separation

reliability = (SD² − MSE) / SD²    G = sqrt( (SD² − MSE) / MSE )    strata = (4G + 1) / 3

SD is the standard deviation of the measures and MSE the mean squared standard error, both over non-extreme persons (or items).
