We already have good ways to draw cause (the Graph Tool) and space (the Map). Time was the dimension left. RCN Timeline treats a moment in time the same way those tools treat a node or a parcel: as a first-class object you can place, relate, share, and reason over — not a decoration on a chart.

The third member of the family

Each RCN tool is the same shape underneath: regions plus typed relations. Time turns out to be exactly that kind of object — an interval on a line, with a small, closed vocabulary of relations between intervals.

What makes it different

It isn't a Gantt chart and it isn't a slideshow timeline. Four ideas set it apart:

  • Fuzzy
    Time is allowed to be uncertain. Every span has a soft, fading edge — an event you're sure happened in 2018 but whose exact start you're guessing shows a widening band, not a fake-crisp bar. History is fuzzy; the tool says so.
  • Relational
    You don't have to know the dates. Say "the pilot was before the grant" and "the grant meets the scale-up," and a solver places everything from the relations. Group knowledge composes into something tighter than any one person knew.
  • Movable
    Drag to plan and forecast. Floating events slide within their constraints; rigid links haul their neighbors along; slack is visible. Push a task past a hard deadline and it lights up red — the timeline polices itself.
  • Dual face
    Say it or drag it — same assertion. Typing "Report = Jan 2026 .. Jun 2026" and dragging a bar's edge are two views of one underlying fact.
Accuracy is a group activity. Nobody asserts the date; everyone contributes bounds they can defend, each carrying who said it and on what evidence. Contributions only narrow the uncertainty — so refining a timeline is a barn-raising, not a turf war. When two claims genuinely conflict, the tool points at the exact disagreement instead of averaging it away.

What it looks like

Fuzzy interval bars

A solid "certain" core with soft shoulders that fade in and out. Instants show as a point.

Pinned vs. floating

📌 Pinned facts are locked to real dates; floating events are placed by the solver and nudged by hand.

Real calendar dates

Everything reads as Mon D, YYYY — never a decimal — with an axis that shifts from years to months to days as you zoom.

Before & meets links

Loose "before" links show slack; rigid "meets" links weld end-to-start and move as a block.

Contradiction flags

Push something past a pinned deadline and the link, the object, and an issues list turn red.

Provenance on everything

Who asserted it, the evidence, and a confidence level — shown on hover, tinting how solid the bar looks.

How it compares

Gantt / project tools (MS Project, Asana)Overlaps

They have dependencies and slack, but every task needs a crisp start date and there's no notion of uncertainty or evidence. Built for a manager's plan, not for a group reconstructing what's true.

TimelineJS / Sutori (story timelines)Different

Beautiful for presenting a fixed sequence of dated events. But they're display-only — nothing is movable, relational, uncertain, or collaboratively refined.

Spreadsheets & hand-drawn timelinesDifferent

Infinitely flexible and completely un-computable. No solver, no contradiction checking, no shared object you can link to.

RCN TimelineThe gap it fills

Fuzzy + relational + movable + provenanced + linkable — a computable time object that both plans the future and reconstructs the past, and that a group sharpens together.

The pairing: trace the logic, then trace the time

The Timeline is the temporal sibling of the RCN Graph Tool, and using the two in sequence is expected to become the common path: see and discuss the logic, then see and discuss the effects across time.

In the Graph Tool, a trace tags a path of edges and animates it outward from its roots. That animation is a partial order — it says this, then this, then this. It runs at a fixed speed in milliseconds, so a step that took a week and a step that took four years look exactly alike.

Carry the same trace here and every step acquires a date, a duration, a gap, and a confidence. The payoff is an argument the graph cannot make: two edges leaving the same node are drawn identically on a graph, but on a timeline one may be welded to its cause while the other trails it by two years and two months — long enough that everyone had declared the work a success and stopped watching. A causal claim with years of slack is a different claim from one with a week of slack, and only the timeline shows which you have.

Filling in the durations is itself the useful part: it is the step where a group discovers which of its confident causal arrows nobody can actually date.

Share what you make

Every object can produce a self-contained link that reopens the exact timeline and highlights the object you were looking at — paste it into a document, a chat, or a FedWiki page. You can also export the picture as SVG or PNG, and the whole model as JSON. Bringing a timeline back in has its own Import button, which checks a file and reports what it finds before anything loads.

Where this is going

v0 is a working prototype meant for play. Next comes richer historical dating (“circa 1200,” “the 1830s,” “unknown”), branching scenarios for “what-if” planning, forecaster calibration that turns good predictions into earned standing, and bridges so a causal node in the Graph Tool or a parcel on the Map can carry its own interval — one timeline layer beneath all three tools.
