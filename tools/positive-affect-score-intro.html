Positive Affect Score

Introduction — what it measures, what it does not, and where it is used

Document updated: August 2026
Introduction User Manual Open the Tool ↗ Conversation Navigator ↗
Ten words, each rated one to five, adding to a score between 10 and 50. That is the whole instrument. It takes under a minute and it gives a group or a person one honest number for how much positive engagement is actually present — at this moment, or across the past week.

Why a separate tool

The ten positive-affect words of the PANAS were already embedded in two RCN tools — the Shared Care Plan, where the score paces how much of a care record is shown at once, and My Health Picture. Both used it as a means to an end, and neither let a person simply take the check-in, keep a history, and look at the trend.

Pulling it out into its own tool does three things. It gives the check-in a life of its own, outside any health record. It makes one file the canonical definition of the item list and the scoring, so the copies embedded in the other tools cannot quietly drift apart. And it lets the same instrument be used in settings that have nothing to do with health — a working session, a class, a board meeting.

The ten words

ActiveAlertAttentiveDeterminedEnthusiastic ExcitedInspiredInterestedProudStrong

An optional second set of ten measures negative affect — afraid, ashamed, distressed, guilty, hostile, irritable, jittery, nervous, scared, upset. It is scored separately and never subtracted from the first. Positive and negative affect are close to independent; a person can be high on both at once.

The timeframe is the whole design

The same ten words answer two quite different questions depending on the window you put around them, and the tool makes you choose before you start.

FramingWhat it readsWhat it is for
Right now State affect — this moment. Moves hour to hour, and is supposed to. Before and after a meeting; tracking a day; anywhere the change matters more than the level.
Past week Closer to a trait reading — your general level lately. Much steadier. Health check-ins, where a stable reading is what the record needs.

Every saved result records which framing produced it, and the tool charts the two separately. A right-now score and a past-week score are not comparable, and putting them on one line would invent a trend that is not there.

Bands are past-week only, and are not a clinical result

A past-week score maps to one of four activation bands — Learning the basics, Building awareness, Taking action, Staying the course — using a provisional crosswalk borrowed from patient-activation practice. That crosswalk is not validated, it is calibrated for the past-week framing only, and it is never shown for a right-now score. It is a starting view for a care conversation, not a diagnosis and not a judgment about a person.

Where it is used

On its own

Take it, keep a history in your own browser, watch the trend, export JSON or CSV. Nothing leaves the machine.

In a shared session

Everyone opens one link and takes it. The group sees the distribution and the mean — not a ranking.

Conversation Navigator

A right-now check-in sits beside intent and bias self-reports. Retake it at the end to see how the room moved.

Shared Care Plan

A past-week reading sets how much of the care record is shown at once. The full record is always there.

What it is not

It is not a screening instrument, and a low score is not a finding. It does not measure mood in general, wellbeing, or life satisfaction — those are different constructs with different instruments. It cannot be compared across people as a ranking; the useful comparisons are a person against their own earlier score, and a group against its own earlier distribution.

Above all, the four bands are not a description of a person. They exist to pace a health record, and they have been deliberately kept out of the Conversation Navigator for exactly that reason.

Position

Most group tooling measures what was said. This measures what was present. One number, taken twice, says something a transcript cannot: whether the people in the room had more capacity at the end than they had at the start.

It is small on purpose. Ten words is a thing a group will actually do twice in a meeting, which is what makes the change visible at all.
