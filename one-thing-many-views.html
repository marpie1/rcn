A short walk through an idea

One thing,
many views.

This is a project that keeps producing diagrams — timelines, flowcharts, loops, matrices — of what looks like different subjects. It isn't. They are one object, read five ways. Here is what that means, using a single small example you can watch change without anything being added or taken away.

i. — Start with one small picture

Six things, and the arrows between them.

Imagine a neighborhood. Something isn't working — call it a problem. The problem stirs up motivation. A person feels that motivation and takes an action. The action produces a result, and it happens inside some organization — a co-op, a council, a church group.

That's it. Six things, a handful of arrows showing what leads to what. You could sketch it on a napkin. Everything that follows is this same little sketch — nothing more.

ii. — The same sketch, read five ways

Each reader sees a different thing in the same place.

Here's the surprising part. The little sketch quietly carries more than one kind of information at once. Every arrow knows several things about itself: the order events happen in, whether one thing pushes another up or down, how strongly, who first drew it. Most pictures only show one of these at a time — and that's exactly why the same sketch can look like five unrelated diagrams.

iii. — The turn

Nothing was added. Nothing was removed.

Go back and check. The timeline, the loop, the grid, the gold-flecked map, the family boxes — not one of them introduced a new thing or dropped an old one. Each simply lit up a different layer of information the sketch was already carrying and dimmed the rest.

This is the whole idea, and it's worth saying slowly: they were never five different diagrams of five different things. They were five readings of one thing. The disagreements between them aren't contradictions to resolve — they're just different questions being asked of the same object.

A timeline and a feedback loop can look nothing alike and still be the same sketch — because one is reading the order and the other is reading the direction.

Once you see it here, you start seeing it everywhere. A subway map and a street map of the same city look completely different and neither is wrong. A song's sheet music and its waveform are the same performance, read two ways. The trick this project is built on is simply refusing to throw away the other readings when you draw one of them.

iv. — Why it's built this way

One shared object, and a set of lenses.

Most tools force a choice up front. A timeline tool throws away everything that isn't order. A diagram tool throws away everything that isn't a picture. Draw your neighborhood in one, and the moment you want to ask a different kind of question, you're starting over — re-drawing, and quietly losing what the first drawing knew.

The alternative is to keep one underlying object that holds all the layers at once, and treat each tool as a lens that knows which layer to read and which to ignore. The object is written once. The lenses are many. Here is what each layer is for:

Its kind
What a thing is — the plain word for it. This is the identity: it's how two people's separate drawings recognize that they meant the same thing.
Its name
What a person calls it — "seriousness of the problem" rather than just "problem." This is the human layer; it's what makes the loop and the grid actually mean something to a reader.
Its direction
Push or pull — does more of this cause more, or less, of that. This is what lets the sketch be simulated — run forward to see how it behaves.
Its strength
How hard one thing moves another. This is what sorts the parts into levers and symptoms, so you know where a small push does the most good.
Its timing
What comes before what. This is the join across iterations — how one turn of the loop hands off to the next.
Its source
Who drew it. This is the join across people — the layer that lets many hands build one shared map and shows where they already agree.

That last one is quietly the most important, because it's what makes the whole thing a commons rather than one person's model. When two contributors — a coffee shop owner downtown, an organizer three towns over — independently draw the same idea, the object merges them and marks the shared node gold. Nobody decided in advance that it mattered. The agreement surfaced. Gold is what shared ground looks like when it's discovered rather than declared.

—

So: not a pile of diagrams. One object, patiently learning to be seen from every side.

The timelines and loops and grids aren't the work. They're the views. The work is the single shared thing underneath — an object that a whole neighborhood, or a hundred neighborhoods, can draw together, each person adding the layer they understand best, no drawing ever cancelling another.

A person who knows when things happen adds timing. A person who knows what pushes what adds direction. A person who just knows their own street adds a place on the map. And because it's all one object, every lens gets richer each time anyone contributes — through any door, in whatever language they already speak.

That's the bet: build the thing once, and let everyone see it their own way.
