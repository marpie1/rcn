# The Group Of Five

Wright treats composition as a property a group *has*. The more useful reading, and the one Marc was reaching for in the margin, is that composition is a repertoire a group *learns* — and that a standing group of about five, cycling through all three modes as the problem in front of it changes, will develop two things without being taught either.

The first is fluency in all three modes. The second, and the one that matters more, is Follett's participation: the whole in every part and every part in the whole, arrived at as synthesis rather than compromise.

## Why five

Wright supplies no number, so this is speculation checked against his arithmetic rather than derived from it. What the arithmetic does say is that five is roughly where all three modes are simultaneously *practicable*.

A pack of five is worth `+ln 5 = +1.61` logits — enough to take on a problem a good distance above the group's mean ability, which is the whole point of packing. A pack of three is worth only `+1.10`, which is thin; you would rarely reach for it.

A team of five needs to average only `+0.40` logits over its problem to be the right choice. That is a reachable bar on work the group has already standardised.

A chain of five costs `−1.61` logits, which is bad but survivable, and five links is a realistic length — it is the length of an actual referral pathway. At ten the penalty is `−2.30` and chain practice becomes mostly a lesson in failure. At ten, too, a team must keep every one of ten members above `D`, which almost never holds.

So below five the pack is too weak to be worth running, and above five the chain and the team stop being genuinely available. Five is where a group can really do all three.

There is a second reason. The gap between the best and worst mode is `2 ln N` — at five, 3.22 logits. That is large enough that choosing the wrong composition visibly hurts, and therefore large enough to learn from, while the group is still small enough that every member occupies every position in every mode.

## What each mode teaches

This is the part that does the real work, and it is why cycling produces Follett rather than merely producing competence.

**A Pack teaches that the parts make up the whole.** Its rule requires each member to try something no one else is trying. Every person spends the pack phase being a genuinely distinct part, holding a piece nobody else holds, and watching the group's outcome assembled out of those separate pieces. Failure costs nothing, so the distinctness is safe.

**A Chain teaches that the whole is in every part.** Its rule is that one break loses everything. Every person spends the chain phase carrying the entire outcome, because the entire outcome does in fact live in each link. Nobody can be a fraction of the result; each is the whole of it.

**A Team is where the two arrive together.** Nothing happens until all agree, so the decision is at once one thing the group holds and something every member holds entirely. That is not compromise, which splits the difference between parts, and not consensus in the thin sense of everyone assenting. It is Follett's synthesis, and it is only available to a group that has already had both of the other experiences.

Stated plainly: **Follett's two clauses are one mode each, and the third mode is where they resolve.** Parts making up the whole is the Pack. The whole in every part is the Chain. Synthesis is the Team — and a Team that has not been a Pack and a Chain has no materials to synthesise, which is another way of saying it is a mob.

## What follows for practice

A neighborhood team should be composed once, at about five, and then *re-wired* repeatedly rather than re-staffed. The unit of design is the mode, not the membership.

Rotation matters. If the same person is always the first link, or always the one whose idea the pack adopts, the group learns the modes but not the participation — some members will have experienced only being a part and others only carrying the whole. Everyone occupies every position, which at five is arithmetically possible and at twelve is not.

The switch itself should be explicit and named. "We are packing this" and "this is a chain, so keep it short" are the two most useful sentences available to a standing group, and they are only sayable once the modes have names.

And the sequence is not optional. [[Freedom Participation Solidarity]] sets out why: Pack → Chain → Team is the path participation has to walk, and each shortcut has a pathology. The group of five is the smallest vehicle that can actually walk it.

This is where [[Discovering Standard Processes]] and this page meet. A group of five cycling the modes is simultaneously running the discovery loop — packing at hard problems, standardising what worked, chaining the handoffs, teaming on what has been made easy — and building the mutual understanding that lets it do so without anyone being coerced. The loop produces capability; the cycling produces the participation that makes the capability legitimate.
