# Packs Chains And Teams

In the 1920s the physicist Norman Campbell argued that you cannot measure a social variable, because you cannot concatenate people. Length is measurable because sticks lie end to end. Weight is measurable because bricks pile up. People, Campbell said, do no such thing, and so social science has no metre stick.

Benjamin Wright's answer, seventy years later, was that people are concatenated constantly — we just do it under three different rules, and each rule has its own arithmetic. Once you write the arithmetic down, group organisation stops being a matter of temperament and becomes something you can calculate.

## The three rules

A **Team** wins when everyone agrees and the agreement is right. A two-member team has only two outcomes available to it, (11) and (00) — the rules of Team behaviour eliminate disagreement entirely. Juries work this way. So do committees, steering groups, and every consensus body ever convened.

A **Pack** wins when any single member succeeds. Members are supposed to try different things; helpful disagreements (10) and (01) are what the Pack runs on, and (11) is eliminated because two people doing the same thing is not Pack behaviour. Search parties work this way. So does the immune system, and so does Darwinian evolution.

A **Chain** loses when any single member fails. Perfect agreement (11) wins, either disagreement loses, and (00) cannot occur. Referral pathways work this way. So do supply chains, hand-offs, and every process where one dropped ball loses the whole thing.

## The arithmetic that follows

Applying Rasch model odds to each rule gives three laws. Write Bn for a person's ability, D for the difficulty of the problem, and N for the number of people.

**Team:** `BT − D = Σ (Bn − D)`. Strength *relative to the task*, accumulated linearly. Task difficulty enters the sum N times. A member more able than the problem adds; a member less able than the problem subtracts, by their full deficit. Wright's dry note: more is often not better, as has been frequently observed about committees.

**Pack:** `BP = B̄ + ln(N·W)`. Absolute strength, accumulated exponentially. Task difficulty does not appear at all. Adding a member always helps. The `W` term rises with the spread of member abilities, so heterogeneity helps too.

**Chain:** `BC = B̄ − ln N`. Weakness accumulated exponentially. Task difficulty does not appear here either. Adding a link always hurts. There is no length at which a chain stops bleeding.

## What the laws do to the same ten people

Wright's own example. Ten people, each 1 logit *below* the difficulty of the task in front of them.

As a Team they are `10 × (−1) = −10.0` logits. As a Chain, `−1 − ln 10 = −3.3`. As a Pack, `−1 + ln 10 = +1.3`.

The same ten people, facing the same problem, are catastrophic, mediocre, or sufficient — depending on nothing but how they are wired together. That is the whole claim, and it is worth sitting with before going further, because almost every convening decision in community health is made without anyone asking which of the three is being built.
