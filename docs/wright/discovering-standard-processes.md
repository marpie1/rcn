# Discovering Standard Processes

At the foot of Wright's 1996 page there is a sentence: "Only when problems can be made easy, because we have become better able to manage them, can Team-work succeed." Marc's margin, in red, glosses it: **discovering standard processes**.

That gloss is the most valuable thing on the page, and it is not Wright's. Wright treats "problems become easy" as something that simply happens. Naming the mechanism turns a description into a control loop.

## The loop

A **Pack** goes out at a problem harder than the people facing it. Many attempts, deliberately different, first success wins, failures cost nothing.

**What worked** is one member's success. It is still an anecdote. It has lowered `D` for nobody.

A **standard process** is that success named, written and taught. This is the only lever anyone actually has, because you cannot raise `B` quickly and you cannot choose an easier world. Standardising is how `D` comes down.

With `D` down, the work is specifiable, so it can be handed off — a **Chain**, kept as short as the arithmetic demands. And with `D` below the group's average `B`, consensus finally becomes the strongest composition: a **Team**, which is a *reward* for having made the problem easy, not a way of attacking it.

The **control chart** is what tells you whether the problem is still easy. A process in control is the empirical signature of a `D` that has stayed down. A **special cause** says `D` has risen or `B` has fallen, the group is no longer entitled to Team-work, and the loop reopens.

## Why this is an SPC argument

Pack → Chain → Team is not a maturity model that a group climbs once. It is a control loop, and statistical process control is its instrumentation.

This connects Wright to a practice already in the toolset rather than adding a new one. The rational subgroup and Wright's composition analysis are the same argument in two dialects: **aggregation is not neutral, and the wrong aggregation destroys the signal.** A control chart drawn over a Pack and a control chart drawn over a Chain are charting different variables even when the raw counts are identical.

The uncomfortable corollary, in Wright's own words, is that a Team working a problem that has not been made easy is a mob. Most healthcare committees are exactly that: consensus bodies convened against problems far above their `B̄`, in the one region where the arithmetic says consensus performs worst of all three.

## SODOTO is the other blade

Standardising lowers `D`. Teaching raises `B`. Both close the same gap, and See One / Do One / Teach One is how a standard process actually propagates — an unwritten, untaught practice lowers `D` for nobody.

That reframes SODOTO usefully. It does not organise the group. It moves `B`, and moving `B` is what changes which composition is optimal. It is the mechanism by which a Pack earns the right to become a Team.

## RenDanHeYi, exactly here

Wright's closing practical note is that productive consensus-building "requires that each difficult task be divided into several easy tasks, so that there is a high probability that each group member, acting alone, would have made the consensus-decision."

That is 单 — the concrete single order — sized so that Team-work is genuinely the optimal composition rather than an aspiration. Haier's zero-distance is the Chain-shortening move standing next to it. Both are the same operation Wright describes: get `D` under `B̄` before asking anyone to agree.
