# Ostrom Design Principles And Agreement Coding

For each of Ostrom's design principles: what it means in plain words, what in an agreement's coding counts as evidence, the question the Agreement Checker would ask, and what has to come from practice instead. The idea behind this page is on [[Commons Viability From Agreement Coding]]. A first try on a garden bylaw is on [[Garden Bylaw Viability Profile]].

The numbering follows Ostrom (1990) as refined by Cox, Arnold and Villamayor Tomás (2010), who split principles 1, 2 and 4 in two.

## At a glance

| Principle | Plain words | Readable from the text? |

| 1A User boundaries | Everyone knows who is in | Yes |
| 1B Resource boundaries | Everyone knows what the shared thing is | Yes |
| 2A Fit with local conditions | The rules suit this place | No |
| 2B Costs in proportion to benefits | What you put in matches what you take | Partly |
| 3 Collective choice | The people affected help make the rules | Yes |
| 4A Monitoring users | Someone watches how the rules are kept, and answers to the users | Yes |
| 4B Monitoring the resource | Someone watches the shared thing itself | Yes |
| 5 Graduated sanctions | Consequences start small and grow | Yes |
| 6 Conflict resolution | There is a cheap, local way to settle disputes | Yes |
| 7 Recognition of rights | Outside authorities let the group make its own rules | Partly |
| 8 Nested enterprises | Larger systems are built of smaller self-governing ones | Partly |

## 1A · User boundaries

Plain words: everyone knows who is in and who is out.

Evidence in the coding: a creating agreement whose WHAT ARE WE CREATING? names the members, and whose FORMED? and OUT OF WHAT? say how someone becomes one. "Anyone who holds a plot counts as a gardener." Also rules whose WHO is a defined group rather than "anyone".

The checker asks: "This assumes everyone knows who counts as a gardener. Nothing here says." It already asks this today.

From practice: whether people outside the boundary actually stay out.

## 1B · Resource boundaries

Plain words: everyone knows what the shared thing is and where it ends.

Evidence in the coding: a creating agreement that defines the resource: the tank, the plots, the fishing grounds, the shared fund. Also TO WHAT? answers in the rules that point at a defined thing rather than a vague one.

The checker would ask: "These rules are about the water, but nothing says what the shared water is. Which tank, which taps?"

From practice: whether the boundary on paper matches the boundary on the ground.

## 2A · Fit with local conditions

Plain words: the rules suit this place, this resource, and these people.

Evidence in the coding: none that can be trusted. The text can mention local things, but whether the rules fit is a judgment about the place.

The checker would ask nothing. The profile says "can't tell from the text."

From practice: everything. Ask the people who live under the rules, and watch the resource over time.

## 2B · Costs in proportion to benefits

Plain words: what each person puts in matches what they take out.

Evidence in the coding: pairs of sentences. A permission or benefit, "Gardeners may water on their assigned days", matched by a duty with the same WHO, "Gardeners must give four work hours a season." Proportion shows when the duty scales with the benefit: hours per plot, dues per share.

The checker would ask: "Gardeners get water, but no sentence asks anything of them in return. Is that the agreement?"

From practice: whether the contributions are actually made, and whether people think the balance is fair.

## 3 · Collective choice

Plain words: the people affected by the rules help make and change them.

Evidence in the coding: collective-choice rules, whose DO WHAT? is amend, vote, decide, propose, or choose, and whose WHO includes the people the operational rules apply to. "Gardeners may propose a change to these rules at the spring meeting." "A change passes when two thirds of gardeners present agree."

The checker would ask: "Nothing here says how these rules can be changed, or by whom."

From practice: whether people affected actually take part, and whether changes they ask for happen.

## 4A · Monitoring users

Plain words: someone watches whether the rules are kept, and that someone answers to the users.

Evidence in the coding: a duty whose DO WHAT? is monitor, check, inspect, record, or report, held by a role. Then a creating agreement for that role whose OUT OF WHAT? is the users themselves, or a rule that the role reports to them.

The checker would ask: "The water keeper suspends watering for rule breakers, but no sentence says anyone watches for them, or who the keeper reports to."

From practice: whether the watching happens, and whether it is trusted.

## 4B · Monitoring the resource

Plain words: someone watches the condition of the shared thing itself.

Evidence in the coding: a duty to measure, read, or record the resource, held by a role. Also triggers: a WHEN? such as "when the tank is below half" assumes someone reads the tank.

The checker would ask: "This rule switches on when the tank is below half. Who reads the tank, and how does everyone find out?"

From practice: the measurements themselves, kept over time. This is where SPC charts belong.

## 5 · Graduated sanctions

Plain words: consequences start small and grow with how serious or repeated the breach is.

Evidence in the coding: several OR ELSE sentences for the same rule, with WHEN? answers that step up: "for a first breach, a warning"; "for a second, one week without watering"; "for a third, the plot is reassigned."

The checker would ask: "Every breach gets the same consequence. Does a first slip get the same as a third?"

From practice: whether the consequences are applied, and applied evenly.

## 6 · Conflict resolution

Plain words: there is a cheap, quick, local way to settle disputes, including disputes about the rules.

Evidence in the coding: a creating agreement for a venue (a mediator, a meeting, a council) and a rule that says who MAY bring a dispute to it and when.

The checker would ask: "If a gardener thinks a suspension was unfair, where do they take it?"

From practice: whether disputes get taken there, and settled.

## 7 · Recognition of rights

Plain words: governments and landowners outside the group respect the group's right to make its own rules.

Evidence in the coding: partial at best. A group's own text may name its lease, charter, or permit. The recognition itself lives in someone else's statements: the city's ordinance, the landowner's lease. Coding those outside statements too would make this readable.

The checker would ask: "These rules depend on the garden's right to the land. What gives the garden that right?"

From practice: whether outsiders override the group's rules, and how often.

## 8 · Nested enterprises

Plain words: when the commons is part of something larger, governance is layered, each layer self-governing and linked to the next.

Evidence in the coding: creating agreements that link the group to a larger body: "The garden sends one delegate to the neighborhood council." Also rules whose WHO is a body at another level.

The checker would ask, only when other levels exist: "These rules mention the neighborhood council. What links the garden to it?"

From practice: whether the layers actually talk to each other.

## Sorting by level

Every rule can also be sorted by what its action is about. Operational: doing the work. Collective choice: making or changing the operational rules. Constitutional: deciding who makes rules and how. A sentence's DO WHAT? usually tells which. A profile should report how many sentences sit at each level, because principle 3 depends on there being any collective-choice rules at all.
