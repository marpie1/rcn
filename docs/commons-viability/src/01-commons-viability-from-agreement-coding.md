# Commons Viability From Agreement Coding

Elinor Ostrom asked why some commons last for centuries while others collapse in a generation. Her answer was eight design principles: patterns that long-lasting commons share. Most of them are about the rules a group makes for itself. So if a group's agreements are coded with Ostrom's own grammar, part of the answer to "will this commons last?" can be read straight from the coding.

This page sets out the idea and its limits. The crosswalk itself is on [[Ostrom Design Principles And Agreement Coding]]. A first try, on a real-looking garden bylaw, is on [[Garden Bylaw Viability Profile]].

## The coding

Every agreement sentence answers a small set of questions. A rule about what people do answers WHEN?, WHO?, MUST, MAY, OR MUST NOT?, DO WHAT?, TO WHAT?, HOW?, and OR ELSE?. A creating agreement, which makes a role, group, or thing, answers WHAT ARE WE CREATING?, MUST OR MAY?, FORMED?, and OUT OF WHAT?. These are Ostrom's Institutional Grammar (IG 2.0) in plain words, and the Agreement Checker fills them in from ordinary sentences.

Once an agreement is coded, its answers can be searched. Is there any sentence whose action is "monitor" or "inspect"? Does any OR ELSE step up for a second offence? Who MAY change the rules, and are the people affected among them? Each of these is a design principle, asked of the coding.

## What the coding can show

Seven of the eleven design principles leave a clear trace in an agreement's text: boundaries, the people affected making the rules, monitoring of users and of the resource, graduated sanctions, and a way to resolve conflict. Three leave a partial trace. One, whether the rules fit local conditions, cannot be read from the text at all.

The coding can also sort every sentence by Ostrom's levels of action. Operational rules are about doing the work: water, pay, harvest. Collective-choice rules are about making and changing those rules: amend, vote, decide. Constitutional rules are about how the rule-makers are chosen and how they decide. A commons with operational rules and nothing above them can follow its rules but cannot change them, and that shows plainly in the coding.

A second sort uses Ostrom's seven rule types: boundary, position, choice, aggregation, information, payoff, and scope. Each rule's action says which type it is. The NDC School page [[Seven Questions]] puts these types in plain words. How the grammar, the rule types, the levels, and the design principles fit together is on [[How The Ostrom Frames Fit Together]].

## What the coding cannot show

The coding records the rules on paper. A commons lives or dies by the rules people actually use. Ostrom's own fieldwork showed written rules ignored and unwritten rules obeyed, again and again. So a profile built from coding is a structural screen. It can say "these rules have no monitoring in them." It cannot say whether anyone watches the water.

The evidence of use has to come from somewhere else: what people do, measured over time with SPC charts, asked in surveys such as the protection survey, or seen at the site. The most useful picture puts the two side by side, on paper and in use, principle by principle.

## How a profile should read

The output is a profile with one line per design principle. The lines are not added up into a score. Each line says one of four things: present, partial, absent, or can't tell from the text. Each says why, in plain words, and points to the sentences it read.

The design principles go with commons that last; they do not guarantee it. A profile with every line present is a good sign. It does not promise the commons will last. A profile with gaps is a list of conversations the group has not had yet.

The coding is only as good as the coder. The Agreement Checker's guesses are heuristic, and the group's corrections are what make the data trustworthy. Coding done by the people who live under the rules is slower and better.

## What it would take

One coding schema shared by every RCN tool: the Graph Tool, the database behind it, FedWiki pages, and the Agreement Checker. Then any agreement written anywhere carries its coding with it. A proposal is on [[Shared Shape For Coded Agreements]].

A viability lens that reads that coding and writes the profile in plain words, for example: "Nobody affected can change these rules."

Evidence from practice beside it, so a group sees what it wrote and what it does on the same page.
