# Seven Questions

Elinor Ostrom's working rule types, put as questions a nine-year-old can ask of any situation — a town council, a grazing permit, a family, a basketball game, a project crew.

## The questions

- Boundary. Who is in and who is out. How do you get in. How do you get out.
- Position. What roles exist here. Roles rather than people, because the role outlives the person.
- Choice. What is each role allowed to do, required to do, forbidden to do.
- Aggregation. How do we decide. Who has to agree for it to count.
- Information. What has to be shared, with whom, and when. What may be kept quiet.
- Payoff. Who bears the cost and who gets the benefit. What happens if you break it.
- Scope. What outcomes are we allowed to affect. What is off limits.

Aggregation is the one to install first and hardest, from about age six, at the level of who picks the game. Most conflict is two people silently assuming different aggregation rules. A tool that takes one decision, one set of positions, and shows what each aggregation rule would have produced from the same inputs teaches more about politics than a year of the subject.

## The three levels

Operational rules govern day-to-day action in an arena — who takes water, who repairs the fence, who speaks, who gets the permit. Changing one is cheap and frequent.

Collective-choice rules say who is entitled to change the operational rules, and by what procedure. Council votes, board policy, permit conditions, a union contract, the bylaws of a water co-op.

Constitutional rules say who is entitled to change the collective-choice rules. Charters, a district's authorising law, a land exchange statute, treaty status, incorporation.

Beneath all three is the meta-constitutional level — culture, what people believe is even thinkable. It is unwritten, which is why it is the strongest.

The same seven rule types operate at every level, which is what makes the questions general rather than a civics lesson. And the gap between rules in form and rules in use is the actual subject matter, findable only by observation — sitting in the room, walking the allotment, asking the clerk. That is the part no online course can do and a Superior school can do trivially.

Mapped to Donella Meadows: operational sits at leverage points twelve through seven, collective-choice at six and five, constitutional at four through one. That tells you which tool to reach for at each level — causal loop and stock and flow diagrams operationally, rule maps at collective-choice, dialogue and constitutions at the top.

## The formal upgrade

For older students, the ADICO grammar from Crawford and Ostrom. Every rule statement decomposes into Attribute, who it applies to; Deontic, may or must or must not; aIm, the action; Condition, when and where; and Or-else, the sanction.

The investigative payoff is immediate. In real documents the or-else slot is almost always empty. A statement with no or-else is not a rule, it is a norm or a shared strategy. So the first mechanical move on any ordinance, permit, policy or charter is to find the or-else. No or-else, no enforcement, and the rules in use will drift immediately and predictably. A student with a highlighter and ADICO can work through a town code in an afternoon and find things a reporter would miss. It also makes rules machine-comparable, which is what a rule map needs.

## Mapping rules rather than actors

Every stakeholder map ever drawn puts people in circles. Nobody draws the sediment the people leave behind.

A rule graph has rules as nodes. An ordinance, a permit condition, a statute rider, a board policy, a deed restriction, a union clause, an unwritten we-always-do-it-this-way. The edges are authorises, constrains, delegates, supersedes, exempts, funds, triggers, sunsets. Three horizontal bands hold the three levels, and an edge crossing bands upward is a rule reaching above its station, which is the interesting case.

Four properties make it more than a picture.

- Provenance. Every rule carries who made it, at which level, and when, so the rule graph and the timeline are one object seen two ways. Rules have birthdays and most have no death date.
- Two kinds of effect edge, in two colours. Intended effect, taken from the rule's own preamble, and observed effect, taken from measurement. A rule whose observed edges do not overlap its intended edges has drifted. A rule with intended edges and no observed edges was never enforced. A rule with observed edges nobody mentioned is the unintended consequence, now visible as a shape rather than an anecdote.
- Rules are a stock. Draw the stock and flow: rule-making inflow, repeal outflow. In most institutions the repeal valve is missing or has an area near zero, so the stock only grows. Where is the repeal valve is a question a fourteen-year-old can ask a town council, and it is devastating.
- The aggregator. Tag every decision with the aggregation rule actually used — majority, supermajority, consensus, consent, unanimity, single authority, default by inaction, whoever showed up.

Reading the town's rules is practised by maintaining our own. Each activity writes a one-page constitution using the seven rule types and amends it in public, so the school runs its own rule graph with its own sediment, its own drifted clauses and its own missing repeal valve. Students will find their own dead rules faster than the town's, which is the honest order. See [[Constitution Of The Gap Desk]].
