# Campfire Not Bonfire

A theory and practice of holding hard conversations that do not escalate, and that do not abandon the shared drawing for war paint. The constitution that governs one is in [[Constitution Of The Gap Desk]]. This page is sourced; the earlier draft was written from memory and got several things wrong.

## What turns a campfire into a bonfire

- A decision attaches to the talk. The moment something is at stake in this conversation, advocacy displaces inquiry. This is the primary cause.
- An audience appears. Recording, press, somebody posting. People begin speaking to the absent third party rather than to each other.
- Speaking turns become scarce, and competition for the floor begins.
- Identity is threatened, and the shared drawing is claimed by one side as its banner. That is the war paint moment: the drawing stops being a common object and becomes a flag.
- Time is compressed. Short meetings escalate.

## What Bohm gives and what he does not

Formal Bohmian dialogue supplies the cognitive discipline. Suspension, which means holding an assumption out in front of you where you and others can see it, neither suppressing it nor acting from it. Proprioception of thought. No agenda, no decision, a circle, a facilitator who fades.

It is powerful and it is culturally thin. A circle of strangers with no entry ritual, no relationship before the room and no obligation after it, and explicitly no purpose. That works in a seminar. In a town where everyone will see each other at the grocery store tomorrow it is the wrong model.

## Two talanoa lineages, which are not the same thing

They are routinely conflated and they came from different places for different purposes. Keeping them apart matters for us, because it is the first one we actually want.

Sitiveni Halapua, a Tongan development economist and then director of the East-West Center's Pacific Islands Development Program, developed the talanoa process for political mediation. He used it during the Cook Islands economic collapse of 1996, in Fiji's reconciliation after the May 2000 coup, and in ethnic reconciliation meetings in the Solomon Islands. The Fiji work ran as five two-day sessions plus smaller working sessions between November 2000 and July 2002, drawing in political opponents, religious leaders, former hostages and coup sympathisers, and eventually the leaders of both main ethnic parties, who agreed to form standing committees on land and constitutional reform.

Timote Vaioleti, an academic of Tongan ancestry, separately picked up talanoa as a research methodology, arguing in 2006 for an appropriate approach to researching Pacific educational and social issues in Aotearoa. That is the lineage most of the academic literature follows.

The political lineage is the one closest to what a Superior fire circle needs, and Halapua's own framing is better than anything in our earlier draft.

## Halapua's rules, which correct ours

Talanoa proceeds without any predetermined expectations for agreement. In Halapua's words, to say people have to agree to something is starting at the wrong end.

The aim is understanding rather than agreement. Living with the differences, and understanding them better, is a good start.

Participants set their own ground rules, and in Fiji those were inclusion, reconciliation and mutual respect.

The process deliberately takes the issue out of the arena where points are scored. Prime Minister Qarase described it as taking the issues away from debate in parliament, away from scoring political points and glossing over issues.

That last one vindicates our design rule but states it better. The rule is not that no decision may ever be made at a campfire. The rule is that the campfire is not the arena where the decision is won, and nobody arrives owing anybody agreement.

## Vā, and why it is the thing being protected

Vā is the space or the relationship between people — in Melani Anae's phrase, the sacred, spiritual and social spaces of human relationships. In Tongan, vā is the distance between or distance apart, and tauhi means to look after or tend, so tauhi vā is the nurturing of sociospatial ties, as Tēvita Kaili set out in The Contemporary Pacific in 2005. The Samoan form is teu le vā, which Anae wrote on in 2010.

Vā is what Bohm has no word for, and it reframes the whole problem. The reason a fire must not become a bonfire is not that heat is bad. It is that the vā is the thing that has to still be there afterwards. A community will accept that statement of the rule because it names what they are already protecting.

The cultivation of vā in talanoa aims at understanding rather than agreement, and thereby at information that is true or authentic — moʻoni. When it goes well it produces mālie and māfana.

## Kakala, and the project arc

Konai Helu Thaman introduced kakala, the Tongan garland, as a framework in 1992, with three stages: toli, tui and luva. Seuula Johansson-Fua — Tongan, Director of the Institute of Education at the University of the South Pacific, and convenor of the Rethinking Pacific Education Initiative — expanded it to six components in her 2014 chapter Kakala Research Framework: A Garland in Celebration of a Decade of Rethinking Education, in Of Waves, Winds and Wonderful Things, adding teu and incorporating mālie and māfana from Linitā Manuatu's Auckland theses of 2000 and 2001.

- Teu — preparation. Conceptualising, designing and planning, and examining your own position before beginning.
- Toli — the selection and collection of flowers. Who is included, how, and with what ethics. The ethics at this stage are what make the gathering authentic rather than merely complete.
- Tui — weaving the garland. Analysis and composition. Johansson-Fua describes it as collective work between the older women who string the kakala and the young girls sent out for flowers, with the pattern changing and being negotiated and corrected between them as it goes.
- Luva — the presentation of the finished garland. Giving a gift with heartfelt sincerity, humility and honour, carrying the knowledge that much time, work and sacrifice went into it. In research it is the giving back of the findings to the people they came from.
- Mālie — an expression of appreciation, in performance the word the audience calls out, and in the framework the judgement that what has been presented is of high quality, monitored against utility, applicability and relevance to the context. Manuatu conceptualises it as energy that moves and transforms, draws on passion and uplifts the heart.
- Māfana — warmth. The heartfelt feeling that causes an emotional reaction in everyone involved.

Two things follow directly for us. Luva is the graduation requirement, said better than we said it: a thing left behind is a gift given with sincerity and honour, and the giving is the completion rather than the keeping. And mālie and māfana are better conversation measures than any count of steelmanned arguments, because a community can judge them without training. Keep a behavioural count as well, but the felt measure is the real one.

## Vanua, which may fit Superior better than talanoa

Unaisi Nabobo-Baba's Fijian Vanua Research Framework, set out in 2008 in Decolonising Framings in Pacific Research, is the Fijian rather than Tongan frame. Vanua is a universal whole with four interrelated dimensions — physical, social, cultural and spiritual — covering land, people, chiefs, knowledge, relationships and spirituality together.

Its value here is that it is a protocol rather than a disposition. Plan, seek permission from the vanua, talanoa, gift appropriately, and report back — and the result is a lifelong connection between the researcher and the researched rather than a completed transaction. A school sending students to ask their neighbours questions needs exactly that arc, and it maps onto teu, toli, tui and luva without strain.

Nabobo-Baba and Farrelly also note that talanoa has formal and instrumental forms as well as informal ones; the Fijian formal register is veivosaki.

## The critique we should take seriously

Laumua Tunufai's Pacific Research: Rethinking the Talanoa Methodology, in New Zealand Sociology in 2016, argues that talanoa is best regarded as a research method rather than a methodology, because it lacks the philosophical rationale and the processual clarity that kakala and vanua unambiguously have.

He makes three points that land directly on what we are doing.

- Deconstructing talanoa into tala and noa is a Tongan move. Vaioleti glosses tala as tale, relate, story, tell or declare, and noa as common, old, of no value, without thought, without exertion, nothingness, empty — so talanoa literally becomes talking about nothing in particular. Tunufai accepts this within the Tongan context and argues it is counterproductive elsewhere, and asks pointedly how talking about nothing yields more authentic and trustworthy data.
- The pan-Pacific claim is inconsistent with decolonisation's own emphasis on local relevance. Talanoa is borrowed or foreign for most Pacific nations, and in Samoan the deliberations of titled and elderly people are properly fa'afaletui rather than talanoa at all.
- What talanoa claims to do differently could also be achieved by other methods, of which talanoa may be a nice translation. The point of departure is the cultural qualification and sensitivity of the person holding the conversation, not the name of the method.

That third point is the one aimed at us. If we import the word and not the substance, we have done precisely the thing the literature warns against, and we will have taken a name from people who are still fighting to keep it meaning something.

## Plática, which is Superior's own

The Chicana and Latina tradition of plática is structurally close and locally at home. Edén Fierros and Dolores Delgado Bernal formalised it in Vamos a Platicar: The Contours of Pláticas as Chicana/Latina Feminist Methodology, in Chicana/Latina Studies in 2016, drawing on the curandera Elena Avila, who defines a plática as a deep heart-to-heart talk that continues for as long as it needs, and who says the most important ingredient in the plática is trust.

Their five principles.

- It draws on Chicana and Latina feminist theory, and on other critical theory that centres people the system has treated as marginal.
- It is relational, honouring participants as co-constructors of knowledge rather than sources of it, grounded in respeto for the contributor as a holder and creator of knowledge.
- It connects everyday lived experience to the inquiry rather than minimising it. Conversations that begin about school quickly include family, language, sex and gender, and that is the method working rather than drifting.
- It provides a potential space for healing.
- It relies on reciprocity, vulnerability and the researcher's own reflexivity. Trust exists only if the exchange is reciprocal, so the researcher must be willing to share what they ask of others. Unlike an interview, where the questioner says as little as possible, a plática talks back and forth.

That fifth principle converts straight into a rule for us: a student collecting an oral history answers the same questions they ask. No one-way extraction. It is the single most concrete thing this reading produced and it should go into the newsroom constitution in [[Gap Ledger]].

## The integration, restated

Kakala supplies the project arc, and vanua supplies the protocol for entering a community that is not yours. Talanoa supplies the conversation form, in Halapua's political register rather than the research one. Vā names the thing being protected. Mālie and māfana are the evaluation. Bohm's suspension is one technique used inside the conversation rather than the frame around it. Plática is the local tradition that already carries most of this in Spanish, and it is not borrowed.

Food and setting are not decoration. In a documented talanoa with students, participants hesitated to eat when the setting still felt like an academic one, and relaxed when it did not. The entry ritual is doing real work, and a room that reads as institutional defeats it.

## The caution, which is load bearing

Importing a Pacific framework wholesale into an Arizona copper town, over the top of Apache and Mexican-American communities who have their own practices, would be the exact colonial move these scholars write against — and Tunufai's critique says so about Pacific peoples borrowing from each other, let alone us.

The honest version is three sided. Bring talanoa, kakala and vanua in named, cited and credited as someone else's work. Lead locally with plática, which is already Superior's. And treat Apache council practice as a conversation to be had rather than a literature to be reviewed, because it is not ours to characterise.

Putting plática, talanoa and Apache practice beside each other and finding the shared structure is itself the lesson, and it is the strongest possible demonstration that the school's frameworks are not the school's property.
