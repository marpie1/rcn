# Eleven Steps

The generator behind [[Ferndale Ideal Education Model]]. Marc Pierson's self-certification protocol, published as [[Systems Certification]] on relocalizecreativity.net. One hour, alone, with three or four sheets of paper and a pencil. Do not read ahead.

## The protocol

- Step 1 — Think of an area of present concern and care. It does not matter what, only that it is really meaningful to you personally. Take your time. Be sure it is a big deal to you.
- Step 2 — List anything that comes to mind about it. A list, not an essay. Any thing — idea, person, place, object. At least fifteen items, and no more than fits on one sheet. You should feel that a lot of the stuff related to the topic is on the page.
- Step 3 — Choose about eight of them. You choose. Write those onto a clean sheet with lots of white space, and draw a border around each one.
- Step 4 — Draw lines connecting some of them, in ways that make sense to you. The connections must matter to you.
- Step 5 — Label the connecting lines. What does the line mean.
- Step 6 — Add arrowheads.
- Step 7 — If a line has two arrowheads, erase one and draw a second line to carry it.
- Step 8 — Make sure every line label is a verb that conveys action or change, typically ending in -ing. Expect to erase and redo some. You can rename the nodes at any time. This is your graph, your drawing, about something you care about.
- Step 9 — Read the graph aloud. Say only what is written on the page. If you used verbs you can do this; if you did not, you will find the verbs while trying.
- Step 10 — You have created a clear sparse visual story from thin air, from your own mind and experience. Now go back to Step 1 and recall how you felt when you were asked to do it. Say it out loud, listen to what you say, and write down what you just heard yourself say. Then do the same for every step. This takes about as long as making the drawing did.
- Step 11 — Make sure each step has a note recording how it felt and what you understood there. What have you learned. What could you do with this little graph. Was it interesting, fun, useful.

Only after all eleven, below a fold, does the protocol tell you what you demonstrated.

## What the design is actually doing

Everyone does it alone, first. The group work comes only after each person holds a finished drawing of their own. That is the opposite of how a facilitated workshop usually runs, and it is why the resulting map is federated rather than consensual — it is n personal drawings merged, with the seams still visible.

Step 2 sets a floor and a ceiling in the same breath: at least fifteen, no more than one page. The floor defeats the person who would write three things and stop. The ceiling defeats the person who would write forever rather than choose.

Authorship is handed back at every single step, explicitly. You pick the topic. You choose the eight. The connections must matter to you. This is your graph, your drawing, about something you care about. Nothing is assigned and nothing is marked.

Step 7 is the only formal discipline imposed anywhere in the protocol, and it is a real one: a line with two arrowheads is two relations pretending to be one, so it gets split. That single rule is what turns a doodle into a graph.

Step 8 is the hinge. Forcing verbs is what makes Step 9 possible, and Step 9 is the certification moment — reading the thing aloud proves to the person that they built a story rather than a decoration. Nobody else has to confirm it.

Steps 10 and 11 are half the protocol by time, and they are entirely about affect. Where the big learning is, in the protocol's own words. Every step page carries a Notes field, so the recording surface is present from the first minute rather than bolted on at the end.

## Why it works on people that school has failed

The Ferndale cohort was eighteen students who had given up on schooling and were invited back without a classroom. For that population every design choice above is load bearing.

A student who has learned that school is a place where other people choose the topic, set the length, and mark the result meets a protocol in which they choose the topic, the length is bounded by a sheet of paper, and nobody marks anything. The artifact at Step 9 is proof, produced by them, that they can do a thing they had been told for years they could not do.

And Step 10 asks them to look straight at the feelings that taught them otherwise. The drawing is the evidence; the recall is the diagnosis. Both belong to the learner.

## The nine natural cognitive skills

What the protocol says you demonstrated, once you have done it: focusing; listing, which is looking about, imagining, naming and limiting; culling, which is narrowing and selecting; arraying, which is spatial organising; connecting, which is formally relating; assigning direction, which is vectoring; labelling, which is clarifying; narrating; and reflecting and synthesising.

The point of the list is that every item on it is something the person was already doing before anyone taught them anything. The protocol does not install a skill, it names one. That is what makes self-certification an honest word rather than a soft one, and it is why the list stays hidden behind a fold until the work is done.

In our terms each of the nine is an item on the ruler, and each has a see, a do and a teach threshold. The whole protocol is one badge at do level and a much harder badge at teach level, because walking somebody else through Step 10 without rushing them is genuinely difficult.

## Concept map or causal loop

As written, the eleven steps produce a semantic network rather than a causal loop diagram. Step 2 invites any thing — idea, person, place, object — and Step 8 asks for action verbs, which yields propositions like teachers supporting the learning of science. The Ferndale map is exactly that, and so are its best lines: heartbreak envies love.

A causal loop diagram needs something different. The nodes must be variables that can be more or less, and the arrows carry a sign rather than a verb. Marc and Kerry Turner now start groups with causal loop diagrams instead, which for most adults is right and faster.

It is worth being clear about the trade, because the school needs both doors.

The verb version asks for no abstraction at all and pays off at Step 9 with a sentence anybody can say out loud. Teachers support the learning of science is a sentence. Teachers, arrow, science, plus is not. For someone who has been failed by school, that readable sentence is the entire point of the exercise and a variable is already one abstraction too many.

The causal loop version gets to feedback, which is where the systems insight actually lives, and the Ferndale map shows the cost of not getting there — fifty-four domain-level links, densely cyclic, none of them signed, so the loops are present and invisible.

The recommendation is a Step 12 rather than a different Step 1. Keep the eleven as the first drawing. Then convert: keep only the nodes that can be more or less, restate each verb as same direction or opposite direction, and look for a loop. The learner ends with the same care rendered two ways, which is itself the lesson about representation, and nobody has to start by being abstract.

Which door a person comes through should depend on the person, not the house style.

## The table discipline

The person who tends the table does not look at the work. Marc said in advance that he would not look, and then did not, and sat doing his own unrelated work while each person drew. When a section was finished he explained the next step. People asked, repeatedly, for more help, and he gave none beyond what is written above.

That is the intervention, and it has three parts that all have to be present.

- Declared in advance, so the rule is known before the work starts. Undeclared silence is not withdrawal of judgement, it is surveillance, and it is worse.
- Physically demonstrated. He was occupied with something else, not sitting attentively, not available if needed. The occupation is the proof.
- Held under pressure. They asked and asked. Every refusal re-confirms the rule, and every request is the symptom becoming visible.

He was present the whole time. This is not solo work and the difference matters. Alone in a room there is no authority to have a new kind of relationship with. At the table there is a present authority who declines the role, and that is the thing being unlearned.

A hint is worse than a wrong answer. Once you show that you could have helped, your silence becomes a withholding, and the whole hour is retroactively converted into a test with a person in it who knows the answer. The protocol does not survive one hint.

The line is clean. Anything said to everyone before Step 1 is framing. Anything said to one person during the steps is help. If we learn that a particular kind of person hits a particular wall, the fix goes into the opening framing for everybody, never into a whisper.

So the badge is not being able to run the eleven steps. It is being able to sit at the table and not help, which is much harder, and which is the only part of this that needs training.

## The harder version, in the group

The same discipline held through the integration, where five students worked on each other's material to build one diagram. Marc encouraged and did not judge or suggest.

Refusing in the group costs something different from refusing at the table. Alone, what you are holding back is the urge to teach. In the group, what you are holding back is the urge to arbitrate — five people arguing about whether two words mean the same thing, and every adult instinct saying settle it.

Settling it is what would have destroyed the work. In a group the facilitator's judgement does not merely decide a question, it colonises the language, and one ruling on whether two words are synonyms makes the whole merged vocabulary the adult's. Since the six weeks were about the words, that is everything.

So the group rule is not do not correct. It is do not resolve. Let the disagreement about a word stay open as long as it needs to.

Encouragement is allowed precisely because it carries no propositional content. Keep going says nothing about the map. I think those two are the same thing says everything about it.

## What the difficulty shows

Many people have now done this and many of them found some steps hard, meaning uncomfortable rather than difficult. Marc added Steps 10 and 11 because he noticed the fear. The reflection is not a metacognition addition, it is a response to observed harm, built into the instrument afterwards.

The difficulty does not fall evenly. It concentrates wherever judgement is absent.

- Step 1, because nobody has asked in twelve years what you care about, because your choice exposes you, and because there is no way to tell whether you picked a good one.
- Step 2, because the floor of fifteen pushes past where you would have stopped, and items eight to fifteen are the ones you were not going to admit.
- Step 3, because you select without being asked to justify it, and you spend the whole step anticipating a question that never comes.
- Step 5, because naming a relationship is where vocabulary insecurity lives in anyone who has been corrected on words all their life.
- Step 8, because in school erasing means you were wrong, and here it means the work is progressing. Understanding that inversion is easy and feeling it is not.
- Step 9, because reading your own work aloud is the classic site of school humiliation, and now there is nobody to say whether it was good, so you have to be your own judge having been taught you are not qualified.

So the sharper statement is that school installs a dependence on external judgement, and this protocol withdraws judgement at every step. What people feel is withdrawal.

That is also why the reflection works. Naming it turns I am bad at this into I was trained to need something that is not in this room, and those are different experiences of the same minute.

The difficulty is adaptive rather than damage. Everyone finished. The strategy was rational in the place it was learned and does not transfer here.

## The ordering, which runs backwards

At the kitchen table, Noah — the student who had given up on school — found none of the steps hard. A lawyer found every step very hard. Everyone else fell between.

That is the reverse of what a school would predict from the same two people, and it is what the withdrawal reading predicts: difficulty tracks years of successful submission to judgement rather than years of education. A lawyer is the limiting case, having been schooled, examined, called, and then employed in a profession where an unsupported assertion is a fault and every document is reviewed. Almost every step here asks for exactly that — assert a relationship you cannot evidence, choose without justifying, stop when the paper runs out.

One alternative reading was worth testing: that her difficulty was a trained professional prohibition on doing what the steps ask rather than fear, which would feel much the same from inside but would want a different fix. In her case it was settled by later conversation, at length, and it was fear. The distinction is still worth keeping for the next person, because the two want different responses, but it is not what was happening here.

Two people at the ends of a small range is a direction, not a measurement. But the direction is clear enough to keep the record from here on.

## Keeping the record

Two instruments, both cheap.

The Notes are the first, in the person's own words, step by step. Across many people that is a dataset rather than an anecdote, and it can discriminate between explanations, because they attach to different steps and produce different words. Judgement anxiety attaches to being seen. Novelty anxiety attaches to not knowing what is expected. I cannot draw clusters at Steps 3 and 4.

Requests for help are the second, counted silently by whoever is tending the table. They need nobody to report anything and they measure the dependence directly.

Both are Rasch shaped: eleven steps as items, people as persons, one scale. A Wright map of the eleven steps would show which step is hardest and for whom as a measurement rather than a claim, which is also the only form in which a school could receive it.

One constraint on keeping it. The subject is the protocol, not the person, exactly as in [[Gap Ledger]]. A help-request count attached to somebody's name would be a score, and we would have rebuilt the thing we are describing. Aggregate it or do not keep it.

## Do not hand over the diagnosis

The reflection is first person only. I noticed I felt exposed is data. You feel that because school did this to you is an insult even when it is true, and it is another judgement handed down, which is the thing being unlearned.

Marc's assessment of the cause belongs in the adult's head and never in the learner's ear. If a person arrives at it themselves it is theirs; if they are told it, it is just a new authority.

## Why this does not make us adversaries of the public schools

The finding reads as an indictment, and the strategy in [[Credit Ladder]] needs teachers as allies. Four things resolve that, and only the last is tactical.

The subject is the structure rather than the people. No teacher chose to make marking the medium of the relationship; they inherited it and are measured by it. And the phrase what has become of public schooling points at the right thing: the teachers did not get worse, the measurement regime got denser — testing, accountability metrics, inspection, appraisal — so there is more judgement per hour in a school now than there was, falling on staff and students alike.

Give it to the staff first. At Ferndale the facilitator did it, then her administrator did, each alone at the same table. If adults who work in schools find the same steps hard, the finding stops being you did this to children and becomes look what was done to all of us — which is not a softening, it is more accurate. Teachers are the most exposed population of all, having been schooled, trained, inspected and appraised.

Never hand over the diagnosis, as above.

And the position is a consequence rather than a tactic. The signature of not seeing a system, in [[Interrogating A System]], is asking who did that to me. If our founding observation is that people blame instead of seeing systems, and then we blame schools, we have failed our own test in public at the first opportunity. Attacking schools would be blaming Fred for the dominoes.

What we offer a school is not the criticism but the instrument. Any teacher can run these steps with their own class and read the Notes, and they will find what we found, and it will be theirs.

## Which door

[[Interrogating A System]] is Kerry Turner's route to the same place — dialogic, question driven, variables from the start, and closing on a real intervention. It is authored throughout and nothing in it is top down.

Both start from the person's live concern, in their own words, with no answer key, reading the relation aloud, and letting the constraint do the correcting. This one is instrumented for feeling and has no intervention step. That one is instrumented for consequence and has no reflection step.

Eleven steps to make it yours, Kerry's questions to make it true, an experiment to make it matter, and back to the diagram.

Which door a person comes through should depend on the person, not the house style. And on the evidence so far, the person who will find this easiest is the one the school has already given up on — which means they are not being included, they are the most qualified person in the room to run it.
