# The Neighborhood Protection Survey — latent conditions, speaking up, breakdowns

**State (as of 2026-09-16):** First draft, pipeline markdown, in Claude Code. Awaiting Marc's reactions. Stays .md until Marc says publish.

**What it is.** The instrument behind Section 2b's Principle Seven (latent conditions are read before incidents) and Principle Six (speaking up is produced by engagement, and both are measured). It is Tripod Delta's Failure State Profile put into neighborhood terms, with Mahoney's verbal-vigilance items and a short breakdown block added, so that one instrument gives a neighborhood its latent-condition profile, its willingness to speak, and its recent breakdowns, by World.

**How it is run.** With the e-VSM survey, in the same sitting, from the same config: the same Worlds, the same respondents, the same session. Everyone answers every question; nothing is split by role. A respondent's Worlds tell the aggregator whose corner an answer comes from. Run in Phase 1 alongside e-VSM; re-run at every retrospective; any later incident analysis (an NRM drawing) is read against the profile that was in force when the work was chartered.

**Design rules, from Tripod Delta, kept.** Every latent-condition item is an observable event, not an evaluation. Every item is bounded: *in the last three months, in this neighborhood's work*. Every item asks what happened to the respondent or in front of them, never what they think of the organization. Every latent-condition item is worded so that **Yes means a condition of concern occurred**; the profile is the share of Yes.

---

## Worlds

Use the e-VSM config's Worlds. Choose three or four per survey in the neighborhood's own words, from the Phase 1 list, and add these, which this survey needs and e-VSM did not: **employed by an institution that serves the neighborhood** (school, clinic, agency, church, city); **receives services from one of those institutions**; **funder, municipal staff or elected**; **member of an ME or a between-institution project**; **holds a small group together**; **convener or Platform**. Any World the neighborhood needs can be added. The point of the added Worlds is Section 2b's reading of Mahoney: "the outside" is the municipality, the funders and the service professionals, and the survey must be able to show their answers beside residents' answers to the same questions.

## Block A — Latent conditions (the eleven Basic Risk Factors in neighborhood terms)

Response for every item: **Yes / No / Didn't come up.** "Didn't come up" means the respondent had no occasion to see it; it is excluded from the bar and reported as coverage. Optional free text after each block: *If you answered Yes to any of these, say a little about it.*

Four items per factor. The first two of each four are the **core** items for a short form (22 items). The full form is 44. A neighborhood that already sits through e-VSM's 44 items may prefer Delta's rotation: each respondent gets the two core items plus one of the other two at random.

### A1. HW — The things we work with

*What Shell called Hardware: the tools, materials and equipment. Here: the room, the kitchen, the van, the printer, the sign-up system, the supplies.*

HW-1. In the last three months, did a piece of neighborhood work stall or get dropped because a space, vehicle, tool or supply wasn't there when it was needed?
HW-2. Did you, or someone you know, pay out of pocket, or use your own car, phone or home, because the shared one wasn't available?
HW-3. Did a shared thing (a room, a kitchen, a van, a printer, a sign-up system) not work when someone went to use it?
HW-4. Did anyone do a task with something not meant for it because the right thing wasn't at hand?

### A2. DE — How things are set up

*Design: layouts and arrangements that make it hard to do the right thing. Here: how a room, a form, a schedule or a hand-off is laid out.*

DE-1. Did you have to work around the way something is set up (a room, a form, a sign-up, a schedule) to get a job done?
DE-2. Is there a step in a neighborhood task that people regularly skip because it is awkward or slow?
DE-3. Did a meeting, an event or a hand-off happen at a time or place that made it hard for the people who needed to be there?
DE-4. Did a form, an app or a sheet ask for something that made people give up or guess?

### A3. MM — Keeping things up

*Maintenance management: whether shared things get kept in working order. Here: shared things and shared agreements alike.*

MM-1. Is there a shared thing that has been broken, missing or unusable for more than a month?
MM-2. Did an agreement (with a church, a school, a landlord, the city, a funder) turn out to have lapsed when someone needed it?
MM-3. Did a list, a calendar or a shared document turn out to be out of date when someone relied on it?
MM-4. Is there a shared thing that everyone knows needs fixing and nobody is on the hook for?

### A4. HK — Keeping track

*Housekeeping: tidiness of the workplace. Here: the state of the commons, physical and informational.*

HK-1. Were you unable to find something you needed (a document, a key, a password, a contact, the notes from last time)?
HK-2. Is the list of who is doing what out of date, or is there no such list?
HK-3. Did two versions of the same thing (a plan, a flyer, a roster) circulate at once?
HK-4. Did something (leftover supplies, a space, a shared account) get left in a state that made trouble for the next person?

### A5. EC — Strain

*Error-enforcing conditions: time pressure, fatigue, and conditions that make mistakes likely. Here: what the work asks of people's evenings, bodies and households.*

EC-1. Did you do neighborhood work past the point of exhaustion?
EC-2. Was a deadline set by an outside body (a funder, the city, a school) met on volunteers' evenings and weekends?
EC-3. Did you take on something because no one else would, though you did not have the time?
EC-4. Did money worries, health or family strain affect your ability to do what you had promised here?

### A6. PR — How we do things

*Procedures: whether the way to do a task is written, findable, correct and usable.*

PR-1. Is there a task where two people would do it two different ways and neither knows which is right?
PR-2. Did you have to ask three people to find out how something is done here?
PR-3. Is the written way of doing something, where there is one, out of step with how it is actually done?
PR-4. Did you finish a task and not know who to tell?

### A7. TR — Being shown first

*Training: competence and experience. Here: whether anyone was shown before being expected to do. This is SODOTO's See One.*

TR-1. Did you do a task you had never seen anyone do?
TR-2. Is there a task that only one person knows how to do?
TR-3. Did anyone step into a role here without anyone showing them what it involved?
TR-4. Did you want to learn how something is done and find there was no one to show you?

### A8. CO — Knowing what's going on

*Communication: whether information reaches the people who need it.*

CO-1. Did you learn of a decision that affected you after it was made?
CO-2. Did two groups do the same work without knowing of each other?
CO-3. Did a message that mattered (a change of time, a cancellation, a need) fail to reach someone who needed it?
CO-4. Did you first hear something important about the neighborhood's work as a rumor?

### A9. IG — Pulled two ways

*Incompatible goals: having to choose between the right way and production, money, politics or personal goals. This is the factor McKnight's critique lives in.*

IG-1. Did you have to choose between what a funder or an institution wanted and what residents needed?
IG-2. Did work bend toward producing a report, a count or a photo rather than a result for residents?
IG-3. Did you feel you had to choose between doing something the careful way and doing it in time?
IG-4. Did anyone's paid job pull against what they had promised here?

### A10. OR — Who does what, who decides

*Organization: structure, philosophy and process. Here: roles, standing and ownership of decisions. This is the factor the three IAD bands read.*

OR-1. Is there a decision here that nobody knows who owns?
OR-2. Did a role go unfilled because nobody knew it was a role?
OR-3. Were you unsure whether you had the standing to decide something, and decided, or didn't, anyway?
OR-4. Did something fall between two groups because each thought the other had it?

### A11. DF — Catching it when it goes wrong

*Defences: protection against the consequences when the rest fails. Here: whether anything or anyone is watching, and whether a near miss gets said.*

DF-1. Did something go wrong here that no one had been watching for?
DF-2. When something went wrong, was there no one whose job it was to notice?
DF-3. Is there a piece of work where, if the one person holding it were absent, nothing would catch a problem?
DF-4. Did a near miss (something that almost went wrong) go unmentioned afterwards?

## Block B — Speaking up (Mahoney's six, neighborhood wording)

Response: four-point **Disagree / Somewhat disagree / Somewhat agree / Agree**, as Mahoney ran them. No middle option.

Definition shown to the respondent once, above the block: *"The outside" means the city and county, the funders, and the institutions and professionals that provide services here: schools, clinics, agencies, churches as service providers.*

B-1. My concerns about harm to this neighborhood would be acted upon if I raised them with the outside.
B-2. The outside does not knowingly trade this neighborhood's wellbeing for its own productivity, budgets or reporting.
B-3. I am encouraged by my neighbors to speak up about any concern I have about harm here.
B-4. If I were the one needing help, I would feel safe being helped by the people doing this work.
B-5. This neighborhood places a major emphasis on preventing harm in the work it does.
B-6. Regardless of the situation, I feel perfectly free to speak up if I see something I think is a threat to this neighborhood or the people in it.

Mahoney's originals, for the record: (1) My suggestions about patient safety would be acted upon if I expressed them to the management/leadership of [hospital/clinic/lab]. (2) Management/leadership of [hospital/clinic/lab] does not knowingly compromise patient safety concerns for productivity. (3) I am encouraged by my coworkers to report any patient safety concerns I may have. (4) I would feel safe being treated at [hospital/clinic/lab] as a patient. (5) This [hospital/clinic/lab] places a major emphasis on improving patient safety. (6) Regardless of the situation, I feel perfectly free to speak up if I see something I think is a threat to patient safety. Alpha .89 across 6,751 respondents. B-6 is the criterion item; B-1, B-2 and B-5 loaded on Safety Culture Leadership; B-3 and B-4 are kept as written with the referent changed.

## Block C — Engagement (the inside)

Response: the same four-point scale. Mahoney's Engagement factor had three components, teamness, culture and people, and predicted B-6 at .65. His engagement items are not in the deck, so these six are new and are marked as such; two per component. They ask about the inside: the small groups, the ME, the neighbors doing the work.

C-1. (teamness) The people I do neighborhood work with can count on me, and I can count on them.
C-2. (teamness) When something needs doing, I know who I would call.
C-3. (culture) Here, saying "I made a mistake" is met with help, not blame.
C-4. (culture) We talk about what nearly went wrong, not only about what went wrong.
C-5. (people) I feel that I belong to the group doing this work.
C-6. (people) Someone here would notice if I were struggling.

## Block D — Breakdowns

Response as marked. The block exists so that Principle Five (breakdowns are blameless by default) has a measure: not whether breakdowns happen, which they will, but what happened to the people who named them.

D-1. In the last three months, did you see something in the neighborhood's work that you thought could harm someone? **Yes / No.**
D-2. If yes: did you say so to anyone? **Yes / No.**
D-3. If you said so: what happened next? *(free text)*
D-4. In the last three months, did anyone stop a piece of work, or call for it to stop, because they thought it was harmful? **Yes / No / Don't know.**
D-5. If someone here raised a concern about harm in the last three months, how were they treated afterwards? **Better off / The same / Worse off / Don't know / No one did.**

E-1. What is the one thing that could go wrong here that nobody is talking about? *(free text; answered last; read first at the Phase 1 meeting.)*

## Scoring and reading

**The Failure State Profile.** Eleven bars, one per factor, height = share of Yes among Yes/No answers on that factor's items, pooled across items and respondents. A twelfth bar, **Speaking up**, is the share of Disagree and Somewhat disagree on Block B; a thirteenth, **Engagement**, the same on Block C. Draw them in the graph tool or the e-VSM report as a bar chart with the factor's neighborhood name, the Shell code beneath, and the coverage (how many answered Yes/No rather than "Didn't come up") as a number under each bar. Bars are comparative, not absolute: there are no thresholds. The reading is which factors stand highest, which Worlds see them, and how the bars move between the Phase 1 run and the retrospective run.

**By World.** Every bar redrawn per World, side by side, as the e-VSM aggregator shows spheres per World. The comparison this survey was built for is residents' bars beside institution employees' bars on IG (pulled two ways), CO (knowing what's going on) and B-1 and B-2 (the outside): where those diverge, Phase 2 has its first thing to talk about.

**Free text.** Read in respondents' own words at the Phase 1 meeting, after the WHY round, as the e-VSM free text is read. E-1 first.

**Rasch.** Block A is dichotomous and fits the Rasch dichotomous model: each respondent gets an exposure measure (how much latent condition they met), each item a difficulty (how rare that condition is here). The item map is the more useful of the two: it orders the neighborhood's conditions from the ones everyone meets to the ones few do, which is a Wright map of what to fix first. Blocks B and C are polytomous and fit the rating-scale model, as Mahoney fit them. Calibrate in `tools/rcn-rasch.html` once there are enough respondents (a few dozen for a stable profile; more for stable item calibrations); until then report shares. Mahoney's calibrations are not recoverable; the neighborhood's own responses re-derive them.

**What feeds where.** The profile is a Phase 1 reading beside e-VSM. Its highest bars are latent conditions the charter's protection clause (Principle One) should name and the barrier reading (Principle Four) should guard. At the retrospective the profile is re-run and the two are laid side by side; where a barrier failed, the NRM drawing's Underlying Cause carries the BRF code, and the survey's bar for that code is the evidence that the condition was already present. Block D's D-5 is the standing measure of Principle Five.

## Notes for the tool

Ride on e-VSM's config and response JSON rather than a new tool: the same `sofi-config.json` carries Worlds and survey metadata; add an `items` array for this survey with `id`, `block`, `factor`, `text`, `scale` (`ynd`, `agree4`, `yn`, `free`, `d5`), and a `core` flag; the response file adds one entry per item. The aggregator's per-World view already does the by-World comparison; the profile chart is new. Short form: `core` items only. Rotation: core plus one random non-core per factor, seeded per respondent so the aggregator can reconstruct which items were shown.

Item ids are stable and are the keys the Overall Schema uses: a `LatentCondition` node per factor per neighborhood per run, with the bar, the coverage and the run date as properties, so that an NRM `UnderlyingCause` with `brf: "CO"` can be linked to the `LatentCondition` reading that preceded it.
