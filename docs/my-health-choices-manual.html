My Health Choices

An AI-assisted conversation about a specific health problem — loading the person's full clinical record and exploring options, evidence, and tradeoffs together. The Option Box shows evidence as 1,000 colored dots.

What My Health Choices Is

My Health Choices is an AI-assisted conversation about a specific active health problem. It opens pre-loaded with the person's full Shared Care Plan context — about-me, vitals, diagnoses, symptoms, medications, allergies, history, and providers — so no clinical setup is required. The conversation is focused on a specific problem passed in from My Health Picture via URL parameter.

The person and health partner ask questions in plain language. The AI responds drawing on the medical literature. When the AI discusses specific options with quantified evidence, it embeds an Option Box — a 1,000-dot SVG display that makes the evidence visible at a glance. The conversation and Option Box appear side by side: 58% chat, 42% dots.

After each AI response, suggestion chips appear — good follow-up questions the person can tap to keep the conversation moving. At the end of the exploration, a "Save Summary" button generates a structured record. The health partner's role is to sit with the person, guide the conversation, and help them understand what they are looking at — not to steer the decision.

This is similar in intent to Larry Weed's Problem Knowledge Coupler — connecting each active problem to the relevant medical knowledge, options, and evidence — but built for AI-assisted real-time exploration rather than pre-authored option sets.

The 1,000-dot Option Box

When the AI discusses specific options with quantified evidence, it triggers the Option Box: a grid of 1,000 colored dots on a 40×25 display. Each dot is one person — people in a similar situation with the same health problem and general risk profile. You can see, with your own eyes, how many people were helped, how many were harmed, and how many were unaffected.

This is not a calculator or a recommendation system. It is a way of seeing the evidence clearly enough to have a real conversation.

Reading the Dot Display

Every option in My Health Choices is shown as a grid of 1,000 colored dots. The colors always mean the same thing:

Teal — benefit: bad outcome prevented These people had a bad outcome prevented by this option. Without it they would have had a serious event — heart attack, stroke, fracture, hospitalization. With it, they didn't. Teal is what you hope to see more of.
Red — harmed by this option These people had a side effect or complication caused by the treatment — it would not have happened if they hadn't taken it. This is the cost side. More red means more people harmed.
Grey — unaffected by this option These people were neither helped nor harmed. Most of the 1,000 dots are usually grey. This is important — it shows that most people in the group are not strongly affected either way. Many people who take a medication are in the grey zone.
The most important thing to notice: how many teal dots are there compared to red dots? If a treatment helps 40 people out of 1,000 and harms 3, that is a strong benefit. If it helps 8 and harms 12, that is a reason to think carefully. The dots let you see this at a glance — no arithmetic required.

When to Use My Health Choices

Not every health situation calls for a formal Health Choices session. Use it when a real decision is approaching — when the person is weighing whether to start or stop a treatment, when there are genuinely different options with meaningfully different tradeoffs, or when the person is unsure what they want to do.

Good situations for Health Choices

  • Starting a new long-term medication (statin, blood pressure medication, diabetes medication)
  • Choosing between two treatments for the same problem
  • Deciding whether to pursue a screening test (colonoscopy, mammogram, PSA)
  • Weighing a surgical procedure against a non-surgical option
  • Reconsidering a medication the person has been on for years without review
  • Any situation where the person says "I'm not sure what to do"

Situations where Health Choices is not the right tool

  • Emergency or urgent situations — get care first, review choices later
  • Situations where there is only one medically appropriate option and no real alternative
  • Situations where the physician has already reviewed the evidence with the person in detail and a decision has been made — don't revisit without reason
  • Routine monitoring or follow-up that does not involve a new decision

How to Conduct a Health Choices Session

A Health Choices session is a conversation, not a presentation. You are not teaching the person what to choose — you are sitting beside them while they think it through. Your job is to make sure they understand what they are looking at and that their questions get answered honestly.

Allow at least 45 minutes for a first exploration. Returning to the same problem in a follow-up session is usually much shorter.

  1. Choose the right moment. Do this at home, sitting side by side, with no time pressure. Not the morning before a surgeon's appointment. Not while the person is in pain or distressed. Find a calm moment at least a few days before any decision deadline.
  2. Open My Health Choices from My Health Picture. Tap "Explore choices" on the relevant problem. The tool opens pre-loaded with the person's full SCP context and the problem already in focus. No setup is needed — the AI already knows the clinical picture.
  3. Start with the suggestion chips. Starter questions appear immediately. These are good beginning points — tap one together or type a question in your own words. The AI responds in plain language.
  4. Ask questions together as they come up. There is no set order. Follow what the person is curious about. Let the conversation go where it needs to go. If the person has a question the tool can't answer, write it down for the physician package.
  5. When an Option Box appears, take time with it. The dots show what the evidence says about this specific option. Explain teal (benefit), red (harm), and grey (unaffected) before you start reading the numbers together. Let the person look. Let them point. The display is there to be examined, not just shown.
  6. After each Option Box, pause and ask open questions. "What do you make of that?" "Is there anything here that surprises you?" "Does this change how you're thinking about it?" Do not ask "What are you going to do?" — let them arrive at that in their own time.
  7. Use follow-up suggestion chips to go deeper. After each AI response, new chips appear. Use them to explore further, or type your own question. The conversation can go as long as needed.
  8. When the exploration feels complete, press Save Summary. This generates a structured record — the problem, options discussed, key evidence, questions raised, and the person's tentative thinking. It saves to the Shared Care Plan automatically.
  9. If they are ready to decide, document it in My Health Picture. Record their choice in the Choices and Preferences section. Note the option they chose, the date, and a brief description of what mattered most to them.
  10. If they are not ready, that is fine. Schedule a follow-up. The Shared Care Plan now has a record of what was explored. Some people need to sleep on it, talk to family, or return more than once. That is the point — real decisions take time.

Language to Use — and Language to Avoid

Your role is to support the person's thinking, not to steer it. The most important discipline in a Health Choices session is staying neutral — presenting what the evidence shows without adding your own opinion about what the person should do.

Explaining the 1,000-person display

Opening explanation

"This shows 1,000 people — each dot is one person — who have the same health situation as you. We're going to look at what happened to them with each option. The colors tell us who was helped, who was harmed, and who wasn't really affected either way."

Explaining teal dots

"These teal dots — [number] out of 1,000 — are people who would have had a [heart attack / stroke / fall / other event] but didn't, because of this treatment. This is what the treatment helped prevent."

Explaining red dots

"These red dots — [number] out of 1,000 — are people who had a problem caused by the treatment that they wouldn't have had otherwise. This is the cost side."

Explaining grey dots

"All these grey dots — most of the 1,000 — are people who weren't really affected either way. The treatment didn't help them, but it didn't hurt them either."

This is often surprising to people and important to name — most people taking a medication are in the grey zone.

Staying neutral — phrases that work

  • "The numbers show... what do you make of that?"
  • "That's a question for the physician — I can write it down so we don't forget."
  • "There's no right answer here — it depends on what matters most to you."
  • "Some people in your situation would choose A. Others would choose B. The evidence doesn't tell you which one is right for you."
  • "Is there someone you'd want to talk to about this before deciding?"

Phrases to avoid

  • "I think you should..." — do not offer your personal recommendation
  • "Most people choose..." — this is social pressure, not information
  • "Your doctor thinks..." — unless this is directly documented, do not put words in the physician's mouth
  • "You really need to..." — urgency language undermines voluntary decision-making
  • Any framing that suggests one option is obviously correct
The hardest situation: the person is about to make a choice you believe is medically risky. You may say, once and clearly: "I want to make sure you understand what the numbers show here — [brief plain restatement]. This is your decision to make. I just want to make sure you have the full picture." Then let them decide. Document what they chose. If the risk is serious, note it in the Health Picture and include it in the next physician package.

Questions You Cannot Answer

A Health Choices session will generate questions. Some you can answer by pointing to the display — "the green dots show that." Others you cannot answer, and you should not try.

Type of question How to respond
"What does this number mean for me specifically?" The display shows what happens to people in a similar situation — it cannot predict what will happen to any one person. That is an honest answer. Write the question down for the physician package.
"Which option is better?" The display shows what the evidence says. Which option is better depends on what matters most to them — that is not in the display. Stay with the question: "What matters most to you in this decision?"
"Why didn't my doctor tell me this?" Acknowledge the feeling without commenting on the physician. "These numbers can be hard to discuss in a short appointment. That's part of why this tool exists."
A medical question about a drug, a condition, or a mechanism Write it down. It goes in the physician package. Do not guess at clinical answers.
"What would you do?" "That's not my decision to make for you — and I genuinely don't know what I'd do in your situation, because I'm not in it. What I can do is help you think it through."

Documenting the Decision

Every Health Choices session should result in a documented entry, whether or not the person reaches a decision. This entry goes in two places: My Health Picture (Choices and Preferences section) and a note in My Shared Care Plan.

What to record in My Health Picture

  • The decision topic — the health problem and the specific choice being considered
  • The option set used — the name and date of the Health Choices content reviewed
  • The person's decision — what they chose, or that they have not yet decided
  • What mattered most to them — a brief note in their own words about their reasoning
  • Any questions they want to bring to the physician
  • The date and your name

What to record in the Shared Care Plan

A brief note: "Health Choices session completed on [date] regarding [topic]. Person reviewed [option set name]. Decision: [what they chose, or 'still considering']. Questions for physician: [list or 'none at this time']."

Example — Statin decision for Rosa

Rosa's physician recommended starting a statin for elevated cholesterol. You schedule a Health Choices session for Tuesday afternoon at her home. You open the "Statin therapy — primary prevention" option set together. Rosa reads through all three options. She is surprised that so many dots are grey — she assumed the medication would help most people who took it. She asks why anyone would take it if so few people are helped. You answer honestly: "Some people decide it's not worth it for them. Others think even a small benefit is worth having, especially if the harm risk is low. That's really what this decision is about."

Rosa decides she wants to talk to her daughter before deciding. You write the session in her Shared Care Plan, note her question about the grey zone for the physician, and schedule a follow-up for the following week.

When the AI Does Not Know

My Health Choices draws on the AI's knowledge of the medical literature. It covers the vast majority of common health decisions a person will face. If a question is outside what the AI can reliably answer — an unusual condition, a very new treatment, a question that requires the person's specific test results — the AI will say so. That is a signal to write the question down and include it in the physician package.

Do not push past an honest "I don't know" from the AI. The value of the tool is in the questions it can answer well. The questions it cannot answer belong with the physician.

The Save Summary

At the end of a Health Choices exploration, press the "Save Summary" button. This generates a structured record of the session:

  • The problem that was explored
  • The options that came up in the conversation
  • The key evidence presented — including any Option Box data discussed
  • The person's questions — what they asked, what they were uncertain about
  • The person's tentative thinking — what direction they are leaning and why

The summary saves to the Shared Care Plan automatically. This means the next time you or anyone else on the care team opens the person's record, the exploration is there — the thinking is visible, the questions are captured, and nothing has been lost to memory.

If the person reaches a decision during the session, record it in My Health Picture (Choices and Preferences section) in addition to the Save Summary. The Summary captures the exploration; the Health Picture captures the decision.
