Option Box — Authoring Guide

How fact boxes are created, validated, and published
Who this is for
This guide is written for clinical curators — clinicians, clinical informaticists, or trained health communicators who have the background to evaluate published evidence and translate it into patient-facing numbers. It is not a guide for patients. Every fact box requires a person with clinical training to verify the numbers before publication.

What is an Option Box?

An Option Box is a one-page shared decision-making tool. It presents the benefits and harms of a clinical choice in plain numbers — not relative risks or percentages, but absolute frequencies: of 1,000 people like you over 5 years, 18 fewer will have a heart attack.

The design follows the fact box model developed by the Harding Center for Risk Literacy (Berlin). The core principle: patients deserve to know what actually happens to groups of people who make each choice, not how doctors summarize those choices. Every number must come from published evidence. The tool never makes a recommendation.

Option Boxes appear in the Shared Care Plan (SCP) workflow. When a clinician opens a patient's problem in the coupler and a matching fact box exists, a Fact Box button appears in the toolbar. Clicking it opens the Option Box in a new tab, pre-loaded to the relevant decision. Clinician and patient review it together.

What makes a good candidate decision?

A good Option Box decision has all of these:

  • A genuine fork. Reasonable, informed people make different choices. There is no single right answer.
  • Quantified evidence. At least one major RCT or high-quality meta-analysis exists with event counts per denominator.
  • A meaningful time horizon. The outcomes play out over a knowable period (1, 5, or 10 years).
  • A watchful waiting comparator. Every fact box should include a "no treatment" or "lifestyle only" option — this anchors the patient's choice in reality.
  • Patient-relevant outcomes. Not surrogate endpoints (LDL level, blood pressure number) but outcomes patients care about: stroke, heart attack, death, disability, quality of life.
Hard rule
Never build a fact box from surrogate endpoints alone. "Reduces LDL by 30%" is not an outcome a patient can reason about. "18 fewer heart attacks per 1,000 people over 5 years" is.

The authoring pipeline

  1. Choose the decision Identify a clinical fork that meets the candidate criteria above. Current library: atrial fibrillation anticoagulation, statin for primary prevention, hypertension medication vs lifestyle. Good candidates: knee OA (PT vs surgery vs watchful waiting), breast cancer screening, PSA testing, low back pain imaging.
  2. Research the numbers Find event counts from the highest-quality available evidence. Preferred sources in order:
    1. Cochrane systematic reviews with meta-analysis
    2. Cholesterol Treatment Trialists / Blood Pressure Lowering Treatment Trialists (for CV decisions)
    3. USPSTF evidence reviews
    4. Individual large RCTs (SPRINT, JUPITER, AVERROES, etc.)

    You need: event count with option, event count without option, denominator, and time horizon — for each outcome. Do not use relative risks alone; convert to absolute numbers per 1,000.
  3. Author the JSON file Create data/nnt-library/{decision-id}.json following the schema. Use an existing fact box as your template. Required fields per option: id, label, type, benefits[], harms[], burden, unknowns[]. Each outcome needs with_option, without_option, and an evidence block with basis, grade, source, and funding.

    Harms also take a rac field — a two-character severity × probability code (e.g. 3C = major severity, possible probability). See the RAC table below.
  4. Validate 
    node validate-optionbox.js data/nnt-library/{file}.json
    Fix any errors before proceeding. The validator checks required fields, RAC code consistency, value bounds vs denominator, and watchful waiting presence.
  5. Fetch drug safety data For any drug-type option, run: 
    python3 drug-fetch.py "{generic drug name}"
    This resolves the drug name to an RxCUI, fetches the FDA label (including any boxed warnings), pulls the top 20 most-reported adverse events from FDA FAERS, and fetches plain-language MedlinePlus definitions for each adverse event term. Everything is cached to data/cache/ and appears automatically in the rendered Option Box — no further wiring needed.

    Make sure the option's rxnorm field in the JSON contains the ingredient-level RxCUI (e.g. "29046" for lisinopril). The fetch script creates alias cache files for all related product-level RxCUIs.
  6. Register in the library Add one entry to data/nnt-library/index.json: 
    {
  "id": "your-decision-id",
  "label": "Plain label for the Library picker and coupler sidebar",
  "file": "data/nnt-library/your-decision-id.json",
  "keywords": ["term1", "term2", "condition name"]
}
    Keywords drive the automatic match in the coupler sidebar. Use terms that will appear in a clinician's problem statement — plain language and clinical synonyms both.
  7. Review in the renderer Open http://localhost:8770/index.html, click Library ▾, and select your fact box. Review every outcome for: correct natural frequency sentence, correct icon array colors, NNT/NNH badges, RAC badge, evidence chips, drug safety panel, cascade notes. Load in the coupler and confirm the Fact Box button appears on the right problems.
  8. Commit Stage and commit the JSON file, any drug cache files, and the updated index.json. Do not commit data/cache/ — it is gitignored and regenerated on demand.

The icon array — what each color means

Every outcome displays a grid of 1,000 dots — one per person in the reference population over the stated time horizon. The colors carry precise meanings:

Green — Prevented by this choice. People who would have experienced this bad outcome without the treatment, but do not experience it with the treatment.
Amber — Treatment failure (benefit rows only). People who experienced the bad outcome despite taking the treatment. The drug did not protect them.
Red — Caused by this choice. People who experienced this harm specifically because of the treatment — it would not have happened without it.
Orange — Background rate (harm rows only). People who experienced this side effect regardless of treatment — from other causes. The drug is not responsible for them.
Grey — Not affected. People for whom this outcome is irrelevant either way.
Amber vs Orange
These look similar but mean opposite things. Amber (in benefit rows) signals a limit of the drug's effectiveness — the treatment didn't work well enough for these people. Orange (in harm rows) signals a background event — the side effect was already happening in this population for other reasons, and the drug is not to blame. Never conflate them.

RAC codes

Every harm outcome carries a RAC (Risk Assessment Code) — a two-character code that combines severity and probability. Use it to communicate how serious and how likely a harm is, not just how frequent it is in trial data.

A — Rare (<1%) B — Unlikely (1–10%) C — Possible (10–40%) D — Likely (>40%)
I — Minor1A1B1C1D
II — Moderate2A2B2C2D
III — Major3A3B3C3D
IV — Catastrophic4A4B4C4D

Examples from the current library:

  • 4B — Stroke without anticoagulation in AF. Catastrophic outcome (disability or death), unlikely over 5 years for an individual.
  • 3C — Major bleeding on apixaban. Major outcome (hospitalization), possible probability.
  • 1D — ACE inhibitor cough. Minor outcome (resolves on stopping), likely to occur.
  • 4A — Angioedema from lisinopril. Catastrophic if airway involved, rare probability.

Evidence grading

GradeBasisWhen to use
High Meta-analysis of RCTs, or large well-designed RCT Multiple consistent trials; effect estimate unlikely to change substantially
Moderate Single RCT, or meta-analysis with limitations One dominant trial; or trials with heterogeneity, early stopping, or surrogate outcomes
Low Observational studies, registry data No RCT available; confounding possible
Very low Expert opinion, extrapolation, case series Use sparingly; always flag in population_caveat

Writing the unknowns section

Every option must include an unknowns array — explicit statements of what the evidence does not tell us. This is not a disclaimer; it is clinically important information. Good unknowns address:

  • Populations excluded from or underrepresented in the trials
  • Time horizons beyond the trial follow-up
  • Subgroups where the benefit-harm balance may differ substantially
  • Methodological caveats (early stopping, surrogate endpoints, open-label design)
  • Decisions where individual variation dominates group averages
Good unknown example
"Whether people who stop statins due to perceived muscle symptoms would have the same symptoms on placebo — SAMSON showed ~90% of symptoms are nocebo-driven, but predicting individual response is not yet possible."

Scaling and throughput

The current pipeline produces approximately 2–4 well-researched fact boxes per week with one clinical curator. Steps 4–6 (validation, drug fetch, library registration) are automated and take under 5 minutes. The bottleneck is steps 2 and 3 — finding and verifying the numbers, and authoring the JSON.

AI assistance is appropriate for steps 2 and 3 as a drafting tool — generating a research brief, pulling candidate numbers from the literature, and producing a draft JSON. A clinician must review and approve every number before the fact box is published. AI output is a starting point, not a finished product.

Steps 1 and 7 (decision selection and final clinical review) must remain human. These are judgment calls about clinical relevance and patient safety that require clinical training and accountability.
