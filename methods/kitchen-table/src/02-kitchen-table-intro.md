# Kitchen Table Intro

What the [[Kitchen Table Exercise]] is, who it is for, and what it costs. Nothing on this page will spoil the exercise.

## The short version

A person sits down with paper and a pencil and, in about an hour and in eleven steps, turns something they care about into a diagram they can read out loud. Nobody teaches them, nobody helps them, and nobody looks at the result unless they choose to show it.

Then they spend about the same amount of time again writing down how each step felt.

The drawing is the visible product. The second hour is where most of the learning is.

## Why it is called that

Because that is where it was done. People came to a kitchen table, worked on their own drawing, and the person whose table it was sat doing unrelated work and did not look. They asked for help and did not get any. The table is not decoration — the arrangement is the method, and it is described in full in [[Kitchen Table Manual]].

## What it is for

Four things, roughly in the order people notice them.

- You end up with a usable picture of something that matters to you, which you did not have before.
- You find out that you can make one. Most people do not believe this until it has happened.
- You get a record of which parts were hard and what that felt like, which turns out to be more informative than the drawing.
- You acquire the beginning of a shared visual language, which means your picture and somebody else's can be put side by side and argued about.

## Who it is for

Anyone from about ten years old upward. No prior knowledge of systems, diagramming, or anything else is needed or helpful.

It has been done by students, teachers, administrators, and professionals. The pattern so far is that people who have spent longer in formal education and credentialing find it harder, not easier. That is discussed after the fold in the manual.

## What it costs

An hour for the drawing. About an hour for the reflection. Paper, a pencil, an eraser. If you are hosting it for others, a room and some patience.

There is no software, no licence, and no instructor.

## Why a folk school method rather than a course

RCN tools have so far been things you use. This is the first thing we are publishing that is a way of working rather than a thing to work with.

The distinction that matters is what happens to authorship. In a course, somebody else chooses the topic, sets the length, and says whether the result is any good. Here you choose the topic, the paper sets the length, and nobody says. Folk schools run on curiosity and experiment rather than on assessment, and this method is what that looks like when you write it down.

The practical consequence is that the method scales by being copied, not by being taught. Anyone who has done it can host it for someone else, because hosting it consists mostly of not helping.

## What it is not

It is not a test, and nothing is scored.

It is not a systems-thinking course. You will not be taught any theory. What the exercise demonstrates is that you were already doing most of this before anyone named it.

It is not a causal loop diagram, although it is one useful step away from one. See the end of [[Kitchen Table Manual]].

It is not group work. The group version exists and it comes afterwards, once everyone is holding a drawing of their own.

## Where it came from

Marc Pierson developed it and published the original as Systems Certification on relocalizecreativity.net. It was used in Ferndale, Washington in 2019 with a cohort of students who had left school, and the eleven steps, the notes, and the refusal to help are all as they were then.

The reflection steps were not in the first version. They were added because people were visibly afraid, and the fear needed somewhere to go.
