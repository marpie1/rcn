# Kitchen Table Manual

The user manual for the [[Kitchen Table Exercise]]. It has two halves and a hard line between them.

## Read this half now

You need an hour, a second hour afterwards, three or four sheets of paper, a pencil and an eraser. Do it alone. Start at [[Kitchen Table Step 1]] and do not read ahead.

Everything below the fold assumes you have finished. It names what the exercise was doing and why parts of it may have been uncomfortable. Reading it first will not make the exercise easier — it will make it not work, because you will spend the hour trying to produce what you have been told to expect.

If you are here to host it for other people, you still do it yourself first. The whole of the hosting section below will read as obvious once you have, and as advice you can ignore if you have not.

## What you just did

Here is the list of what you demonstrated, which was kept out of sight until now.

Focusing. Listing, which is looking about, imagining, naming and limiting. Culling, which is narrowing and selecting. Arraying, which is spatial organising. Connecting, which is formally relating. Assigning direction, which is vectoring. Labelling, which is clarifying. Narrating. Reflecting and synthesising.

Nine things, and the point of the list is that every single one of them is something you were already doing before anybody taught you anything. The exercise did not install a skill. It named one you had.

That is why self-certification is an honest description rather than a soft one, and it is why the list stayed hidden. Being told in advance what you are about to demonstrate turns it into a target, and then you spend the hour aiming at it instead of thinking about your own concern.

## One account of why some of it was hard

What follows is what has been observed across the people who have done this. It is an account of a pattern, not a diagnosis of you. Your own notes are better evidence about your hour than this page is, and if this account does not match them, trust your notes.

The difficulty does not fall evenly across the eleven steps. It concentrates wherever nobody is telling you whether you are right.

- Step 1, because a great many people have not been asked in years what they actually care about, because whatever you choose exposes something about you, and because there is no way to tell whether you picked a good one.
- Step 2, because the floor of fifteen pushes you past the point where you would have stopped, and items eight to fifteen tend to be the ones you were not going to admit to.
- Step 3, because you select without being asked to justify it, and you can spend the whole step bracing for a question that never comes.
- Step 5, because naming a relationship is where vocabulary insecurity lives, in anyone who has been corrected on their words.
- Step 8, because erasing has usually meant you were wrong, and here it means the work is progressing. Understanding that inversion is easy. Feeling it is not.
- Step 9, because reading your own work aloud is a familiar place to be humiliated, and this time there is nobody to say whether it was any good, so you have to be your own judge having been taught that you are not qualified.

The common thread is judgement, and its absence. Most of us learned to work in places where somebody else says whether it counts. This exercise removes that at every step, and what people often feel is the removal.

If that is what happened, the useful sentence is not I am bad at this. It is I was trained to need something that is not in this room.

And the difficulty is adaptive rather than damage. It was a reasonable strategy in the place it was learned. It simply does not transfer here. You finished, which is the evidence that matters.

## The range so far

Fewer than twenty people have done this with records kept informally or not at all, so what follows is a direction and not a measurement.

At one end, a student who had left school found none of the steps hard. At the other, a lawyer found every step very hard, which later conversation confirmed was fear rather than professional caution. Everybody else fell somewhere between.

That ordering runs backwards to what most schools would predict about those two people. It is what you would expect if the difficulty tracks years of successful submission to judgement rather than years of education.

It also means that if you found this easy, that is worth knowing about yourself, and if you found it hard, you are in ordinary company and in the majority.

## What to do with your drawing

Read it out loud to one other person. Do not hand it over and ask what they think — read it to them. The difference matters and you will feel it.

Do not tidy it up. The wobbly version is the one you made.

In a month, draw it again from a blank sheet rather than editing this one. What changes between the two is the finding.

Keep your notes. If you do this again on another topic, the two sets side by side say more than either does alone.

## Sitting at the table for somebody else

This is the part that needs practice, and it is not the part you would expect. Running the exercise is easy. Not helping is hard.

The arrangement has three components and all three have to be present.

- Declare it in advance. Say plainly that you will not look at their work and will not help, before they start. Silence that has not been declared is not a withdrawal of judgement, it is surveillance, and it is worse than marking.
- Demonstrate it. Do your own unrelated work while they draw. Not sitting attentively, not available if needed. Being visibly occupied is the proof that the declaration was true.
- Hold it. They will ask. They will ask repeatedly, and some will ask in ways that are hard to refuse. Every refusal re-confirms the rule.

Be present the whole time. This is not the same as leaving them alone in a room. Alone, there is no authority to have a different kind of relationship with. At the table there is one, and he or she declines the role, and that is the thing being unlearned.

### A hint is worse than a wrong answer

If you give one hint, the protocol is over. The moment you demonstrate that you could have helped, your silence stops being a rule and becomes a withholding, and the whole hour is converted backwards into a test with somebody in the room who knows the answer. From that point on they are working out what you want.

This is why there are no examples anywhere in the eleven steps. A worked example is a hint about what a good answer looks like, which puts the judge back in the room in written form.

### The line between framing and help

Anything said to everybody before Step 1 is framing, and it is allowed. Anything said to one person during the steps is help, and it is not.

So if you discover that a particular kind of person hits a particular wall, the fix goes into the opening framing for everyone, and never into a whisper to that person.

The framing in [[Kitchen Table Exercise]] is deliberately short and stops at saying that some steps may feel uncomfortable and that this is normal. It does not say why, because saying why would hand over the answer to Step 10 before they have got there.

### What you may say

- Take your time.
- There is no right answer to this one.
- Nobody will see this unless you decide to show them.
- Keep going.
- Yes, that is the whole instruction.
- I am not going to help you with that, and that is on purpose.

### What you may not say

Anything that evaluates, suggests, or gives an example. Anything that starts with "you could try". Anything that answers "is this right". Any face you make at their paper, which is why you are not looking at it.

### Their asking is the data

The requests for help are not an interruption of the exercise, they are the most useful observation in it. Someone who asks nine times and someone who asks nothing have told you something about themselves that neither would have been able to report.

Count them silently if you want a record. But the count belongs to the method and never to the person: a help-request number attached to somebody's name is a score, and we would have rebuilt the thing this exercise exists to remove. Aggregate it or do not keep it.

## Doing it with a group

Everyone does their own, separately and simultaneously, in the same room. Not together. The group work comes afterwards, once each person is holding a finished drawing of their own.

Then they can try to build one diagram out of several. In Ferndale five students took six weekly sessions to do this with sticky notes on a board, and the work turned out to be almost entirely about language — connecting nodes, long arguments about what words meant, renaming, a few additions, and combining near-synonyms.

Expect that ratio. Merging personal maps is terminology negotiation and it is slow. Any plan that treats the merge as a quick step after the fun part has it backwards.

### The group rule is harder than the table rule

Alone, what you are withholding is the urge to teach. In a group, what you are withholding is the urge to arbitrate — five people arguing about whether two words mean the same thing, and every adult instinct saying settle it.

Settling it is what would destroy the work. In a group the facilitator's judgement does not merely decide a question, it takes over the language, and one ruling on whether two words are synonyms makes the whole shared vocabulary yours. Since the merge is about the words, that is everything.

So the group rule is not do not correct. It is do not resolve. Let a disagreement about a word stay open as long as it needs to.

Encouragement is allowed, and the reason is precise: it carries no propositional content. Keep going says nothing about the map. I think those two are the same thing says everything about it.

### Leave the seams

A merged diagram will end up with the same idea appearing in two places, because two people raised it and nobody had the authority to rule. Leave them. Those duplicates are a record of where the group's language has not yet converged, which is more useful than a tidy diagram would have been.

## Where to go next

The drawing you made is a semantic network — things joined by action verbs. It reads aloud as sentences, which is what makes Step 9 work.

There is one more move available, and it is worth the work.

Go through your nodes and keep only the ones that can be more or less — quantities that go up and down. Then restate each verb as either same direction or opposite direction. Then look for a circle. What you will have is a causal loop diagram, and the circles are feedback, which is where systems behaviour actually lives.

Two things about doing that conversion.

Keep a list of the nodes that refuse to become variables. That list is the finding. A node that will not convert is usually either a thing rather than a quantity, or an abstraction hiding the quantity that actually matters, and prying it open teaches more about your situation than the finished diagram does.

And the question that does the conversion is: what is it about this that matters here? Forget for now whether you could measure it. The question is whether it matters.

From there the next steps are a larger diagram built by asking those questions of somebody else, and eventually a model you can run. The RCN graph tool will take the drawing once you want it on a screen, and the same picture can then be looked at in several other ways.

## Where this came from

Marc Pierson developed the eleven steps and published them as Systems Certification on relocalizecreativity.net. This manual is the method package version and the step wording is his.

It was used in Ferndale, Washington in 2019 with a cohort of students who had left school. One of them did it first, then the cohort's facilitator did it, then her administrator, each alone at the same table, and then four more students at once. The order of that spread — outward from the most disaffected student rather than down from the most senior adult — was not a gesture. He was the one who found it easiest.

The reflection steps were not in the first version. They were added because the fear was visible and needed somewhere to go.

## What would be useful back

Nobody has kept proper records, and fewer than twenty people have done this. If you run it, the two things worth keeping are the participants' own step-by-step notes, which belong to them, and an aggregate count of how often people asked for help at each step, which belongs to nobody.

Thirty or forty people in, that would be enough to say which step is hardest as a measurement rather than as an impression — and a measurement is the only form in which this finding can be offered to a school without it sounding like an accusation.
